\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:4.1}

This chapter reports what happened when the procedure described in Chapter~3 was actually run. It begins with the data the models were trained and tested on (Section~4.2), then moves through the clustering outcome (Section~4.3), the proposed CFL-EDLSTM's own performance (Section~4.4), and its comparison against the baseline models (Section~4.5), before breaking the results down by forecast horizon (Sections~4.6 and~4.8) and by station cluster (Section~4.7). The remaining sections turn to interpretability (Section~4.9), station-level forecasts (Section~4.10), the comparison against FFWC information (Section~4.11), and a closing discussion that places the findings alongside earlier work (Sections~4.12 and~4.13).

Throughout, model performance is judged mainly on MAE and RMSE in metres, since these stay directly comparable across architectures and are expressed in the same unit the water-level readings themselves use.

\section{Experimental Setup and Dataset Characteristics}
\label{sec:4.2}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All results in this chapter use the station-wise records of water level, rainfall, MDD, evaporation, and temperature from the 25 monitoring stations described in Section~\ref{sec:3.5}. For every station, we hold out the last 15\% of the timeline as the test set, so the models are always judged on data that comes after what they were trained on. Each model looks back over 30 days of history (150 observations) and predicts the next 15 days (75 observations). We report accuracy at the seven horizon checkpoints defined in Section~\ref{sec:3.15}. Every figure is computed after converting the outputs back to metres, and we have not rounded anything beyond what the result files themselves contain.

Training and evaluation ran on a single desktop machine with an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 six-core processor (3.60~GHz), 24~GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3060 (12~GB VRAM). The PyTorch models used CUDA where it was available, and XGBoost was set up to run on the same GPU.

\subsection{Processed Dataset}

The final dataset brings together hydrological and meteorological records from 25 gauge stations in the Brahmaputra basin. It starts on 1 January 2010 and ends on 31 March 2026, which is 5{,}934 days, or a little over sixteen years. Each station contributes 29{,}670 observations, giving 741{,}750 rows and 44 columns in total. Since every station covers the same period and uses the same set of columns, the panel is balanced. This is useful for the clustered federated setup, because each station can act as a client with the same time span and the same input structure.

\paragraph{Temporal structure.}
Observations are recorded five times a day, at 06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00, and 18:00. About 80\% of consecutive records are 3 hours apart, and the rest are the 12-hour overnight gap between 18:00 and 06:00 the next morning. We chose to keep this uneven spacing instead of forcing the data onto a regular grid. To help the models cope with it, a \texttt{delta\_hours} column stores the normalised time gap (0.25 for a 3-hour step and 1.0 for the overnight jump).

\paragraph{Variables.}
Water level is what we are forecasting, and it is the only variable measured several times a day. The other variables describe the conditions around it. Some of them are recorded at a coarser resolution, so their value stays the same across all observations within a day.

Rainfall, evaporation, and temperature come from fewer measurement points than there are water-level stations, so each station is matched to its closest relevant record and neighbouring stations sometimes share the same series. Across the 25 stations there are 12 distinct rainfall series, 7 evaporation series, and 9 temperature series. Discharge is available at 15 of the 25 stations and is recorded as zero at the other 10.

In the processed data, water level averages 12.51~mMSL (SD 11.28, range $-0.92$ to 52.90) and differs a lot from one station to another. Rainfall averages 4.79~mm with a maximum of 540~mm, and about 75\% of records are zero, which is what we would expect with a long dry season. Evaporation averages 1.67~mm, and temperature averages 25.58~\textdegree C, ranging from 8.65 to 36.83~\textdegree C.

\paragraph{Engineered features.}
To give the models some memory of recent conditions, we added look-back features. These include lagged values at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 14 days for rainfall, discharge, temperature, and evaporation (24 columns), cumulative rainfall over 3, 7, 14, and 30 days (4 columns) to reflect how saturated the catchment is, and rolling mean discharge over the same four windows (4 columns). We did not create lags of water level, because it is the target. Hour of day and day of year are each encoded as a sine and cosine pair (4 columns), so that 18:00 sits close to 06:00 and 31 December sits close to 1 January. The forecast horizons (3~h, 6~h, 12~h, and 1, 3, 7, and 15 days) are created when the training windows are built, so they do not appear as separate columns in the file.

\paragraph{Missing data.}
The raw records had gaps, mostly in rainfall, discharge, temperature, and evaporation, and the amount missing differed from station to station (see Table~\ref{tab:4.1}). Our first idea was to recover these values from outside sources, so we went through the available reports from the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre (FFWC). Unfortunately, this helped very little. The reports covered only part of the gaps and often did not match the stations or time stamps we were missing, so we could fill only a small number of values this way. The remaining gaps were filled using forward filling followed by backward filling, and the final dataset has no missing values. It is worth remembering that filled values are estimates, not measurements. The long dry-season stretches with constant values, and the 10 stations with no discharge record at all, should be kept in mind when reading the results.

XGBoost still has the lowest overall error, with an MAE of 0.303~m and an RMSE of 0.501~m. CFL-EDLSTM comes second (MAE 0.321~m, RMSE 0.521~m), and the gap is small: 0.018~m in MAE (about 6\%) and 0.020~m in RMSE (about 4\%). Its NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} is 0.9979, against 0.9981 for XGBoost. Against the other sequence models the difference is much larger. CFL-EDLSTM lowers MAE by about 35\% relative to Vanilla LSTM, 36\% relative to GRU, and 44\% relative to TCN, and lowers RMSE by roughly 28\%, 30\%, and 39\%. Among the neural models it is therefore clearly the strongest, and it is the only one that gets close to XGBoost.

XGBoost's lead fits the role Chapter~3 gave it as a sanity-check baseline: the lag and rolling features it works from already capture much of the predictable structure in this dataset, so it sets a bar the sequence models must approach rather than one they clear simply by being more complex. It also fits the wider literature, where XGBoost has matched or outperformed LSTM in comparable hydrological settings \cite{Hameed2025} and no single architecture wins everywhere \cite{Aghenda2025}. The more informative comparison for the proposed design is the one with Vanilla LSTM, since both use LSTM cells. The improvement over it comes from the encoder--decoder structure and the clustered federated training together, and this table cannot separate the two. The role of clustering is examined in Section~\ref{sec:4.7}.

Because NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} \cite{NashSutcliffe1970} is already at or above 0.994 for every model, it does little to separate them. The differences show up in MAE and RMSE, where they can be read directly in metres. The LSTM--GRU comparison deserves one caution: GRU was trained jointly across all stations while LSTM was trained station by station (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}), so the gap between them reflects that difference in data representation as well as the cell design, and should not be attributed to gating alone.

One further detail concerns the window counts. XGBoost is evaluated on 109{,}425 test windows and the three neural baselines on 105{,}675, a difference of 3{,}750, or exactly 150 per station, the length of one input window. This most likely reflects how XGBoost's windows may draw lag inputs from just before the test boundary, while the neural baselines' windows must lie entirely inside the test split (Section~\ref{sec:3.9.5}). CFL-EDLSTM is evaluated on 111{,}275 windows, which is 224 more per station than the neural baselines and most likely comes from the common timestamp axis the federated pipeline builds across stations (Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). Since every metric is averaged over the windows a model sees, the five models are scored on slightly different window sets. The differences are small relative to the totals, but the comparison is not strictly like for like.

The spread across stations is considerably wider than the overall averages in Table~\ref{tab:4.2} suggest on their own. For XGBoost, station MAE ranges from 0.182~m at SW294 up to 0.429~m at SW63, more than double, within a model whose overall MAE is 0.303~m. CFL-EDLSTM shows a similar spread, from 0.177~m at SW294 to 0.466~m at SW225. TCN shows the widest spread of any model by far: its MAE at SW62 (0.399~m) is actually lower than XGBoost's worst station, while at SW15J it reaches 2.648~m, nearly seven times higher.

CFL-EDLSTM is not simply a weaker copy of XGBoost at the station level. It has a lower MAE than XGBoost at 10 of the 25 stations (SW151, SW228.5, SW294, SW45.5, SW49A, SW63, SW68.5, SW68A, SW77, and SW97), although the margin is very thin at a few of them, for example 0.0007~m at SW77. In RMSE it comes out ahead at only 5 stations (SW294, SW49A, SW62, SW7.5, and SW97). The fact that it wins more often on MAE than on RMSE suggests that a small number of larger errors weigh more heavily on CFL-EDLSTM than on XGBoost, though we did not examine the error distributions here. Against the neural baselines the picture is more consistent. By our reading of the tables, CFL-EDLSTM has a lower MAE than Vanilla LSTM, GRU, and TCN at 24 of the 25 stations, and a lower RMSE at 23. The clear exception is SW291.5R, the station sitting near 50~mMSL, far above all the others (Figure~\ref{fig:raw-wl-timeseries}). There CFL-EDLSTM has an MAE of 0.378~m, compared with 0.216~m for XGBoost and 0.284~m for GRU. This is the largest station-level gap in the table, and a single station-specific level is a plausible reason, though we did not test it.

A pattern worth flagging before the formal cluster-wise analysis in Section~\ref{sec:4.7}: the stations where the baselines struggle most, SW15J, SW49A, SW49, SW228.5, SW97, and SW46.9R, sit predominantly in the Middle and Downstream clusters (Table~\ref{tab:3.5}), while several of the consistently easiest stations, SW179, SW180, SW7.5, SW294, and SW291.5R, are Urban or Upstream. Three of those six harder stations (SW49A, SW228.5, and SW97) are also among the ones where CFL-EDLSTM beats XGBoost on MAE. This is only a visual pattern at this stage, not a formal cluster-level result. It points in the direction the clustering argument in Chapters~1 and~2 anticipated: a single global model is asked to perform well across stations whose difficulty differs by a wide margin. Section~\ref{sec:4.7} returns to this with the proper cluster-level breakdown.

XGBoost has the lowest RMSE at all seven horizons and the lowest MAE at the first six. Its lead is largest where forecasts are easiest: at 3 hours its MAE is about 0.058~m, roughly 2.5 times lower than CFL-EDLSTM's 0.146~m and more than four times lower than Vanilla LSTM's. The lead narrows steadily with lead time. At 7 days the two MAE values are close (0.323 and 0.332~m), and at 15 days CFL-EDLSTM moves ahead, with an MAE of 0.436~m against 0.449~m for XGBoost, about 3\% lower. The crossover is only partial, because in RMSE XGBoost is still slightly better at 15 days (0.672 against 0.690~m). The margin is also small and, since CFL-EDLSTM was trained without a fixed random seed, we read it as a sign that the two models converge at long horizons rather than as clear superiority at 15 days.

The same trend shows up in how fast error grows. XGBoost's MAE rises about 7.7-fold from 3 hours to 15 days, against roughly 3.0-fold for CFL-EDLSTM, 2.6-fold for Vanilla LSTM, and 1.9-fold for both GRU and TCN. CFL-EDLSTM therefore starts from a much lower floor than the other neural models, about half of Vanilla LSTM's at 3 hours, and degrades fairly gently, so the gap to XGBoost closes as the horizon lengthens. Its MAE is almost flat over the first day (0.146 to 0.160~m), which suggests the model holds up well for short-range forecasts.

CFL-EDLSTM has a lower MAE and RMSE than every neural baseline at every horizon. At 3 hours its MAE is about 44\% below Vanilla LSTM's, the best neural baseline at that horizon. At 15 days, where GRU is the strongest neural baseline, CFL-EDLSTM still has an MAE about 25\% lower (0.436 against 0.583~m) and an RMSE about 18\% lower (0.690 against 0.838~m).

Among the neural baselines the ordering is not fixed. Vanilla LSTM beats GRU at every horizon from 3 hours through 7 days on both metrics, but at 15 days GRU takes over (MAE 0.583 against 0.675~m; RMSE 0.838 against 0.990~m). TCN behaves differently again. Its MAE is almost flat from 3 hours to 1 day (0.416 to 0.419~m), and its 3-hour error is larger than XGBoost's at 7 days, which suggests something other than forecast difficulty sets its floor at short horizons. It edges past GRU at 7 days on both metrics, but it is the weakest model at 15 days. It also trails Vanilla LSTM at every horizon, a less favourable showing than the competitive TCN performance reported by \cite{Lin2021}, though on a different dataset.
