\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:4.1}

This chapter presents the results of the procedure described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}. Section~\ref{sec:4.2} describes the data on
which the models were trained and tested, Section~\ref{sec:4.3} presents the
clustering outcome, and Section~\ref{sec:4.4} reports the performance of
CFL-EDLSTM on its own. Section~\ref{sec:4.5} compares CFL-EDLSTM with the
baselines, first as centrally trained models and then, in
Section~\ref{sec:4.5.1}, as versions trained under the same clustered federated
scheme, to show why the encoder--decoder was chosen as the model to federate.
The results are then examined by forecast horizon (Sections~\ref{sec:4.6}
and~\ref{sec:4.8}), by cluster (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}) and by station
(Section~\ref{sec:4.10}), with the SHAP analysis in Section~\ref{sec:4.9}. The
remaining sections compare the model with FFWC (Section~\ref{sec:4.11}),
discuss the findings (Section~\ref{sec:4.12}) and place them alongside earlier
studies (Section~\ref{sec:4.13}), and the chapter closes with a summary
(Section~\ref{sec:4.14}). Performance is judged mainly on MAE and RMSE in
meters, because both remain comparable across architectures and are expressed
in the same unit as the readings.

\section{Experimental Setup and Dataset Characteristics}
\label{sec:4.2}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:4.2.1}

All results use the station-wise records of water level, rainfall, MDD,
evaporation and temperature from the 25 stations listed in
Section~\ref{sec:3.5}. For every station, the first 70\% of the timeline is
used for training, the next 15\% for validation and the last 15\% for testing,
so the models are always evaluated on data that come after the data they were
trained on (Section~\ref{sec:3.9.5}). Each model looks back over 30 days (150
observations) and predicts the next 15 days (75 observations), and accuracy is
reported at the seven horizon checkpoints defined in Section~\ref{sec:3.15}.
All values are computed after the outputs have been converted back to meters
and are reported without rounding beyond what the result files contain.
Training and evaluation were carried out on a single desktop machine
(Section~\ref{sec:3.19}).

\subsection{Processed Dataset}
\label{sec:4.2.3}

The final dataset combines hydrological and meteorological records from 25
gauge stations in the Brahmaputra River system. It runs from 1 January 2010 to
31 March 2026, covering 5{,}934 days, or a little over sixteen years. Each
station contributes 29{,}670 observations, giving 741{,}750 rows and 44
columns. Because every station covers the same period with the same columns,
the panel is balanced. This suits the clustered federated setup, since each
station enters its client with the same time span and input structure.

\paragraph{Temporal structure.}
Observations are recorded five times a day, at 06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00 and
18:00. About 80\% of consecutive records are three hours apart, and the
remainder are the twelve-hour overnight gaps from 18:00 to 06:00. This uneven
spacing was retained rather than forced onto a regular grid, and a
\texttt{delta\_hours} column stores the normalized gap (0.25 for a three-hour
step and 1.0 for the overnight jump).

\paragraph{Variables.}
Water level is the forecast target and the only variable measured several
times a day. The other variables are recorded at a coarser resolution, so their
value is the same for all observations within a day. Rainfall, evaporation and
temperature come from fewer measurement points than there are stations, so
each station is matched to its closest relevant record, and neighboring
stations sometimes share a series. Across the 25 stations there are 13 distinct
rainfall series, 7 evaporation series and 11 temperature series.
Discharge is available at 15 stations and recorded as zero at the other 10.

In the processed data, water level averages 12.51~mMSL (SD 11.28, range
$-0.92$ to 52.90) and differs greatly between stations. Rainfall averages
4.79~mm with a maximum of 540~mm, and about 75\% of the records are zero, as
expected given the long dry season. Evaporation averages 1.67~mm, and
temperature averages 25.58~\textdegree C, ranging from 8.65 to
36.83~\textdegree C.

\paragraph{Engineered features.}
To give the models a memory of recent conditions, look-back features were
added: lagged values at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 14 days for rainfall, discharge,
temperature and evaporation (24 columns), cumulative rainfall over 3, 7, 14 and
30 days (4 columns), and rolling-mean discharge over the same four windows (4
columns). No lags of water level were created, because it is the target and
the input window already carries its history. Hour of day and day of year are
each encoded as a sine and cosine pair (4 columns), so that 18:00 lies close to
06:00 and 31 December close to 1 January. The forecast horizons are created
when the training windows are built, so they do not appear as columns.
Table~\ref{tab:features} lists the feature groups.

\paragraph{Missing data.}
The raw records contained gaps, by far the largest in discharge and
temperature, and the amount missing differed between stations
(Table~\ref{tab:4.1}).
We first attempted to recover these values from the reports of the Flood
Forecasting and Warning Centre (FFWC), but the reports covered only part of the
gaps and often did not match the missing stations or times, so only a few
values could be filled in this way. The remaining gaps were filled by linear
interpolation, followed by forward and backward filling, and any value that
still could not be filled, such as discharge at the ten stations without a
sensor, was set to zero (Section~\ref{sec:3.9.2}). The final dataset has no
missing values. Filled values are estimates rather than measurements, and the
long dry-season stretches with constant values and the ten stations without a
discharge record should be kept in mind when interpreting the results.

\paragraph{Water level separates the clusters most clearly.}
The average water level falls steadily from the Upstream cluster
(28.24~mMSL) through Mid-river (12.55~mMSL) and Downstream (5.75~mMSL) to the
Urban cluster (2.11~mMSL). This ordering matches the cluster names, which
suggests that the grouping reflects the stations' position along the river and
their elevation. The difference between the highest and lowest clusters is
more than tenfold, larger than for any other variable.

\paragraph{Temperature and evaporation differ little.}
Average temperature stays within a narrow band, from 25.03~\textdegree C
(Upstream) to 25.49~\textdegree C (Urban), a range of less than half a degree.
Evaporation varies more in relative terms, from 1.32 to 1.90~mm, but the
absolute differences are small. The clusters therefore share a broadly similar
climate and are distinguished mainly by hydrological behavior rather than by
temperature.

\paragraph{Rainfall and discharge require caution.}
The discharge averages are close across clusters (4.27 to 5.47), but discharge
is available at only 15 of the 25 stations and is zero at the rest
(Section~\ref{sec:4.2.3}), so these averages depend partly on how many stations
in each cluster have a record. The Mid-river rainfall value (9{,}087) is about
21 to 35 times larger than those of the other three clusters (260 to 430) and
far above the overall mean of 4.79~mm reported in Section~\ref{sec:4.2.3}, so
it is not comparable with them and probably reflects how rainfall was
aggregated in this table. Rainfall and discharge are therefore not used to
distinguish the clusters.

\paragraph{Implications for modeling.}
The clusters clearly differ, mainly in water level. This supports giving each
cluster its own model state rather than forcing all 25 stations to share a
single set of parameters. Whether this actually improves the forecasts is a
separate question, addressed in Section~\ref{sec:4.7}.

\section{Overall Performance of CFL-EDLSTM}
\label{sec:4.4}

This section examines the performance of CFL-EDLSTM on its own, before it is
compared with the baselines in Section~\ref{sec:4.5}. Each station is scored on
its own chronological test split using the model of its cluster, with a 30-day
input and a 15-day forecast, and the model is evaluated on 111{,}275 test
windows across the 25 stations. Table~\ref{tab:4.cfl-overall} gives the
results.

\paragraph{Size of the errors.}
On average, the forecasts differ from the observations by about 0.32~m (MAE),
with an RMSE of 0.52~m. These errors are small relative to the spread of water
levels in the data (SD 11.28~mMSL, range $-0.92$ to 52.90~mMSL). The RMSE is
about 1.6 times the MAE, which indicates that most forecasts are close to the
observations while a smaller share carry larger errors. Where these larger
errors occur is examined in the horizon and station results.

\paragraph{Goodness of fit.}
The fit metrics are all close to 1: NSE and R\textsuperscript{2} are both
0.9979, EVS is 0.9979 and IOA is 0.9995 \cite{NashSutcliffe1970}. NSE and
R\textsuperscript{2} are identical and are listed separately only for
completeness. EVS is almost the same as NSE, and because EVS ignores a constant
bias whereas NSE does not, the model shows little systematic over- or
under-prediction. A high NSE is to be expected for series with strong seasonal
cycles and stations at very different levels, since even a simple model can
capture much of that variation. MAE and RMSE are therefore treated as the more
informative measures.

Figure~\ref{fig:4.cfl-cluster-forecast} compares predicted and observed water
levels over a 15-day forecast at four representative stations, one from each
cluster: Kaunia (Upstream), Fulchari (Mid-river), Taraghat (Downstream) and
Mirpur (Urban). The model captures the general downward trend at Kaunia,
Fulchari and Taraghat, although it does not fully follow the sharper declines
in the observed levels. At Mirpur, the predictions stay relatively close to the
observed levels but miss some short-term fluctuations. Overall, the model
captures broad trends but has difficulty reproducing sudden changes, and its
behavior differs across the four stations.

\paragraph{What the overall figures conceal.}
These values average over all stations and all forecast steps, so they are a
summary rather than the error at any single point. MAE is about 0.15~m at
3~hours and about 0.44~m at 15~days (Table~\ref{tab:4.3}), and station-level MAE
ranges from 0.177 to 0.466~m (Table~\ref{tab:4.2a}). Section~\ref{sec:4.5} first
places the overall values alongside those of the baselines.

CFL-EDLSTM achieves the best value on every metric. Its MAE of 0.321~m is
about 35\% lower than that of the next best model, Vanilla LSTM (0.496~m), and
its RMSE of 0.521~m is about 28\% lower than the 0.722~m of Vanilla LSTM.
Relative to XGBoost, the reductions are about 40\% in MAE and 49\% in RMSE, and
MSE falls by about three quarters. Relative to GRU and TCN, MAE is lower by
about 36\% and 44\% and RMSE by about 30\% and 39\%, respectively.
NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} follows the same order, from 0.9979 for CFL-EDLSTM to
0.9919 for XGBoost, but because it is at or above 0.991 for every model, it does
little to separate them. The differences become clear in MAE and RMSE, which
can be read directly in meters.

The position of XGBoost depends on the metric. On MAE it ranks fourth, ahead of
TCN, but on RMSE it is the weakest of the five models (1.025~m), and its RMSE is
1.9 times its MAE, compared with about 1.5 to 1.6 for the other models. Because
RMSE penalizes large errors more heavily, a small number of large errors weighs
on the score of XGBoost; Sections~\ref{sec:4.6} and~\ref{sec:4.10} show that
these errors come from the longer horizons and from a handful of stations. Some
studies report XGBoost matching or outperforming LSTM in hydrology
\cite{Hameed2025}. Here this holds only at short lead times, which is consistent
with the observation that no single architecture performs best in every setting
\cite{Aghenda2025}.

The most direct comparison for the proposed design is with Vanilla LSTM,
because both use LSTM cells. The improvement comes from the encoder--decoder
structure and the clustered federated training together, and this table cannot
separate the two. The inputs also differ: Vanilla LSTM sees 10 features per
step and CFL-EDLSTM 42 (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}). The LSTM--GRU comparison
requires a similar caution: the GRU was trained jointly across stations and the
LSTM station by station, so the gap between them reflects this difference as
well as the design of the recurrent cell.

The number of test windows also differs between models. XGBoost is scored on
109{,}425 test windows and the three neural baselines on 105{,}675, a
difference of exactly 150 windows per station, equal to the length of one input
window; this most likely reflects the lag inputs of XGBoost being drawn from
just before the test boundary. CFL-EDLSTM is scored on 111{,}275 windows, 224
more per station than the neural baselines, which most likely results from how
its windows are generated on the shared timeline (Section~\ref{sec:3.12.2}).
Because every metric is averaged over the windows a model sees, the models are
scored on slightly different window sets. The differences are small relative to
the totals, but the comparison is not strictly like for like.

CFL-EDLSTM has the lowest MAE at all seven horizons and the lowest RMSE at six
of them. Relative to CFL-GRU, its MAE is 20\% lower at 3~hours and 4\% lower at
15~days, and about 12\% lower on average over the seven checkpoints. Relative to
CFL-TCN, it is 51\% lower at 3~hours and 11\% lower at 15~days, and about 33\%
lower on average. The only exception is the RMSE at 15~days, where CFL-GRU is
slightly ahead (0.676 against 0.690~m). The margin over the federated GRU and
TCN is largest at short lead times and narrows as the horizon grows, because the
error of CFL-EDLSTM increases faster: about 3.0-fold from 3~hours to 15~days,
compared with 2.5-fold for CFL-GRU and 1.6-fold for CFL-TCN.

CFL-XGBoost performs far worse. Its MAE stays between 4.8 and 5.7~m at every
horizon, so the MAE of CFL-EDLSTM is 92 to 97\% lower, and it barely changes
with lead time (1.2-fold), unlike the centrally trained XGBoost, whose MAE is
0.07~m at 3~hours. Boosted trees have no shared parameter vector, so FedAvg
cannot be applied to them as it is to recurrent and convolutional networks.
CFL-XGBoost therefore keeps one estimator per cluster and combines their
predictions at inference time, without exchanging model updates. Each estimator
sees only the stations of its own cluster, whose water levels differ by tens of
meters from those of the other clusters (Table~\ref{tab:4.cluster-features}),
and trees cannot predict beyond the range of values seen in training.
Predictions blended across clusters are then pulled toward the wrong level,
which is consistent with an error that is large, roughly constant and
independent of lead time. This explanation was not tested, so CFL-XGBoost
should be read as showing that prediction-level aggregation does not work for
this baseline, not as a general statement about tree models in federated
learning.

The federated GRU and TCN are also more accurate than their centrally trained
versions, with 15-day MAEs of 0.455 against 0.583~m for GRU and 0.488 against
0.777~m for TCN (Table~\ref{tab:4.3}). This is consistent with the clustered
federated scheme being beneficial, but it is not attributed to federation alone,
because the central and federated runs differ in their inputs and window
construction (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}). Taken together, the results support the
choice of the encoder--decoder LSTM as the model to federate: under identical
federated training, it was the most accurate of the four architectures on MAE at
every horizon, with GRU as its closest competitor.

The best model depends on the horizon. XGBoost has the lowest MAE and RMSE at
the four shortest checkpoints (3~hours to 1~day), and CFL-EDLSTM at 3, 7 and
15~days, so the two cross between 1 and 3~days. At 3~hours, the MAE of XGBoost
(0.071~m) is roughly half that of CFL-EDLSTM (0.146~m) and about 3.7 times lower
than that of Vanilla LSTM. By 1~day the lead has narrowed to 0.127 against
0.160~m, and from 3~days the order reverses and the gap widens quickly: the MAE
of CFL-EDLSTM is about 25\% lower than that of XGBoost at 3~days, 56\% lower at
7~days and 62\% lower at 15~days (0.436 against 1.149~m). At 15~days the RMSE gap
is larger still, 0.690 against 2.372~m, a reduction of about 71\%.

The crossover results from the very different rates at which the errors grow.
From 3~hours to 15~days, the MAE of XGBoost rises about 16-fold, compared with
about 3.0-fold for CFL-EDLSTM, 2.6-fold for Vanilla LSTM and 1.9-fold for both
GRU and TCN, and at 7 and 15~days XGBoost is the weakest of the five models on
both metrics. A likely reason lies in how each model uses its inputs. XGBoost
receives the 150 most recent water-level values directly, together with the
current readings of the other variables, and tree ensembles are well suited to
structured inputs of this kind. At short lead times, when the next value is
close to the most recent ones, its trees can read the answer almost directly
from these values. This advantage fades with the horizon, because the most
recent values carry less information about water levels several days ahead and
each of its 75 regressors is trained independently, with no shared trajectory
across steps. The sequence models must first learn a temporal representation
from the input window, which costs them accuracy at short lead times, but they
carry that representation forward. They therefore start from a higher error
floor but degrade more gently, and the MAE of CFL-EDLSTM is almost flat over the
first day (0.146 to 0.160~m).

CFL-EDLSTM has a lower MAE and RMSE than every neural baseline at every horizon.
At 3~hours its MAE is about 44\% below that of Vanilla LSTM, and at 15~days,
where GRU is the strongest neural baseline, it is still about 25\% lower (0.436
against 0.583~m), with an RMSE about 18\% lower (0.690 against 0.838~m). The
ranking among the baselines is not fixed. Vanilla LSTM outperforms GRU at every
horizon from 3~hours to 7~days, and GRU takes over at 15~days (MAE 0.583 against
0.675~m). The MAE of TCN is almost flat over the first day (0.416 to 0.419~m),
which suggests that something other than forecast difficulty sets its error
floor at short horizons. TCN edges past GRU at 7~days but is the weakest neural
model at 15~days, and it trails Vanilla LSTM at every horizon. This is a less
favorable result than the competitive TCN performance reported by
\cite{Lin2021}, although on a different dataset.

The rise in error with lead time agrees with earlier multi-step
flood-forecasting studies \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Yuan2023}.
Figure~\ref{fig:4.4} presents the same MAE and RMSE values as a

\paragraph{Three phases of error growth.}
The error does not rise at a constant rate. Over the first day it is almost
flat: MAE increases from 0.146 to 0.160~m, about 9\%, and the 3- and 6-hour
values are practically identical. This suggests that at short range the error
is determined mainly by a floor, such as observation noise and the model's own
approximation error, rather than by the difficulty of the forecast, although
this was not tested directly. Between 1 and 7~days the error rises fastest: MAE
grows to 0.226~m at 3~days and 0.332~m at 7~days, roughly doubling, at about
0.033~m per day between 1 and 3~days and 0.026~m per day between 3 and 7~days.
Beyond 7~days the growth slows, with a further 0.104~m over eight days (about
0.013~m per day) to 0.436~m at 15~days. The error therefore continues to
increase but at a decreasing rate, and the 15-day forecast does not break down.
A likely reason for the steady rise is that the inputs contain only past
observations, so rainfall that has not yet fallen cannot inform the forecast.
This pattern is consistent with earlier multi-step flood-forecasting studies
\cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Yuan2023}.

\paragraph{Shape of the error.}
The ratio of RMSE to MAE stays between 1.54 and 1.58 at every horizon. A stable
ratio suggests that the shape of the error distribution changes little with lead
time: longer horizons shift the errors upward more or less uniformly rather than
adding a heavier tail of occasional large misses. This is an indirect reading
based on two summary metrics.

\paragraph{Gap to XGBoost.}
The last column of Table~\ref{tab:4.8} shows how the comparison with XGBoost
changes with the horizon, and Figure~\ref{fig:ratio} plots the same ratio. At
3~hours, the MAE of CFL-EDLSTM is about twice that of XGBoost (2.06 times). The
ratio falls to 1.26 at 1~day, drops below 1 between 1 and 3~days, and reaches
0.75 at 3~days, 0.44 at 7~days and 0.38 at 15~days. The advantage of XGBoost is
therefore confined to roughly the first day, and from 3~days onward CFL-EDLSTM
is clearly more accurate. The crossover owes as much to the rapid deterioration
of XGBoost (about 16-fold in MAE) as to the gentle error growth of CFL-EDLSTM.
Because the runs were not repeated, run-to-run variation is unknown.

\paragraph{Practical interpretation.}
The average error of CFL-EDLSTM is about 0.16~m at a one-day lead, 0.23~m at
three days, 0.33~m at seven days and 0.44~m at fifteen days. These are averages
over all stations and test windows, so individual stations and events may be
better or worse (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}). Whether errors of this size are

\section{SHAP-Based Interpretability Results}
\label{sec:4.9}
\label{sec:shap_results}

This section reports what SHAP reveals about the proposed CFL-EDLSTM
(Section~\ref{sec:3.17}). The results pool all four cluster models and the four
explained horizons (day~1, 3, 7 and 15), giving 768 explained test windows of
equal weight. Figure~\ref{fig:shap_feature_overall} ranks the input features,
and Figure~\ref{fig:shap_temporal_overall} shows how the attribution is
distributed over the 30-day input window.

\subsection{Which Inputs the Model Relies On}
\label{sec:4.9.1}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures2/shap_overall_feature_importance.jpeg}
    \caption{Top 20 features by mean absolute SHAP value (all clusters and horizons).}
    \label{fig:shap_feature_overall}
\end{figure}

As Figure~\ref{fig:shap_feature_overall} shows, the two seasonal terms rank
highest: the cosine of day of year has a mean absolute SHAP value of about
$3.6\times10^{-4}$, and its sine counterpart about $2.5\times10^{-4}$. The
temperature features follow, with current temperature at about
$1.7\times10^{-4}$ and its 1-, 2-, 7-, 14-, 3- and 5-day lags between roughly
$0.7$ and $1.5\times10^{-4}$, and then the hour-of-day terms at around
$0.8$--$1.0\times10^{-4}$.

Rainfall-based features are weaker. The 14-day accumulation is the strongest of
them (about $0.8\times10^{-4}$), followed by the 30-, 7- and 3-day
accumulations, while raw rainfall sits near the bottom of the ranking alongside
the evaporation features, at roughly $0.4$--$0.5\times10^{-4}$. No
discharge-related feature appears among the top 20. Summing the bars within each
group makes the pattern clearer: the temperature features contribute about
$8.6\times10^{-4}$ in total, the day-of-year terms $6.1\times10^{-4}$, the
rainfall features $2.8\times10^{-4}$, the hour-of-day terms $1.8\times10^{-4}$
and evaporation $1.3\times10^{-4}$. These totals are read from the chart and
cover only the top 20 features, so they indicate relative size rather than exact
shares.

Two cautions apply. First, the absolute magnitudes are small because SHAP values
are expressed in standardized units and because the attribution of past water
level, the dominant input, was removed before plotting
(Section~\ref{sec:3.17.5}); the ranking is therefore the meaningful part of the
result. Second, temperature varies smoothly with the monsoon cycle, so it may
act partly as a seasonal indicator, which would be consistent with the high rank
of the day-of-year terms. The model thus appears to depend more on the time of
year at which a forecast starts, and on recent temperature, than on any single
rainfall variable. This does not mean that rainfall is physically unimportant:
rainfall, its lags and its accumulations are strongly correlated, and SHAP
divides credit among correlated inputs, so each can appear weaker than the
signal they carry together.

\subsection{How Far Back the Model Looks}
\label{sec:4.9.2}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures2/shap_overall_temporal_importance.jpeg}
    \caption{Mean absolute SHAP value by input timestep (0 = oldest, 149 = latest).}
    \label{fig:shap_temporal_overall}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:shap_temporal_overall} shows that attribution is concentrated
at the end of the input window. Over the oldest 100 timesteps, roughly the first
20 days, the mean absolute SHAP value remains at or below about
$1\times10^{-4}$. It then rises slowly and climbs steeply over the last 20
timesteps, about the final four days, to a peak of about $1.1\times10^{-3}$ at
the most recent observation. The model therefore relies mainly on the latest
observations, which agrees with the steady growth of error with lead time
(Section~\ref{sec:4.8}). This does not mean that the distant past is ignored:
the recent timesteps already contain the 14-day lags and the 30-day rainfall
accumulation, so they carry information about earlier conditions.

SHAP describes what the models have learned, not what drives the river, so
these values are not causal effects. Each cluster--horizon result rests on 48
test windows measured against 32 background windows, which adds sampling noise,
so small differences between neighboring features should not be
over-interpreted.

\paragraph{Spread across stations.}
The spread across stations is wider than the overall averages in

CFL-EDLSTM is the most consistent: its MAE ranges from 0.177~m at SW294 to

The overall RMSE of XGBoost (Table~\ref{tab:4.2}) is driven by a few stations.

the water-level scale in Figure~\ref{fig:raw-wl-heatmap}. XGBoost also achieves
its lowest error of all at SW291.5R, the highest-level station. The reason for
its different behavior at low-level stations was not investigated, but these
stations account for much of the gap in overall RMSE.

\paragraph{CFL-EDLSTM against the baselines.}
CFL-EDLSTM has the lowest MAE of the five models at 24 of the 25 stations and
the lowest RMSE at 21. The exceptions are mostly small. In RMSE, XGBoost is
slightly lower at SW45 (0.528 against 0.549~m) and essentially tied at SW77
(0.5177 against 0.5183~m), and GRU is slightly lower at SW228.5 (0.623 against
0.632~m). The clear exception is SW291.5R, the station near 50~mMSL, far above
all the others (Figure~\ref{fig:raw-wl-timeseries}), where CFL-EDLSTM has an MAE
of 0.378~m, compared with 0.203~m for XGBoost, 0.284~m for GRU and 0.370~m for
Vanilla LSTM. This is the only station at which the proposed model is not among
the best, and its unusual level is a plausible reason, although this was not
tested.

\paragraph{Link to the cluster analysis.}
The station results show that the advantage of CFL-EDLSTM does not depend on a
few easy stations. It holds at most stations across a very wide range of water
levels, and no station produces the large errors seen with XGBoost or TCN.
Section~\ref{sec:4.7} examines these differences by cluster.

\paragraph{Interpreting the comparison.}
The pattern in the averages is consistent with the horizon results of
Section~\ref{sec:4.6}: the learned model is strongest in the first days and
loses its advantage as the lead time increases. The comparison should
nevertheless be read with care. It covers a short period in October 2026, after
the monsoon peak, when water levels change slowly and errors are small, so it
says little about flood conditions. The number of points is small, and the 5-day
comparison drops from 44 points on days 1 to 3 to 32 on days 4 and 5. Hindcasts
are not a substitute for real-time operation, and the values cannot be compared
directly with those of Section~\ref{sec:4.6}, which uses a different period,
different stations and many more points. The performance page shows the same
limits (Figure~\ref{fig:bonna_performance}): at Chak-Rahimpur (SW63), the 7- and
15-day errors were larger than those of a persistence forecast over the same two
weeks, and a per-station NSE over such a short and flat window is not
meaningful. This comparison is therefore treated as an encouraging first check
at short and medium lead times, not as evidence that CFL-EDLSTM outperforms FFWC.
Because the period lies after the end of the training and test data
(31~March 2026), it does provide an out-of-sample check, but a longer real-time
record will be needed to confirm it.

\section{Discussion of Findings}
\label{sec:4.12}

This chapter set out to determine whether a clustered federated encoder--decoder
LSTM can forecast water level across heterogeneous stations at horizons from
3~hours to 15~days. The answer is yes, with qualifications. CFL-EDLSTM achieved
the lowest overall error of the five models and maintained its accuracy as the
lead time increased. The following paragraphs explain what the results suggest
and where they should not be over-interpreted.

\paragraph{Accuracy depends on the horizon.}
No single model was best at every horizon. XGBoost was the most accurate up to a
one-day lead, and CFL-EDLSTM from three days onward (Section~\ref{sec:4.6}). At
3~hours, the MAE of XGBoost is about half that of CFL-EDLSTM, but at 15~days the
MAE of CFL-EDLSTM (0.436~m) is 62\% lower than that of XGBoost (1.149~m). The
difference arises mainly from how fast the error grows: about 16-fold for
XGBoost against about 3-fold for CFL-EDLSTM. A plausible interpretation is that
recent lagged values are highly informative at short lead times and lose value
as the horizon extends, whereas a sequence model holds up better. This was not
tested, but it fits the observed pattern, and it means that the overall averages
in Table~\ref{tab:4.2} combine two regimes. The long-range result also matters
most in practice, since a 15-day forecast offers the greatest benefit for flood
preparedness.

\paragraph{Comparison with the neural baselines.}
CFL-EDLSTM outperformed the centrally trained Vanilla LSTM, GRU and TCN at every
horizon, and its overall errors were roughly 28--44\% lower, depending on the
metric (Section~\ref{sec:4.5}). When GRU, TCN and XGBoost were also trained
under the clustered federated scheme (Section~\ref{sec:4.5.1}), CFL-EDLSTM still
had the lowest MAE at every horizon, so the encoder--decoder structure appears
to contribute beyond the training scheme itself, at least relative to these
three models. What the experiments still cannot separate is the effect of
clustering and federation from that of the architecture, because no
non-federated or single global encoder--decoder was trained, and the centrally
trained baselines use fewer input features. Until such a comparison is made,
the combined design is described as effective without crediting any single
component. Federating XGBoost was far less successful than federating the neural
models (Section~\ref{sec:4.5.1}), which suggests that tree ensembles do not
transfer to federated aggregation as readily.

\paragraph{Stations and clusters.}
Averages also conceal where the errors lie. XGBoost has the weakest overall RMSE
although its MAE ranks fourth, because at six low-level stations its RMSE lies
between 1.49 and 2.01~m, compared with below 0.9~m at the other 19
(Section~\ref{sec:4.10}). CFL-EDLSTM shows no comparable failures: its worst
station (MAE 0.466~m) is still better than the worst station of any baseline.
Its one clear weakness is SW291.5R, the highest-level station, where XGBoost,
GRU and Vanilla LSTM all achieved a lower MAE, possibly because a cluster model
shares parameters across stations at very different levels (a conjecture). By
cluster, the Urban cluster had the smallest errors (MAE 0.252~m) and the
Mid-river cluster the largest (0.394~m) (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}). With four
clusters this is an association, not a cause, and the ranking changes with the
metric because NSE depends on how much the water level of each cluster varies,
so MAE and RMSE are used as the main measures. These results show that
performance differs across clusters, not that clustering improves accuracy,
which would require a comparison with a global model.

\paragraph{What the model relies on.}
The SHAP analysis (Section~\ref{sec:4.9}) identifies seasonal timing (day of
year) and temperature as the strongest inputs, with rainfall accumulations
contributing less, and the most recent days of the 30-day window as the most
influential part of the input. The second finding agrees with the horizon
pattern described above: recent observations matter most, and accuracy fades as
the lead time moves away from them. The first finding should be read
cautiously. Temperature follows the monsoon cycle and may act partly as a
seasonal indicator, and correlated rainfall features share credit in SHAP, so
rainfall is not shown to be unimportant.

\paragraph{Practical meaning of the errors.}
The average error of CFL-EDLSTM is about 0.16~m at a one-day lead, 0.23~m at
three days, 0.33~m at seven days and 0.44~m at fifteen days
(Section~\ref{sec:4.8}). Whether this is adequate depends on the decision being
supported, and these are averages over all stations and events. The comparison
with FFWC (Section~\ref{sec:4.11}) provides an external reference: over a short
period in October 2026, CFL-EDLSTM had the lower MAE for the first seven days
against the FFWC 10-day forecast and for the first three days against its 5-day
forecast, with FFWC ahead at the longest lead times. This is encouraging but
rests on few points in a calm period, and FFWC serves as an operational
reference rather than validated ground truth. The web interface
(Section~\ref{sec:3.18}) makes these forecasts and comparisons available station
by station.

\paragraph{Relation to earlier work.}
Section~\ref{sec:4.13} compares the results with three earlier studies. The gain
over a plain LSTM is of a similar size to that reported by \cite{Ruma2023}, and
the modest benefit of federation reported by \cite{Mahir2025} is consistent with
the federated design adopted here. Both comparisons are indirect, because the
datasets, periods and protocols differ.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Some limitations concern the data. The records contain unexpected gaps, and a
few stations report only during the monsoon. Because gaps were filled, the
filled values are estimates rather than observations. Discharge is missing
entirely at ten of the 25 stations, and rainfall is zero in about three quarters
of the records, which leaves that variable little to contribute on most days.
Data from the upstream river network in India were not accessible, so the models
cannot see conditions beyond the border stations, which may contribute to the
weaker relative fit of the Upstream cluster (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}), although
this was not tested.

Other limitations concern the model and the evaluation. CFL-EDLSTM is not
uniformly better: up to about one day ahead, the centrally trained XGBoost is
more accurate, so CFL-EDLSTM is better described as a longer-horizon forecaster
than as a replacement for centralized models at every lead time. Each model was
trained once, with no fixed random seed and no systematic hyperparameter search,
and the federation was simulated on a single machine. No ablation separates
clustering, federation and the encoder--decoder, and the baselines use fewer
input features than CFL-EDLSTM. Finally, the FFWC comparison covers a short
post-monsoon period of hindcasts, and the SHAP results rest on a small sample of
test windows.

\subsection{Regional Benchmark: Ruma et al.}
\label{sec:4.13.1}

Ruma et al.~\cite{Ruma2023} is the closest regional counterpart. It forecasts
water level at gauge stations across the river network of Bangladesh, uses data
from the same agency and reaches lead times of up to 15 days. Seven of the
stations in this study (SW42, SW45, SW45.5, SW49, SW77, SW225 and SW294) also
appear in theirs. Their model is an LSTM whose hyperparameters are tuned by
particle swarm optimization (PSO), and it outperformed ANN, PSO-ANN and a plain
LSTM in every sub-network they tested.

The two studies differ in several further respects, as summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:4.ruma-setup}. Their stations form cascaded sub-networks in which
upstream gauges feed downstream ones, whereas the stations here are modeled
within their clusters. Absolute errors are also not compared, because the RMSE
and MAE reported in their paper could not be reconciled with their other
statistics: for their Net~1 stations, whose standard deviation is about 1.5~m,
an NSE of 0.9475 implies an RMSE near 0.34~m, yet 1.11~m is reported, and the
units are not stated. The comparison is therefore based on how much each method
improves on a plain LSTM.

\subsection{Federated Learning Counterpart: Mahir et al.}
\label{sec:4.13.2}

Mahir et al.~\cite{Mahir2025} is the closest federated study in Bangladesh and,
like this thesis, combines federated training with SHAP. They train FNN,
LSTM-RNN and TFT-CNN models for Sylhet on a 78-year dataset that includes
upstream rainfall from Meghalaya and Assam, distributed across five simulated
clients. Federation raised R\textsuperscript{2} for all three models, by 0.012
to 0.016, and SHAP identified upstream rainfall as the main driver. This modest
but consistent gain from federation supports the motivation for the federated
design used here.

The two studies nevertheless differ in what they predict. Their data are
monthly, their target is a flood-severity measure rather than a continuous water
level, and lead time is not considered, which the authors themselves list as a
limitation. Their R\textsuperscript{2} values therefore cannot be compared with
the errors in meters reported here. Their clients are random non-IID splits,
whereas the clients here are the four hydrological clusters of
Section~\ref{sec:4.3}, which gives each client a physical meaning. They also
compare federated with non-federated training directly, which this thesis does
not.

\subsection{Architectural Counterpart: Kao et al.}
\label{sec:4.13.3}

Kao et al.~\cite{Kao2020} introduced the LSTM encoder--decoder for multi-step
flood forecasting. For the Shihmen Reservoir catchment, it produced more
reliable and accurate forecasts up to six hours ahead than a feed-forward
encoder--decoder. This is the design idea adopted here: an encoder summarizes
the recent past, and a decoder produces the whole forecast sequence, in this
case 75 steps covering 15 days. Their result supports the choice of an
encoder--decoder for this type of task.

The settings differ considerably, however. Kao et al.\ forecast reservoir
inflow in hours, during typhoon events at a single catchment, whereas this study
forecasts water level at 25 stations over a continuous 16-year record with
horizons of up to 15 days. In addition, it is not possible to say how much of
the advantage of CFL-EDLSTM comes from the encoder--decoder and how much from
the clustered federated training, because no ablation separates them
(Section~\ref{sec:4.5}).

This chapter evaluated CFL-EDLSTM on 25 monitoring stations against four
baselines at seven horizons from 3~hours to 15~days. Table~\ref{tab:4.summary}
lists the main findings and the sections in which they are reported.

Four points stand out. First, CFL-EDLSTM was the most accurate model overall and
from three days onward, and its error grew far more slowly with lead time than
that of XGBoost, making it preferable at the longer horizons that matter most
for early warning. Second, when GRU and TCN were trained under the same clustered
federated scheme, and XGBoost under its prediction-level counterpart, the
encoder--decoder still gave the lowest MAE at every horizon, which supports choosing it as the model to federate. Third, its
performance was more consistent across stations than that of any baseline,
although it varied between clusters and showed one clear weakness at the
highest-level station. Fourth, its gain over a plain LSTM is of a similar size
to that of the regional benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:4.13}), and an initial check
against FFWC was encouraging, within the limits of indirect and short
comparisons. Chapter~5 draws the conclusions of the thesis, states its
limitations and suggests directions for future work.
