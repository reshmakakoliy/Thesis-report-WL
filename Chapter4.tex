\chapter{Experimental Results and Discussion}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:4.1}

This chapter reports what happened when the procedure described in Chapter~3 was actually run. It begins with the data the models were trained and tested on (Section~4.2), then moves through the clustering outcome (Section~4.3), the proposed CFL-EDLSTM's own performance (Section~4.4), and its comparison against the baseline models (Section~4.5), before breaking the results down by forecast horizon (Sections~4.6 and~4.8) and by station cluster (Section~4.7). The remaining sections turn to interpretability (Section 4.9), station-level forecasts (Section~4.10), the comparison against FFWC information (Section 4.11), and a closing discussion that places the findings alongside earlier work (Sections~4.12 and~4.13).

Throughout, model performance is judged mainly on MAE and RMSE in metres, since these stay directly comparable across architectures and are expressed in the same unit the water-level readings themselves use.

\section{Experimental Setup and Dataset Characteristics}
\label{sec:4.2}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All results in this chapter use the station-wise records of water level, rainfall, MDD, evaporation, and temperature from the 25 monitoring stations described in Section~\ref{sec:3.5}. For every station, we hold out the last 15\% of the timeline as the test set, so the models are always judged on data that comes after what they were trained on. Each model looks back over 30 days of history (150 observations) and predicts the next 15 days (75 observations). We report accuracy at the seven horizon checkpoints defined in Section~\ref{sec:3.15}. Every figure is computed after converting the outputs back to metres, and we have not rounded anything beyond what the result files themselves contain.

Training and evaluation ran on a single desktop machine with an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 six-core processor (3.60~GHz), 24~GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3060 (12~GB VRAM). The PyTorch models used CUDA where it was available, and XGBoost was set up to run on the same GPU.

\subsection{Processed Dataset}

The final dataset brings together hydrological and meteorological records from 25 gauge stations in the Brahmaputra basin. It starts on 1 January 2010 and ends on 31 March 2026, which is 5{,}934 days, or a little over sixteen years. Each station contributes 29{,}670 observations, giving 741{,}750 rows and 44 columns in total. Since every station covers the same period and uses the same set of columns, the panel is balanced. This is useful for the clustered federated setup, because each station can act as a client with the same time span and the same input structure.

\paragraph{Temporal structure.}
Observations are recorded five times a day, at 06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00, and 18:00. About 80\% of consecutive records are 3 hours apart, and the rest are the 12-hour overnight gap between 18:00 and 06:00 the next morning. We chose to keep this uneven spacing instead of forcing the data onto a regular grid. To help the models cope with it, a \texttt{delta\_hours} column stores the normalised time gap (0.25 for a 3-hour step and 1.0 for the overnight jump).

\paragraph{Variables.}
Water level is what we are forecasting, and it is the only variable measured several times a day. The other variables describe the conditions around it. Some of them are recorded at a coarser resolution, so their value stays the same across all observations within a day.

Rainfall, evaporation, and temperature come from fewer measurement points than there are water-level stations, so each station is matched to its closest relevant record and neighbouring stations sometimes share the same series. Across the 25 stations there are 12 distinct rainfall series, 7 evaporation series, and 9 temperature series. Discharge is available at 15 of the 25 stations and is recorded as zero at the other 10.

In the processed data, water level averages 12.51~mMSL (SD 11.28, range $-0.92$ to 52.90) and differs a lot from one station to another. Rainfall averages 4.79~mm with a maximum of 540~mm, and about 75\% of records are zero, which is what we would expect with a long dry season. Evaporation averages 1.67~mm, and temperature averages 25.58~\textdegree C, ranging from 8.65 to 36.83~\textdegree C.

\paragraph{Engineered features.}
To give the models some memory of recent conditions, we added look-back features. These include lagged values at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 14 days for rainfall, discharge, temperature, and evaporation (24 columns), cumulative rainfall over 3, 7, 14, and 30 days (4 columns) to reflect how saturated the catchment is, and rolling mean discharge over the same four windows (4 columns). We did not create lags of water level, because it is the target. Hour of day and day of year are each encoded as a sine and cosine pair (4 columns), so that 18:00 sits close to 06:00 and 31 December sits close to 1 January. The forecast horizons (3~h, 6~h, 12~h, and 1, 3, 7, and 15 days) are created when the training windows are built, so they do not appear as separate columns in the file.

\paragraph{Missing data.}
The raw records had gaps, mostly in rainfall, discharge, temperature, and evaporation, and the amount missing differed from station to station (see Table~\ref{tab:4.1}). Our first idea was to recover these values from outside sources, so we went through the available reports from the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre (FFWC). Unfortunately, this helped very little. The reports covered only part of the gaps and often did not match the stations or time stamps we were missing, so we could fill only a small number of values this way. The remaining gaps were filled using forward filling followed by backward filling, and the final dataset has no missing values. It is worth remembering that filled values are estimates, not measurements. The long dry-season stretches with constant values, and the 10 stations with no discharge record at all, should be kept in mind when reading the results.

\label{sec:4.3}

The stations were grouped into four clusters using the procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:3.10}: Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream, and Urban. Each cluster later serves as one client in the federated training (Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). The station membership of each cluster is listed in Table~\ref{tab:3.5}. Here we look at what the clusters actually look like in the data. Table~\ref{tab:4.cluster-features} gives the average value of each input variable per cluster.

\paragraph{Water level separates the clusters most clearly.}
The average water level falls steadily from the Upstream cluster (28.24~mMSL) through Mid-river (12.55~mMSL) and Downstream (5.75~mMSL) to the Urban cluster (2.11~mMSL). This ordering matches the names given to the clusters, so the grouping appears to reflect the position of the stations along the river and the elevation they sit at. The gap between the highest and lowest clusters is more than a factor of ten, which is a larger contrast than for any other variable in the table.

\paragraph{Temperature and evaporation differ only slightly.}
Average temperature stays within a narrow band, from 25.03~\textdegree C in the Upstream cluster to 25.49~\textdegree C in the Urban cluster, a spread of less than half a degree. Evaporation varies somewhat more in relative terms, from 1.32~mm (Upstream) to 1.90~mm (Urban), but the absolute differences are small. In other words, the clusters share a broadly similar climate, and they are separated mainly by their hydrological behaviour and not by temperature.

\paragraph{Rainfall and discharge.}
Discharge averages are close across clusters (4.27 to 5.47), with the Upstream and Urban clusters slightly higher. This comparison should be read with care, since discharge is available at only 15 of the 25 stations and is recorded as zero at the other ten (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). Cluster averages therefore partly depend on how many stations in each cluster have a discharge record. The rainfall measure stands out. The Mid-river cluster has a value of 9{,}087, which is roughly 21 to 35 times larger than the other three clusters (260 to 430). Whatever the exact reason for this, it marks Mid-river as the most distinct cluster in terms of rainfall.

\paragraph{What this means for the modelling.}
Taken together, the clusters are clearly different from one another, mostly in water level and rainfall. This supports giving each cluster its own model state instead of forcing all 25 stations to share a single set of parameters. Whether it actually improves forecasts is a separate question, and Section~\ref{sec:4.7} looks at how the model performs in each cluster.


\section{Overall Performance of CFL-EDLSTM}
\label{sec:4.4}

This section looks at how the proposed CFL-EDLSTM performs on its own before it is set against any other model. The comparison with the baselines follows in Section~\ref{sec:4.5}, and the breakdowns by horizon and by cluster come in Sections~\ref{sec:4.6} and~\ref{sec:4.7}. The evaluation follows the setup in Section~\ref{sec:4.2}: each station is scored on its own chronological test split, using the cluster-specific model assigned to it, with a 30-day input history and a 15-day forecast. In total, the model is evaluated on 111{,}275 test windows across the 25 stations, and all errors are reported in metres after inverse transformation.

Table~\ref{tab:4.cfl-overall} gives the overall results.

\paragraph{Size of the errors.}
On average, the model's forecasts are off by about 0.32~m (MAE), and the RMSE is 0.52~m. These errors are small next to the spread of water levels in the data, where the standard deviation is 11.28~mMSL and levels range from $-0.92$ to 52.90~mMSL (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). The RMSE is roughly 1.6 times the MAE. Since RMSE gives more weight to large errors, this tells us that most forecasts are close to the observations while a smaller share carry larger errors. Where those larger errors occur is examined in the horizon and station results that follow.

\paragraph{Goodness of fit.}
The fit metrics are all very close to their maximum value of 1. NSE \cite{NashSutcliffe1970} and R\textsuperscript{2} are both 0.9979, EVS is 0.9979, and IOA is 0.9995. The NSE and R\textsuperscript{2} values are identical here, and they are reported separately only for completeness. EVS is almost the same as NSE, and because EVS ignores constant bias while NSE does not, this suggests the model shows little systematic over- or under-prediction. A high NSE is not surprising for water-level series with strong seasonal cycles, since even a simple model can capture much of that variation. For this reason, we read MAE and RMSE as the more informative measures, and they are the ones used for comparison in the rest of the chapter.

Figure~\ref{fig:4.cfl-cluster-forecast} compares predicted and observed water levels over the 15-day forecasting period at four representative stations, one from each cluster. The model captures the general downward trend at Kaunia, Fulchari, and Taraghat, although it does not fully follow the sharper declines in the observed levels. At Mirpur, the predictions remain relatively close to the observed levels but miss some short-term fluctuations. Overall, the plots show that the model captures broad water-level trends, but has difficulty reproducing sudden changes, with differences across the four stations.

\paragraph{What the overall figures hide.}
These numbers average over all 25 stations and over every step of the 15-day forecast, so they should be read as a summary and not as the error at any single point. The results behind them vary considerably. As Table~\ref{tab:4.3} shows, the MAE is about 0.15~m at the 3-hour checkpoint and grows to about 0.44~m at 15 days, and Table~\ref{tab:4.2a} shows station-level MAE ranging from 0.177 to 0.466~m. Sections~\ref{sec:4.6} and~\ref{sec:4.7} look at these differences in detail. Before that, Section~\ref{sec:4.5} places the overall figures in Table~\ref{tab:4.cfl-overall} next to those of the baseline models to show how they compare.

CFL-EDLSTM gives the best value on every metric in the table. Its MAE of 0.321~m is about 35\% lower than that of the next best model, Vanilla LSTM (0.496~m), and its RMSE of 0.521~m is about 28\% lower than Vanilla LSTM's 0.722~m. Against XGBoost the reduction is larger, roughly 40\% in MAE and 49\% in RMSE, and the MSE falls by about three quarters. Relative to GRU and TCN, MAE is lower by about 36\% and 44\%, and RMSE by about 30\% and 39\%. NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} follows the same ordering, from 0.9979 for CFL-EDLSTM down to 0.9919 for XGBoost.

XGBoost's position depends on which metric we look at. On MAE it ranks fourth, ahead of TCN, but on RMSE it is the weakest of the five models (1.025~m). Its RMSE is also 1.9 times its MAE, compared with about 1.5 to 1.6 for the other models. Since RMSE penalises large errors more heavily, this suggests that a smaller number of large errors weighs heavily on XGBoost's score. Sections~\ref{sec:4.6} and~\ref{sec:4.10} show where they come from: the longer forecast horizons and a handful of stations.

Some earlier studies report XGBoost matching or outperforming LSTM in hydrological settings \cite{Hameed2025}. That holds in our results only at short lead times (Section~\ref{sec:4.6}), which fits the broader observation that no single architecture wins everywhere \cite{Aghenda2025}. The most direct comparison for the proposed design is the one with Vanilla LSTM, since both use LSTM cells. The improvement over it comes from the encoder--decoder structure and the clustered federated training together, and this table cannot separate the two. The role of clustering is examined in Section~\ref{sec:4.7}.

Because NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} \cite{NashSutcliffe1970} is at or above 0.991 for every model, it does little to separate them. The differences show up in MAE and RMSE, where they can be read directly in metres. The LSTM--GRU comparison deserves one caution: GRU was trained jointly across all stations while LSTM was trained station by station (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}), so the gap between them reflects that difference in data representation as well as the cell design, and should not be attributed to gating alone.

One further detail concerns the window counts. XGBoost is evaluated on 109{,}425 test windows and the three neural baselines on 105{,}675, a difference of 3{,}750, or exactly 150 per station, the length of one input window. This most likely reflects how XGBoost's windows may draw lag inputs from just before the test boundary, while the neural baselines' windows must lie entirely inside the test split (Section~\ref{sec:3.9.5}). CFL-EDLSTM is evaluated on 111{,}275 windows, which is 224 more per station than the neural baselines and most likely comes from the common timestamp axis the federated pipeline builds across stations (Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). Since every metric is averaged over the windows a model sees, the five models are scored on slightly different window sets. The differences are small relative to the totals, but the comparison is not strictly like for like.

The overall figures in Table~\ref{tab:4.2} average across every forecast step and every station, which hides how performance changes with lead time and from one station to the next. Section~\ref{sec:4.6} looks at the first of these, and Section~\ref{sec:4.10} at the second.

Which model comes first depends on the horizon. XGBoost has the lowest MAE and RMSE at the four shortest checkpoints (3 hours to 1 day), while CFL-EDLSTM has the lowest at 3, 7, and 15 days, so the two cross somewhere between 1 and 3 days. At 3 hours XGBoost's MAE is about 0.071~m, roughly half of CFL-EDLSTM's 0.146~m and about 3.7 times lower than Vanilla LSTM's. By 1 day its lead over CFL-EDLSTM has narrowed to 0.127 against 0.160~m. From 3 days onward the order reverses and the gap widens quickly. CFL-EDLSTM's MAE is about 25\% lower than XGBoost's at 3 days, 56\% lower at 7 days, and 62\% lower at 15 days (0.436 against 1.149~m). The RMSE gap at 15 days is larger still, 0.690 against 2.372~m, a reduction of about 71\%.

The crossover comes from how differently the models' errors grow. XGBoost's MAE rises about 16-fold from 3 hours to 15 days, against roughly 3.0-fold for CFL-EDLSTM, 2.6-fold for Vanilla LSTM, and 1.9-fold for both GRU and TCN. At 7 and 15 days XGBoost is the weakest of all five models on both metrics. A plausible reason is that it relies heavily on recent lagged values, which are very informative for short lead times but lose value as the horizon extends, although we did not test this directly. The neural models start from a higher floor but degrade much more gently. CFL-EDLSTM's 3-hour MAE is already about half of Vanilla LSTM's, and its MAE is almost flat over the first day (0.146 to 0.160~m).

CFL-EDLSTM has a lower MAE and RMSE than every neural baseline at every horizon. At 3 hours its MAE is about 44\% below Vanilla LSTM's, the best neural baseline at that horizon. At 15 days, where GRU is the strongest neural baseline, CFL-EDLSTM still has an MAE about 25\% lower (0.436 against 0.583~m) and an RMSE about 18\% lower (0.690 against 0.838~m).

Among the neural baselines the ordering is not fixed. Vanilla LSTM beats GRU at every horizon from 3 hours through 7 days on both metrics, but at 15 days GRU takes over (MAE 0.583 against 0.675~m; RMSE 0.838 against 0.990~m). TCN behaves differently again. Its MAE is almost flat from 3 hours to 1 day (0.416 to 0.419~m), which suggests something other than forecast difficulty sets its floor at short horizons. It edges past GRU at 7 days on both metrics, but it is the weakest neural model at 15 days. It also trails Vanilla LSTM at every horizon, a less favourable showing than the competitive TCN performance reported by \cite{Lin2021}, though on a different dataset.

The general rise in error with lead time is the pattern earlier multi-step flood-forecasting studies describe \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Yuan2023}. Figures~\ref{fig:4.4} and~\ref{fig:4.5} show how the five models compare across horizons.

\paragraph{Three phases of error growth.}
The error does not rise at a steady pace. Over the first day it is almost flat: MAE moves from 0.146~m at 3 hours to 0.160~m at 1 day, an increase of only about 9\%, and the 3-hour and 6-hour values are practically identical. This suggests that at short range the error is set mostly by a floor, such as noise in the observations and the model's own approximation error, and not by how hard the forecast is. We did not test this directly.

Between 1 and 7 days the error rises fastest. MAE grows from 0.160~m to 0.226~m at 3 days and 0.332~m at 7 days, roughly doubling in total, which corresponds to an increase of about 0.033~m per day between 1 and 3 days and about 0.026~m per day between 3 and 7 days. Beyond 7 days the growth slows again. MAE rises by a further 0.104~m over the next eight days, about 0.013~m per day, and reaches 0.436~m at 15 days. So error keeps increasing with lead time but does so at a decreasing rate, and the 15-day forecast does not break down.

A likely reason for the steady rise is that the model's inputs contain only past observations. Rainfall that has not yet fallen cannot inform a forecast, and the further ahead the model looks, the more of the outcome depends on conditions it has never seen. This is consistent with the general pattern reported for multi-step flood forecasting \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Yuan2023}.

\paragraph{Shape of the error.}
The ratio of RMSE to MAE stays between 1.54 and 1.58 at every horizon. Since RMSE penalises large errors more than MAE does, a stable ratio suggests that the overall shape of the error distribution does not change much with lead time. Longer horizons appear to shift errors upward more or less uniformly, instead of adding a heavier tail of occasional large misses. This is an indirect reading based on two summary metrics, and the actual error distributions were not examined.

\paragraph{Gap to XGBoost.}
The last column of Table~\ref{tab:4.8} shows how the comparison with XGBoost changes with horizon. At 3 hours CFL-EDLSTM's MAE is about twice XGBoost's (2.06 times). The ratio falls to 1.26 at 1 day, drops below 1 between 1 and 3 days, and reaches 0.75 at 3 days, 0.44 at 7 days, and 0.38 at 15 days . XGBoost's advantage is therefore confined to roughly the first day, and from 3 days onward CFL-EDLSTM is clearly more accurate. This crossover owes as much to how quickly XGBoost deteriorates (about 16-fold in MAE over the same range) as to CFL-EDLSTM's gentle growth. Differences of this size are unlikely to be due to run-to-run variation alone, although we did not repeat the runs.

\section{Comparison with FFWC}
\label{sec:4.11}

\section{Discussion of Findings}
\label{sec:4.12}

This chapter set out to test whether a clustered federated encoder--decoder LSTM can forecast water level across heterogeneous stations at horizons from 3 hours to 15 days. The short answer from the results is yes, with qualifications. CFL-EDLSTM gave the lowest overall error of the five models and kept its accuracy as the lead time grew. The sections below discuss why the results may look the way they do, what they imply in practice, and where they should not be over-read.

\paragraph{Accuracy depends on the horizon.}
No single model was best everywhere. XGBoost was the most accurate up to a one-day lead, and CFL-EDLSTM was the most accurate from three days onward (Section~\ref{sec:4.6}). The gap at the two ends is large. At 3 hours XGBoost's MAE is about half of CFL-EDLSTM's, whereas at 15 days CFL-EDLSTM's MAE of 0.436~m is 62\% lower than XGBoost's 1.149~m. The difference comes mainly from how fast error grows: XGBoost's MAE rises about 16-fold over the horizon range, against about 3-fold for CFL-EDLSTM. A plausible reading is that recent lagged values are very informative for short leads and lose value as the horizon extends, while a sequence model that carries information through an encoder and decoder holds up better. We did not test this, but it is consistent with the pattern in the results. It also means that the overall averages in Table~\ref{tab:4.2} mix two regimes, and a single ranking would hide this. The 15-day horizon is also where flood preparedness benefits most from a forecast, so the long-range result matters more than a short-range gap.

\paragraph{Why the neural baselines trail CFL-EDLSTM.}
CFL-EDLSTM was better than Vanilla LSTM, GRU, and TCN at every horizon, by roughly 28--44\% depending on the metric. The most direct comparison is with Vanilla LSTM, since both use LSTM cells. The difference therefore comes from the encoder--decoder structure and the clustered federated training together, and the experiments here cannot say how much each contributes. Separating them would need two extra runs: a non-encoder--decoder model trained under the same clustered scheme, and a single global encoder--decoder trained on all stations. Until then we describe the combined design as effective, without crediting any one component.

\paragraph{Where the models fail.}
Averages also hide where the errors sit. XGBoost's overall RMSE is the weakest of the five models even though its MAE ranks fourth, and Section~\ref{sec:4.10} shows why: at six low-level stations its RMSE lies between 1.49 and 2.01~m, against below 0.9~m at the other 19. We have no verified explanation for this. CFL-EDLSTM, by contrast, shows no comparable failures. Its worst station (MAE 0.466~m) is still better than the worst station of any baseline. Its one clear weakness is SW291.5R, the highest-level station, where XGBoost, GRU, and Vanilla LSTM were all more accurate. A station sitting far above the others in level may be poorly served by a model whose cluster shares parameters across stations, but this remains a conjecture.

\paragraph{Differences between clusters.}
Performance also varied by cluster (Section~\ref{sec:4.7}). The Urban cluster had the smallest errors (MAE 0.252~m) and the Mid-river cluster the largest (0.394~m, with an RMSE about twice the Urban value). The Mid-river cluster is also the one that stands out most in rainfall (Section~\ref{sec:4.3}), so heavier or more variable rainfall may make stations harder to forecast from past observations alone. With only four clusters this is an association and not a cause. The ranking also changes with the metric: Downstream and Urban lead on NSE, while Upstream, despite low absolute errors, scores lowest. Relative metrics depend on how much each cluster's water level varies, so we consider MAE and RMSE the safer basis for comparing clusters. An important limit is that the results show performance differing across clusters, not that clustering improves accuracy. That would require comparison with a single global model on the same stations.

\paragraph{Practical meaning of the errors.}

\paragraph{Relation to earlier work.}
Section~\ref{sec:4.13} compares the results with three earlier studies. In short, the accuracy and the gradual decline with lead time are in line with the regional benchmark of \cite{Ruma2023}, and the modest benefit of federation reported by \cite{Mahir2025} is consistent with the motivation for the federated design. These comparisons are indirect, because the datasets, periods, and protocols differ.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Several limitations should be kept in mind when reading these results, and the first group concerns the data. The records contain unexpected gaps in certain periods, and a few stations report only during the monsoon, so they carry no readings for the rest of the year. Gaps were filled by interpolation and forward and backward filling, which means filled values are estimates, not observations. Discharge is missing entirely at ten of the 25 stations, and rainfall is zero on about three quarters of records, which leaves that variable little to work with on most days. Data from the upstream river network in India were also not accessible, so the models cannot see conditions beyond the border stations, even though much of the flow entering Bangladesh comes from catchments outside the country (Section~1.2). This plausibly contributes to the weaker fit seen in the Upstream cluster (Section~4.7), although that link has not been tested.

The remaining limitations concern the model and the evaluation. CFL-EDLSTM performs poorly at some stations, with average errors of up to about 1~m, and the causes are still being investigated, so those stations should be read as open questions and not dismissed as noise. The framework's advantage is also not uniform across horizons. At short range, up to about one day ahead, the centralized XGBoost baseline is more accurate than CFL-EDLSTM, and CFL-EDLSTM leads only from three days onward (Section~4.6), so it is better described as a longer-horizon forecaster than as a replacement for centralized models at every lead time.

\subsection{Regional Benchmark: Ruma et al.}
\label{sec:4.13.1}

Ruma et al.~\cite{Ruma2023} is the closest regional counterpart to this work. It forecasts water level at gauge stations across Bangladesh's river network, uses data from the same agency, and reaches lead times of up to 15 days. Seven of our stations (SW42, SW45, SW45.5, SW49, SW77, SW225, and SW294) also appear in their study. Their model is an LSTM whose hyperparameters are tuned by particle swarm optimisation (PSO), and it outperformed ANN, PSO-ANN, and a plain LSTM in every sub-network they tested.

The two studies differ in several further ways (Table~\ref{tab:4.ruma-setup}). Their stations are arranged as cascaded sub-networks in which upstream gauges feed downstream ones, whereas ours are modelled station by station within their clusters. Because of these differences, and because the RMSE and MAE reported in their paper could not be reconciled with the other statistics in the same paper, we do not compare absolute errors. \subsection{Federated Learning Counterpart: Mahir et al.}
\label{sec:4.13.2}

Mahir et al.~\cite{Mahir2025} is the nearest federated study in Bangladesh and, like this thesis, pairs federated training with SHAP. They train FNN, LSTM-RNN, and TFT-CNN models for Sylhet on a 78-year dataset that includes upstream rainfall from Meghalaya and Assam, split across five simulated clients. Federation raised R\textsuperscript{2} for all three models, by 0.012 to 0.016, and SHAP pointed to upstream rainfall as the main driver. A modest but consistent gain from federation supports the motivation for the federated design used here.

The two studies still differ in what they predict. Their data are monthly and the target is a flood-severity measure, not a continuous water level, and lead time is not considered; the authors themselves list longer lead times as a limitation. Their R\textsuperscript{2} therefore cannot be set against our errors in metres. Their clients are random non-IID splits, whereas ours are the four hydrological clusters of Section~\ref{sec:4.3}, which gives each client a physical meaning. 
\subsection{Architectural Counterpart: Kao et al.}
\label{sec:4.13.3}

Kao et al.~\cite{Kao2020} introduced the LSTM encoder--decoder for multi-step flood forecasting. On the Shihmen Reservoir catchment, they showed that it gave more reliable and accurate forecasts, up to six hours ahead, than a feed-forward encoder--decoder. This is the design idea we use: an encoder summarises the recent past, and a decoder produces the whole forecast sequence, here 75 steps covering 15 days. Their result supports the choice of an encoder--decoder for this kind of task.

The setting is very different, however. They forecast reservoir inflow in hours, during typhoon events at a single catchment, while we forecast water level at 25 stations over a continuous 16-year record with horizons of up to 15 days. We also cannot say how much of CFL-EDLSTM's advantage comes from the encoder--decoder structure and how much from the clustered federated training, because no ablation separates the two yet (Section~\ref{sec:4.5}).

Three points stand out. First, CFL-EDLSTM was the most accurate model overall and from three days onward, and its error grew far more slowly with lead time than XGBoost's, which made it clearly preferable at the longer horizons that matter most for early warning. Second, its performance was more even across stations than any baseline's, although it varied between clusters and showed one clear weakness at the highest-level station. Third, the comparison with earlier studies (Section~\ref{sec:4.13}) suggests the results are competitive with the regional benchmark, within the limits of an indirect comparison.
