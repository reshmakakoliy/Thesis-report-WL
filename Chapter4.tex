\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:4.1}

This chapter reports what happened when the procedure described in Chapter~3 was actually run. It begins with the data the models were trained and tested on (Section~4.2), then moves through the clustering outcome (Section~4.3), the proposed CFL-EDLSTM's own performance (Section~4.4), and its comparison against the baseline models (Section~4.5), before breaking the results down by forecast horizon (Sections~4.6 and~4.8) and by station cluster (Section~4.7). The remaining sections turn to interpretability (Section~4.9), station-level forecasts (Section~4.10), the comparison against FFWC information (Section~4.11), and a closing discussion that places the findings alongside earlier work (Sections~4.12 and~4.13).

Throughout, model performance is judged mainly on MAE and RMSE in metres, since these stay directly comparable across architectures and are expressed in the same unit the water-level readings themselves use.

\section{Experimental Setup and Dataset Characteristics}
\label{sec:4.2}

Results in this chapter come from station-wise data --- water level, rainfall, MDD, evaporation, and temperature, at five observations per day --- across the 25 monitoring stations described in Section~\ref{sec:3.5}. Every model is evaluated on a chronological 15\% test split held out independently for each station, using an input history of 30 days (150 observations) and a forecast horizon of 15 days (75 observations), reported at the seven horizon checkpoints defined in Section~\ref{sec:3.15}. All model performance figures are computed after inverse-transforming outputs back into the original water-level units (metres), and none has been rounded beyond what the underlying result files report. Training and evaluation ran on an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 six-core processor (3.60~GHz) with 24~GB of RAM and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3060 (12~GB VRAM); PyTorch models used CUDA where available, and XGBoost was configured for the same device.

Each entry in Table~\ref{tab:4.1} is built from 25 rows of per-station descriptive statistics --- one mean, SD, minimum, maximum, and missing percentage per station, per variable --- rather than from the raw observation-level data directly, so it is worth being explicit about how each column was pooled across stations.

% Min and Max are exact. The lowest value recorded anywhere in the dataset has to be the lowest of the 25 station-level minimums, and the same logic applies to the maximum, so no information is lost by pooling this way: Water Level's $-0.92$~m minimum comes from station SW147.5, and its 52.9~m maximum from SW291.5R.

% Mean is the unweighted average of the 25 station means --- for Water Level, the sum of all 25 station means divided by 25, giving 12.528~m; for Discharge, the same calculation but divided by 15, since the ten stations without a discharge sensor are excluded from both the sum and the count rather than treated as zero. This is a reasonable summary but not, strictly, the true pooled mean of every individual observation: the mathematically correct pooled mean would weight each station by how many valid readings it actually contributed, and that per-station observation count was not available from the summary file this table was built from. Two stations with very different record lengths count equally in the average reported here, which is worth keeping in mind if a station with an unusually short record turns out to be pulling a variable's mean in one direction.

% SD is the weakest number in the table, which is why it carries the asterisk. It is simply the average of the 25 stations' own standard deviations, and that calculation only captures how spread out readings are \emph{within} each station --- it says nothing about how much the stations' means differ \emph{from each other}. Water level ranges from a mean of under 2~m at some stations to over 50~m at others, reflecting different physical baselines along the river network, so the between-station spread is almost certainly large. A properly pooled SD, using the law of total variance, would combine the within-station variance with that between-station term and would very likely come out meaningfully higher than the 2.033~m reported here. Computing it correctly needs the raw per-station observation counts (or the original time series), neither of which this table draws from, so it is reported as-is with this caveat attached rather than corrected with an unsupported estimate.

% Missing~\% is reported two ways for exactly this reason. The 68.44\% figure for Discharge in the main table is the average missing percentage across all 25 stations, and it comes out high mainly because the ten stations with no discharge sensor at all are counted as 100\% missing rather than excluded. The 47.41\% figure in the footnote instead averages only across the 15 stations that do have a sensor, which is the more useful number for judging how gappy that instrument's record actually is once it exists. The two answer different questions --- the first is closer to "how complete is discharge across the full station panel," the second to "how reliable is the discharge sensor where one is installed" --- and both are kept because both matter for different parts of the pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:3.9.2}.

% With that groundwork in place, two things stand out in the table itself. Missingness is uneven: water level (3.38\%), rainfall (0.48\%), and evaporation (1.66\%) are largely complete, while temperature is missing for 24.08\% of records and discharge for 68.44\%. Much of that reflects coverage rather than failure --- discharge is measured at only 15 of the 25 stations, and even among those the missing share is 47.41\%, while temperature's collection period is shorter than the study period (Table~\ref{tab:3.2}). It is also why discharge is never filled from a global average or borrowed from another station (Section~\ref{sec:3.9.2}).

% The second point is the extremes. The evaporation range ($-145.796$ to 115.15~mm) lies far outside any physically plausible daily value and most likely reflects recording errors in the source records rather than real evaporation; it is noted here for transparency rather than corrected, since the underlying cause has not been confirmed. Rainfall, by contrast, is heavily skewed as expected for a monsoon climate, with a mean of 4.753~mm against a maximum of 540~mm.

XGBoost comes out on top, with the lowest overall MAE and RMSE of the four. That fits the role Chapter~3 gave it as a sanity-check baseline: the lag and rolling features it works from already capture much of the predictable structure in this dataset, so it sets a bar the sequence models must approach or beat rather than one they clear simply by being more complex. It also fits the wider picture in the literature, where XGBoost has matched or outperformed LSTM in comparable hydrological settings \cite{Hameed2025} and no single architecture wins everywhere \cite{Aghenda2025}.

Below XGBoost, Vanilla LSTM and GRU sit close together (overall MAE 0.496 and 0.502~m), with TCN clearly behind at 0.574~m. Because NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} \cite{NashSutcliffe1970} is already at or above 0.994 for every model, it does little to separate them; the differences show up in MAE and RMSE, where they can be read directly in metres. The LSTM--GRU comparison deserves one caution: GRU was trained jointly across all stations while LSTM was trained station by station (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}), so the gap between them reflects that difference in data representation as well as the cell design, and should not be attributed to gating alone.

One further detail concerns the window counts. XGBoost is evaluated on 109{,}425 test windows and the three neural models on 105{,}675 --- a difference of 3{,}750, or exactly 150 per station, the length of one input window. This most likely reflects how XGBoost's windows may draw lag inputs from just before the test boundary while the neural models' windows must lie entirely inside the test split (Section~\ref{sec:3.9.5}); the effect on the comparison is small.

The spread across stations is considerably wider than the overall averages in Table~\ref{tab:4.2} suggest on their own. For XGBoost, station MAE ranges from 0.182~m at SW294 up to 0.429~m at SW63 --- more than double, within a model whose overall MAE is 0.303~m. TCN shows the widest spread of any model by far: its MAE at SW62 (0.399~m) is actually lower than XGBoost's worst station, while at SW15J it reaches 2.648~m, nearly seven times higher. That is a wider range within one model than separates XGBoost from TCN on the overall table.

A pattern worth flagging, even before the formal cluster-wise analysis in Section~4.7: the stations where every model, and TCN especially, struggles most --- SW15J, SW49A, SW49, SW228.5, SW97, SW46.9R --- sit predominantly in the Middle and Downstream clusters (Table~\ref{tab:3.5}), while several of the consistently easiest stations for every model --- SW179, SW180, SW7.5, SW294, SW291.5R --- are Urban or Upstream. This is only a visual pattern at this stage, not a formal cluster-level result, but it points in the direction the clustering argument in Chapters~1 and~2 anticipated: a single global model is being asked to perform well across stations whose difficulty differs by a wide margin, and Section~4.7 returns to this with the proper cluster-level breakdown once CFL-EDLSTM's own results are available for the comparison.

\label{sec:4.6}

XGBoost has the lowest MAE and RMSE at every one of the seven horizons. Its lead is largest where forecasts are easiest: at 3 hours its MAE is about 0.058~m, roughly 6~cm and more than four times lower than Vanilla LSTM's. That lead narrows steadily with lead time. By 15 days XGBoost's MAE is 0.449~m, about 1.5 times lower than LSTM's and 1.3 times lower than GRU's. Its error also grows fastest in relative terms, rising about 7.7-fold in MAE from 3 hours to 15 days, against roughly 2.6-fold for LSTM and 1.9-fold for both GRU and TCN. The neural models start from a higher floor but degrade more gently, so the gap to XGBoost closes as the horizon lengthens.

Among the neural baselines the ordering is not fixed. Vanilla LSTM beats GRU at every horizon from 3 hours through 7 days on both metrics, but at 15 days GRU takes over (MAE 0.583 against 0.675~m; RMSE 0.838 against 0.990~m). That makes GRU the strongest neural baseline at the longest horizon, and so the neural figure CFL-EDLSTM most needs to beat there. TCN behaves differently again. Its MAE is almost flat from 3 hours to 1 day (0.416 to 0.419~m), and its 3-hour error is larger than XGBoost's at 7 days, which suggests something other than forecast difficulty sets its floor at short horizons. It does edge past GRU at 7 days on both metrics, but it is the weakest model at 15 days. It also trails Vanilla LSTM at every horizon, a less favourable showing than the competitive TCN performance reported by \cite{Lin2021}, though on a different dataset.

The general rise in error with lead time is the pattern earlier multi-step flood-forecasting studies describe \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Yuan2023}. These four baselines settle the bar the proposed model has to clear, and Figures~\ref{fig:4.4} and~\ref{fig:4.5} show it visually.
