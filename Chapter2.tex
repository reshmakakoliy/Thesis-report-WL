\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{Ch_Chapter2}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter reviews the literature behind the proposed Clustered Federated
Learning Encoder--Decoder LSTM (CFL-EDLSTM) framework, moving from the broad to
the specific. It begins with flood and water-level forecasting and the data it
uses (Sections~\ref{sec:2.2} and~\ref{sec:2.3}), then covers statistical,
machine-learning, deep-learning and tree-based approaches
(Sections~\ref{sec:2.4}--\ref{sec:2.7}). It turns next to multi-horizon
forecasting and station heterogeneity (Sections~\ref{sec:2.8}
and~\ref{sec:2.9}), which lead into federated and clustered federated learning
(Sections~\ref{sec:2.10} and~\ref{sec:2.11}), and then to explainable AI and
visualization (Sections~\ref{sec:2.12} and~\ref{sec:2.13}). The chapter ends
with a critical comparison of the studies (Section~\ref{sec:2.14}), the research
gap (Section~\ref{sec:2.15}) and a short summary (Section~\ref{sec:2.16}).
Figure~\ref{fig:lit-evolution} gives an overview of how forecasting approaches
have developed.

\section{Flood and Water-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.2}

Flood and water-level forecasting means predicting future river conditions
(water level, discharge or flood occurrence) from historical hydrological and
meteorological records, with early warning as the goal. A review of 363
flood-prediction studies from 2018 to 2025 found that machine learning and deep
learning now dominate the field and that no single architecture wins in every
study \cite{Aghenda2025}. A related review adds that data quality,
interpretability and computational cost remain recurring difficulties
\cite{Kumar2023}. Bangladesh has its own body of work, drawing mostly on
records from the Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB) and the Bangladesh
Meteorological Department (BMD)
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024}. It
shows that data-driven forecasting is feasible here, although the studies differ
considerably in the inputs they use.

\section{Hydrological and Meteorological Data}
\label{sec:2.3}

The choice of data is one of the clearest differences across this literature
and part of the motivation for this thesis. Some studies use a single variable:
Ruma et al.\ forecast water level from water-level records alone
\cite{Ruma2023}, and Islam et al.\ forecast discharge from discharge alone at
two Old Brahmaputra stations, acknowledging that leaving out precipitation is a
limitation \cite{Islam2026a}. Others combine variables: water level with
rainfall and temperature \cite{Islam2026b}, discharge with areal rainfall and
evaporation \cite{Cui2022}, or a reduced set of 16 meteorological features
\cite{Rajab2023}. A third group uses meteorology only, predicting flood
occurrence from BMD records \cite{Rakin2023,HemelSakib2024}. No Bangladeshi
study reviewed here combines discharge, water level, rainfall, temperature and
evaporation in one framework, a point taken up in Section~\ref{sec:2.15}.

\section{Traditional Statistical Approaches}
\label{sec:2.4}

Before machine learning, flood forecasting relied mainly on physically based
hydrological models and classical regression. Little of the literature
collected here is purely statistical, but two studies keep regression in their
comparisons. Rajab et al.\ found Random Forest ($R^2 = 0.768$) and Polynomial
Regression ($R^2 = 0.764$) among the stronger models \cite{Rajab2023}, and Islam
et al.\ kept Linear Regression beside tree-based models and LSTM
\cite{Islam2026b}. Regression remains a simple, interpretable baseline, but it
is generally matched or beaten by tree ensembles and deep learning once the
relationships become nonlinear and time-dependent, as they are in hydrology.

\section{Machine Learning-Based Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.5}

Ensemble and kernel-based methods are widely used for flood-related
forecasting. For flood occurrence in Bangladesh, Rakin et al.\ compared ten
classifiers on 65 years of BMD data, with LightGBM (97.76\%) just ahead of
Random Forest (97.71\%) \cite{Rakin2023}, while Hemel and Sakib found Gradient
Boosting strongest at 95.38\% \cite{HemelSakib2024}. For continuous targets,
Islam et al.\ found LSTM and BiLSTM ahead of Random Forest, LightGBM and Support
Vector Regression for discharge, though the others were not far behind
\cite{Islam2026a}, and Hossain et al.\ compared six models, including XGBoost,
for monthly extreme water level \cite{Hossain2025}. The clearest result is that
of Hameed et al., where XGBoost beat both LSTM and Random Forest for monthly
runoff in a glacierized catchment ($R^2 = 0.904$, NSE $= 0.797$)
\cite{Hameed2025}, a reminder that gradient-boosted trees are a competitive
method and not merely a fallback.

\section{Deep Learning for Water-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.6}

Most recent activity is in deep learning. In one review, LSTM was the most
common architecture, at 21\% of implementations \cite{Aghenda2025}, and LSTM and
GRU are singled out for capturing temporal dependencies, with hybrids such as
ConvLSTM adding spatial structure \cite{Kumar2023}. The reason is that ordinary
RNNs suffer vanishing and exploding gradients, which LSTM's gating is designed
to avoid \cite{Waqas2024}. The subsections below cover LSTM, GRU, the
encoder--decoder LSTM and TCN.

\subsection{LSTM}
\label{sec:2.6.1}

Ruma et al.\ give a direct Bangladeshi example: a particle-swarm-optimized LSTM
forecasting water level across Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna stations reached
an NSE of about 0.95 at 15 days \cite{Ruma2023}. A review of many LSTM variants
concludes that LSTM generally beats plain RNNs and GRUs in hydrology, though
hyperparameter sensitivity and weak generalization across settings remain
problems \cite{Waqas2024}, consistent with LSTM's lead in the wider literature
\cite{Aghenda2025}. None of these studies trains LSTM in a federated or
clustered setting.

\subsection{GRU}
\label{sec:2.6.2}

GRU simplifies LSTM's gating while still handling temporal dependencies. Ni et
al.\ paired it with TCN and kernel-density error estimation and reported a 13\%
RMSE reduction over standalone LSTM, GRU and TCN \cite{Ni2024}, and Li et al.\
included it among several baselines for semi-arid streamflow \cite{Li2024}. GRU
therefore looks like a lighter alternative to LSTM and not a clear improvement
on it, and this thesis treats it as a baseline (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}) and not
as a candidate for the core architecture.

\subsection{Encoder--Decoder LSTM}
\label{sec:2.6.3}

The encoder--decoder LSTM casts multi-step forecasting as a sequence-to-sequence
problem: an encoder compresses past observations into a fixed representation
and a decoder unrolls it into future predictions. Kao et al.\ applied it to
inflow forecasting at Taiwan's Shihmen Reservoir, one to six hours ahead, using
hourly data for 23 typhoon events. Their LSTM-ED beat a feed-forward
encoder--decoder by about 3\% in RMSE at one hour and about 38\% at six hours,
so the benefit grew with the horizon \cite{Kao2020}. Cui et al.\ fed an
exogenous process-model forecast into the decoder across 3--12 hour horizons in
two Chinese basins; it degraded least as the horizon lengthened, reaching NSE of
0.81 and 0.93 at 12 hours \cite{Cui2022}. More recently, a Seq2Seq LSTM
surrogate outperformed a standard LSTM for real-time multi-step flood forecasting
\cite{Lim2025}, and a Time Residual LSTM was proposed to address the degradation
at longer lead times seen in Kao et al.'s baseline \cite{Kao2020,Yuan2023}.
Across these studies, encoder--decoder designs hold up better at longer horizons
than their comparators. Where horizons are reported, however, they stop at 12
hours, and all four studies are centralized. This thesis extends the idea to 15
days and to 25 stations grouped into four clusters.

\subsection{TCN}
\label{sec:2.6.4}

The Temporal Convolutional Network uses dilated causal convolutions instead of
recurrence. Lin et al.\ found TCN competitive with LSTM for multi-lead-time flood
forecasting, though it lost ground at longer lead times \cite{Lin2021}; an
Attention-TCN cut MAE by 6.9--25\% relative to a standard LSTM on semi-arid
streamflow \cite{Li2024}; and a GRU-TCN hybrid outperformed a standalone TCN
\cite{Ni2024}. At the long end, combining Empirical Mode Decomposition with TCN
and GRU reached $R^2 = 0.9922$ and MAPE $= 3.25\%$ for 15-day streamflow, at
considerably greater complexity \cite{Li2025}. TCN is a credible non-recurrent
alternative to LSTM and earns its place as a baseline here
(Section~\ref{sec:3.13}), although it has not been tested in a multi-station or
federated setting.

\section{Tree-Based Models}
\label{sec:2.7}

Gradient-boosted trees sit apart from the regression methods of
Section~\ref{sec:2.4} and the neural architectures above, and, as
Section~\ref{sec:2.5} noted, they hold up well in hydrological forecasting.

\subsection{XGBoost}
\label{sec:2.7.1}

XGBoost appears repeatedly in this literature, both alone and as a comparison
baseline. Hameed et al.\ (Section~\ref{sec:2.5}) give the strongest evidence of
it matching or beating LSTM \cite{Hameed2025}. In Bangladesh it appears among the
models compared at Islampur and Sarishabari
\cite{Hossain2025,Islam2026b} and among the classifiers of Rakin et al.\ and
Hemel and Sakib, who report 94.47\% accuracy for it
\cite{Rakin2023,HemelSakib2024}. Each of these evaluations is confined to a
single station or a single-site classification task, whereas this thesis runs
XGBoost across all four clusters and seven horizons (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}).

\section{Multi-Horizon Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.8}

Forecast accuracy tends to fall as the horizon lengthens, and several studies
document it directly. Kao et al.'s encoder--decoder advantage widened with the
horizon \cite{Kao2020}, Cui et al.'s exogenous-input design degraded least up to
12 hours \cite{Cui2022}, and Yuan et al.\ built the Time Residual LSTM to address
degradation at longer horizons \cite{Yuan2023}. At longer horizons the evidence
is thinner. Ruma et al.\ showed that 15-day water-level forecasting is achievable
in Bangladesh (NSE of about 0.95) \cite{Ruma2023}, Islam et al.\ evaluated
discharge at six horizons out to 30 days and found LSTM strongest at 15 and 30
days \cite{Islam2026a}, and Li et al.\ reached $R^2 = 0.9922$ at 15 days
\cite{Li2025}. None evaluates one architecture across a range as wide as 3 hours
to 15 days, which is the design adopted here.

\section{Heterogeneity in Monitoring Stations}
\label{sec:2.9}

Station behavior differs, sometimes considerably, even within one river system.
Islam et al.\ trained a discharge model at Mymensingh and tested it at nearby
Islampur without retraining; performance held up reasonably but varied by
location and horizon \cite{Islam2026a}. A related study found that transferring
models from Islampur to Sarishabari depended on the input scenario and the target
station \cite{Islam2026b}. Even close stations in one sub-basin are therefore
different enough for performance to shift between them, which is essentially the
non-IID problem described in the federated learning literature
\cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}.

\section{Federated Learning}
\label{sec:2.10}

Federated learning lets distributed parties train a model together without
handing over raw data, which suits hydrological and meteorological records that
are held separately by different stations or offices, as they typically are in
Bangladesh.

\subsection{Conventional FL}
\label{sec:2.10.1}

McMahan et al.'s Federated Averaging (FedAvg) is where this line begins: clients
run several rounds of local training and a server averages their parameters into
a shared model. On image and language tasks this cut the communication rounds
needed to reach a target accuracy by roughly one to two orders of magnitude
compared with federated SGD \cite{McMahan2017}. Train locally and share only
parameters, never the data: this is the mechanism the thesis borrows. FedAvg was,
however, built and tested on generic benchmarks with a single shared model, and
it says nothing about hydrology or about clients that differ strongly, the
problem taken up in Section~\ref{sec:2.10.3}.

\subsection{FL for Time-Series}
\label{sec:2.10.2}

Federated learning for hydrological time series is still rare, but there is a
direct precedent. Amanzholova et al.\ built a federated LSTM for next-day
discharge at two stations in the Chu--Talas basin. It came within about 1\% of
the centralized model at the more homogeneous station (NSE 0.948 against 0.956)
and clearly beat both alternatives at the more divergent one (NSE 0.903 against
0.528 centralized and $-4.230$ local-only) \cite{Amanzholova2026}. On
non-hydrological time-series benchmarks, Chen et al.\ show that standard
federated learning struggles once client data become heterogeneous
\cite{Chen2025}. The precedent is encouraging but limited to two stations, one
variable, a one-day horizon and no clustering. This thesis tests whether the
approach holds with 25 stations, five variables, seven horizons and an explicit
clustering step.

\subsection{Non-IID Data}
\label{sec:2.10.3}

Client data are rarely as similar as the theory would like. Non-IID data can
cause slower convergence, lower accuracy and divergence under standard FedAvg,
and data-based and algorithmic remedies have been surveyed across horizontal and
vertical federated learning \cite{Zhu2021}; Chen et al.\ show the same for time
series \cite{Chen2025}. This maps directly onto the station heterogeneity of
Section~\ref{sec:2.9}. Neither offers a hydrology-specific fix, which leaves the
mitigation of clustering clients before training to the next section.

\section{Clustered Federated Learning}
\label{sec:2.11}

Clustered Federated Learning handles non-IID data by grouping similar clients
and training one model per group instead of one shared model. Ghosh et al.'s
Iterative Federated Clustering Algorithm (IFCA) repeatedly estimates each
client's cluster and updates that cluster's model, beating both global and
purely local training on heterogeneous benchmarks, for example 95.25\% against
89.73\% and 80.05\% on a four-cluster Rotated MNIST setup \cite{Ghosh2021}.
SR-FCA removes the need to know the number of clusters in advance and
outperformed Local, Global and IFCA baselines \cite{Harshvardhan2022}, and a
recent survey organizes the field into server-side, client-side and
metadata-based clustering, noting that choosing the number of clusters remains
open \cite{BenAli2026}. All three rely on image or text benchmarks with
artificially constructed heterogeneity, which leaves open what a cluster should
mean when the clients are monitoring stations. This thesis defines its four
clusters (Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream and Urban) from hydrological domain
knowledge, which none of these algorithms was tested against.

\section{Explainable AI}
\label{sec:2.12}

As forecasting models grow more accurate, explaining their predictions matters
more, particularly for early warning. A review of 83 studies applying explainable
AI to runoff and water-level forecasting found SHAP to be the dominant post-hoc
method \cite{Khorrami2025}.

\subsection{SHAP}
\label{sec:2.12.1}

SHAP, introduced by Lundberg and Lee, attributes a model's output to its input
features and is the only attribution satisfying local accuracy, missingness and
consistency; it was also more sample-efficient than LIME and Shapley sampling in
their experiments \cite{Lundberg2017}. Being model-agnostic, it applies equally
well to hydrology. Huang et al.\ used SHAP with a coupled CNN-LSTM for the
Beijiang River Basin (NSE 0.838 at 25 hours) and found that recent downstream
runoff dominated the predictions \cite{Huang2024}. Hemel and Sakib used SHAP and
LIME on a Bangladesh flood classifier and identified rainfall as a strong
predictor \cite{HemelSakib2024}, and Parasar et al.\ showed that SHAP can reveal
how the influence of rainfall and antecedent discharge shifts under monsoon
conditions \cite{Parasar2025}. None of these sits inside a clustered or federated
model. The analysis in Section~\ref{sec:3.17} applies SHAP to the cluster-specific
models of this thesis.

\section{Visualization and Decision-Support Systems}
\label{sec:2.13}

This is the thinnest part of the literature collected here. A Seq2Seq LSTM
surrogate was built explicitly for real-time operational use \cite{Lim2025}.
Among Bangladesh-focused studies, Hemel and Sakib name real-time updating as a
future direction \cite{HemelSakib2024}, and Ruma et al.\ and Islam et al.\ point
to richer inputs as future work \cite{Ruma2023,Islam2026a}, but none delivers a
decision-support tool. This thesis addresses that gap with an interactive
web-based interface (Section~\ref{sec:web_interface}) that presents station-level forecasts
alongside water-level information published by the Flood Forecasting and Warning
Centre (FFWC), used as an external point of comparison and not as validated
ground truth.

\section{Critical Comparison of Existing Studies}
\label{sec:2.14}

Stepping back, the reviewed literature falls into four groups that have had
little to do with one another. Bangladesh-specific studies use a single data
modality at a time, from a few stations to large pooled networks, but always in
a centralized setting
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024,Hossain2025}.
Deep-learning architecture research establishes strong methods for multi-step
forecasting, but almost entirely outside Bangladesh and in a centralized setup
\cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023,Ni2024,Kumar2023,Hameed2025,Waqas2024,Aghenda2025,Li2024,Lin2021,Li2025}.
Federated and clustered federated learning research shows that FedAvg and
cluster-specific training handle client heterogeneity reasonably well, but only
on generic benchmarks or a two-station hydrological case
\cite{McMahan2017,Zhu2021,Chen2025,Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026,Amanzholova2026}.
And explainable-AI research has made SHAP the standard interpretability method
for hydrological deep learning, without yet placing it inside a clustered or
federated model \cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024,Khorrami2025,Parasar2025}.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:2.15}

No study identified in this review brings these four strands together:
multivariate inputs, a multi-station and multi-cluster network, a wide range of
horizons, federated and cluster-aware training, and SHAP-based interpretation,
compared against an external reference (FFWC) through an accessible
visualization platform. More specifically:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item No Bangladesh-focused study combines discharge, water level, rainfall,
    temperature and evaporation in one forecasting framework
    (Section~\ref{sec:2.3}).
    \item No reviewed study tests one architecture across a horizon range as wide
    as 3~hours to 15~days (Section~\ref{sec:2.8}).
    \item No reviewed study applies clustered federated learning, as distinct from
    a single global federated model or a centralized cross-station transfer
    setup, to a hydrological forecasting network
    (Sections~\ref{sec:2.9}--\ref{sec:2.11}).
    \item No reviewed study applies SHAP within a clustered or federated
    hydrological model (Section~\ref{sec:2.12}).
    \item Dedicated visualization or decision-support tooling for Bangladesh
    water-level forecasting, compared against an external reference such as FFWC,
    is largely absent (Section~\ref{sec:2.13}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:2.16}

This chapter reviewed flood and water-level forecasting, the data and methods
behind it, multi-horizon forecasting and station heterogeneity, and the case for
federated, clustered federated and explainable approaches. Bangladesh-specific
forecasting, deep-learning architectures, federated learning and explainable AI
have largely developed apart, leaving open a multivariate, multi-cluster,
multi-horizon framework that is both federated and interpretable. Chapter~3
describes the CFL-EDLSTM framework built to address that gap, from data
preparation to the web interface.
