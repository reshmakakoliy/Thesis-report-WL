\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{Ch_Chapter2}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:2.1}

This chapter reviews the literature on which the proposed Clustered Federated
Learning Encoder--Decoder LSTM (CFL-EDLSTM) framework is built, moving from the
general to the specific. Sections~\ref{sec:2.2} and~\ref{sec:2.3} introduce
flood and water-level forecasting and the data it relies on.
Sections~\ref{sec:2.4}--\ref{sec:2.7} then review statistical,
machine-learning, deep-learning and tree-based approaches.
Sections~\ref{sec:2.8} and~\ref{sec:2.9} examine multi-horizon forecasting and
the heterogeneity of monitoring stations, two issues that lead directly to
federated and clustered federated learning in Sections~\ref{sec:2.10}
and~\ref{sec:2.11}. Sections~\ref{sec:2.12} and~\ref{sec:2.13} cover
explainable AI and visualization tools. The chapter closes with a critical
comparison of the reviewed studies (Section~\ref{sec:2.14}), the research gap
(Section~\ref{sec:2.15}) and a summary (Section~\ref{sec:2.16}).

Figure~\ref{fig:lit-evolution} outlines how water-level forecasting approaches
have developed. The progression runs from traditional statistical and physical
models, through machine learning and deep learning, to multi-horizon
forecasting and federated learning, and ends with clustered federated
learning, the approach adopted in this thesis. Each stage addresses a
limitation of the one before it, and the sections of this chapter follow
broadly the same order.

\section{Flood and Water-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.2}

Flood and water-level forecasting is the prediction of future river
conditions, such as water level, discharge or flood occurrence, from
historical hydrological and meteorological records, with early warning as its
main purpose. A review of 363 flood-prediction studies published between 2018
and 2025 found that machine learning and deep learning now dominate the field
and that no single architecture performs best across all studies
\cite{Aghenda2025}. A related review adds that data quality, interpretability
and computational cost remain recurring challenges \cite{Kumar2023}. Bangladesh
has developed its own body of work, drawing mostly on records from the
Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB) and the Bangladesh Meteorological
Department (BMD)
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024}.
These studies show that data-driven forecasting is feasible in the country,
although they differ considerably in the inputs they use, as the next section
explains.

\section{Hydrological and Meteorological Data}
\label{sec:2.3}

The choice of input data is one of the clearest differences across this
literature, and it is part of the motivation for this thesis. Some studies use
a single variable. Ruma et al.\ forecast water level from water-level records
alone \cite{Ruma2023}, and Islam et al.\ forecast discharge from discharge
alone at two Old Brahmaputra stations, acknowledging the omission of
precipitation as a limitation \cite{Islam2026a}. Other studies combine
variables: water level with rainfall and temperature \cite{Islam2026b},
discharge with areal rainfall and evaporation \cite{Cui2022}, or a reduced set
of 16 meteorological features \cite{Rajab2023}. A third group relies on
meteorology alone and predicts flood occurrence from BMD records
\cite{Rakin2023,HemelSakib2024}. No Bangladeshi study reviewed here combines
discharge, water level, rainfall, temperature and evaporation in a single
framework, a point taken up again in Section~\ref{sec:2.15}.

\section{Traditional Statistical Approaches}
\label{sec:2.4}

Before machine learning became widespread, flood forecasting relied mainly on
physically based hydrological models and classical regression. Few of the
studies reviewed here are purely statistical, but two retain regression in
their comparisons. Rajab et al.\ found Random Forest ($R^2 = 0.768$) and
Polynomial Regression ($R^2 = 0.764$) among the stronger models
\cite{Rajab2023}, and Islam et al.\ retained Linear Regression alongside
tree-based models and LSTM \cite{Islam2026b}. Regression therefore remains a
simple and interpretable baseline, but it is generally matched or outperformed
by tree ensembles and deep learning once the relationships become nonlinear
and time-dependent, as they are in hydrology.

\section{Machine Learning-Based Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.5}

Ensemble and kernel-based methods are widely used in flood-related
forecasting. For flood occurrence in Bangladesh, Rakin et al.\ compared ten
classifiers on 65 years of BMD data and found LightGBM (97.76\%) just ahead of
Random Forest (97.71\%) \cite{Rakin2023}, while Hemel and Sakib found Gradient
Boosting the strongest, at 95.38\% \cite{HemelSakib2024}. For continuous
targets, Islam et al.\ found LSTM and BiLSTM ahead of Random Forest, LightGBM
and Support Vector Regression for discharge, although the other models were
not far behind \cite{Islam2026a}, and Hossain et al.\ compared six models,
including XGBoost, for monthly extreme water level \cite{Hossain2025}. The
clearest result comes from Hameed et al., in which XGBoost outperformed both
LSTM and Random Forest for monthly runoff in a glacierized catchment
($R^2 = 0.904$, NSE $= 0.797$) \cite{Hameed2025}. Gradient-boosted trees are
therefore a competitive method in their own right rather than merely a
fallback.

\section{Deep Learning for Water-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.6}

Most recent research activity is in deep learning. In one review, LSTM was the
most common architecture, accounting for 21\% of implementations
\cite{Aghenda2025}, and another singles out LSTM and GRU for their ability to
capture temporal dependencies, with hybrids such as ConvLSTM adding spatial
structure \cite{Kumar2023}. The appeal of these models lies in their gating:
ordinary RNNs suffer from vanishing and exploding gradients, which the gates
of an LSTM are designed to avoid \cite{Waqas2024}. The following subsections
review LSTM, GRU, the encoder--decoder LSTM and TCN in turn.

\subsection{LSTM}
\label{sec:2.6.1}

Ruma et al.\ provide a direct example from Bangladesh: a
particle-swarm-optimized LSTM forecasting water level across Ganges,
Brahmaputra and Meghna stations reached an NSE of about 0.95 at a 15-day
horizon \cite{Ruma2023}. A review of many LSTM variants concludes that LSTM
generally outperforms plain RNNs and GRUs in hydrology, although sensitivity to
hyperparameters and weak generalization across settings remain problems
\cite{Waqas2024}. This is consistent with the leading position of LSTM in the
wider literature \cite{Aghenda2025}. None of these studies, however, trains an
LSTM in a federated or clustered setting.

\subsection{GRU}
\label{sec:2.6.2}

The GRU simplifies the gating of the LSTM while still modeling temporal
dependencies. Ni et al.\ combined it with a TCN and kernel-density error
estimation and reported a 13\% reduction in RMSE relative to standalone LSTM,
GRU and TCN models \cite{Ni2024}, and Li et al.\ included it among several
baselines for semi-arid streamflow \cite{Li2024}. The GRU thus appears to be a
lighter alternative to the LSTM rather than a clear improvement on it. For this
reason, it is used in this thesis as a baseline (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}) and
not as a candidate for the core architecture.

\subsection{Encoder--Decoder LSTM}
\label{sec:2.6.3}

The encoder--decoder LSTM treats multi-step forecasting as a
sequence-to-sequence problem: an encoder compresses past observations into a
fixed representation, and a decoder unrolls that representation into future
predictions. Kao et al.\ applied this design to inflow forecasting at the
Shihmen Reservoir in Taiwan, one to six hours ahead, using hourly data from 23
typhoon events. Their LSTM-ED outperformed a feed-forward encoder--decoder by
about 3\% in RMSE at one hour and by about 38\% at six hours, so its advantage
grew with the horizon \cite{Kao2020}. Cui et al.\ fed an exogenous
process-model forecast into the decoder for horizons of 3 to 12 hours in two
Chinese basins; their model degraded least as the horizon lengthened, reaching
NSE values of 0.81 and 0.93 at 12 hours \cite{Cui2022}. More recently, a
Seq2Seq LSTM surrogate outperformed a standard LSTM in real-time multi-step
flood forecasting \cite{Lim2025}, and a Time Residual LSTM was proposed to
address the degradation at longer lead times observed in the baseline of Kao
et al.\ \cite{Kao2020,Yuan2023}.

Across these studies, encoder--decoder designs hold up better at longer
horizons than the models they are compared with. Where horizons are reported,
however, they stop at 12 hours, and all four studies use centralized training.
This thesis extends the approach to a 15-day horizon and to 25 stations
grouped into four clusters.

\subsection{TCN}
\label{sec:2.6.4}

The Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) replaces recurrence with dilated
causal convolutions. Lin et al.\ found the TCN competitive with the LSTM for
flood forecasting at multiple lead times, although it lost ground at the
longer ones \cite{Lin2021}. An Attention-TCN reduced MAE by 6.9--25\% relative
to a standard LSTM for semi-arid streamflow \cite{Li2024}, and a GRU--TCN hybrid
outperformed a standalone TCN \cite{Ni2024}. At longer horizons, combining
Empirical Mode Decomposition with TCN and GRU achieved $R^2 = 0.9922$ and
MAPE $= 3.25\%$ for 15-day streamflow, though at considerably greater
complexity \cite{Li2025}. The TCN is therefore a credible non-recurrent
alternative to the LSTM and is included as a baseline in this thesis
(Section~\ref{sec:3.13}), although it has not yet been tested in a
multi-station or federated setting.

\section{Tree-Based Models}
\label{sec:2.7}

Gradient-boosted trees differ from both the regression methods of
Section~\ref{sec:2.4} and the neural architectures of Section~\ref{sec:2.6},
and, as Section~\ref{sec:2.5} showed, they perform well in hydrological
forecasting.

\subsection{XGBoost}
\label{sec:2.7.1}

XGBoost appears repeatedly in the reviewed literature, both as a main model
and as a comparison baseline. The study of Hameed et al.\
(Section~\ref{sec:2.5}) gives the strongest evidence of it matching or
outperforming the LSTM \cite{Hameed2025}. In Bangladesh, it is among the models
compared at Islampur and Sarishabari \cite{Hossain2025,Islam2026b} and among
the classifiers of Rakin et al.\ and of Hemel and Sakib, who report an accuracy
of 94.47\% for it \cite{Rakin2023,HemelSakib2024}. Each of these evaluations is
limited to a single station or a single-site classification task. In this
thesis, XGBoost is evaluated across all four clusters and seven horizons
(Section~\ref{sec:3.13}).

\section{Multi-Horizon Forecasting}
\label{sec:2.8}

Forecast accuracy generally declines as the horizon lengthens, and several
studies document this directly. The advantage of the encoder--decoder of Kao
et al.\ widened with the horizon \cite{Kao2020}, the exogenous-input design of
Cui et al.\ degraded least up to 12 hours \cite{Cui2022}, and Yuan et al.\
developed the Time Residual LSTM specifically to address degradation at longer
horizons \cite{Yuan2023}. Evidence at longer horizons is thinner. Ruma et al.\
showed that 15-day water-level forecasting is achievable in Bangladesh, with an
NSE of about 0.95 \cite{Ruma2023}. Islam et al.\ evaluated discharge at six
horizons up to 30 days and found the LSTM strongest at 15 and 30 days
\cite{Islam2026a}, and Li et al.\ reached $R^2 = 0.9922$ at 15 days
\cite{Li2025}. None of these studies, however, evaluates a single architecture
across a range as wide as 3 hours to 15 days, which is the design adopted in
this thesis.

\section{Heterogeneity in Monitoring Stations}
\label{sec:2.9}

Station behavior differs, sometimes considerably, even within a single river
system. Islam et al.\ trained a discharge model at Mymensingh and tested it at
nearby Islampur without retraining; performance held up reasonably well but
varied with location and horizon \cite{Islam2026a}. A related study found that
the success of transferring models from Islampur to Sarishabari depended on
the input scenario and on the target station \cite{Islam2026b}. Even nearby
stations in the same sub-basin are therefore different enough for model
performance to shift between them. In the terminology of federated learning,
this is essentially the non-IID problem \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}, which the next
two sections address.

\section{Federated Learning}
\label{sec:2.10}

Federated learning allows distributed parties to train a model jointly without
sharing their raw data. This suits hydrological and meteorological records
that are held separately by different stations or agencies, as they typically
are in Bangladesh.

\subsection{Conventional Federated Learning}
\label{sec:2.10.1}

The Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm of McMahan et al.\ is the starting
point of this line of work. Clients perform several rounds of local training,
and a server averages their parameters into a shared model. On image and
language tasks, this reduced the number of communication rounds needed to reach
a target accuracy by roughly one to two orders of magnitude compared with
federated SGD \cite{McMahan2017}. The principle of training locally and sharing
only parameters, never the data, is the mechanism this thesis adopts. FedAvg
was, however, designed and tested on generic benchmarks with a single shared
model. It does not address hydrology or clients whose data differ strongly, a
problem discussed in Section~\ref{sec:2.10.3}.

\subsection{Federated Learning for Time Series}
\label{sec:2.10.2}

Federated learning for hydrological time series is still rare, but there is a
direct precedent. Amanzholova et al.\ built a federated LSTM for next-day
discharge at two stations in the Chu--Talas basin. At the more homogeneous
station, it came within about 1\% of the centralized model (NSE 0.948 against
0.956), and at the more divergent station it clearly outperformed both
alternatives (NSE 0.903 against 0.528 for the centralized model and $-4.230$
for the local-only model) \cite{Amanzholova2026}. On non-hydrological
time-series benchmarks, Chen et al.\ show that standard federated learning
struggles once client data become heterogeneous \cite{Chen2025}. The
hydrological precedent is encouraging but limited to two stations, one
variable, a one-day horizon and no clustering. This thesis tests whether the
approach holds with 25 stations, five variables, seven horizons and an explicit
clustering step.

\subsection{Non-IID Data}
\label{sec:2.10.3}

In practice, client data are rarely as similar as the theory assumes. Non-IID
data can cause slower convergence, lower accuracy and divergence under standard
FedAvg, and both data-based and algorithmic remedies have been surveyed across
horizontal and vertical federated learning \cite{Zhu2021}. Chen et al.\ report
the same effect for time series \cite{Chen2025}. This maps directly onto the
station heterogeneity described in Section~\ref{sec:2.9}. Neither study,
however, offers a remedy specific to hydrology. One such remedy, clustering
clients before training, is the subject of the next section.

\section{Clustered Federated Learning}
\label{sec:2.11}

Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) handles non-IID data by grouping similar
clients and training one model per group instead of a single shared model. The
Iterative Federated Clustering Algorithm (IFCA) of Ghosh et al.\ repeatedly
estimates the cluster of each client and updates the model of that cluster. It
outperformed both global and purely local training on heterogeneous
benchmarks, reaching, for example, 95.25\% accuracy against 89.73\% and
80.05\% on a four-cluster Rotated MNIST setup \cite{Ghosh2021}. SR-FCA removes
the need to know the number of clusters in advance and outperformed Local,
Global and IFCA baselines \cite{Harshvardhan2022}. A recent survey organizes the
field into server-side, client-side and metadata-based clustering and notes
that choosing the number of clusters remains an open problem
\cite{BenAli2026}.

All three studies rely on image or text benchmarks with artificially
constructed heterogeneity, which leaves open what a cluster should represent
when the clients are monitoring stations. This thesis defines its four
clusters (Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream and Urban) from hydrological domain
knowledge, a setting against which none of these algorithms has been tested.

\section{Explainable AI}
\label{sec:2.12}

As forecasting models become more accurate, explaining their predictions
becomes more important, particularly in early warning. A review of 83 studies
applying explainable AI to runoff and water-level forecasting found SHAP to be
the dominant post-hoc method \cite{Khorrami2025}.

\subsection{SHAP}
\label{sec:2.12.1}

SHAP, introduced by Lundberg and Lee, attributes a model's output to its input
features. It is the only attribution method that satisfies local accuracy,
missingness and consistency, and in their experiments it was more
sample-efficient than LIME and Shapley sampling \cite{Lundberg2017}. Because it
is model-agnostic, it applies equally well to hydrological models. Huang et
al.\ used SHAP with a coupled CNN--LSTM for the Beijiang River Basin (NSE 0.838
at 25 hours) and found that recent downstream runoff dominated the predictions
\cite{Huang2024}. Hemel and Sakib applied SHAP and LIME to a flood classifier
for Bangladesh and identified rainfall as a strong predictor
\cite{HemelSakib2024}, and Parasar et al.\ showed that SHAP can reveal how the
influence of rainfall and antecedent discharge shifts under monsoon conditions
\cite{Parasar2025}. None of these applications, however, involves a clustered
or federated model. The analysis in Section~\ref{sec:3.17} applies SHAP to the
cluster-specific models of CFL-EDLSTM.

\section{Visualization and Decision-Support Systems}
\label{sec:2.13}

This is the least developed area of the reviewed literature. A Seq2Seq LSTM
surrogate was built explicitly for real-time operational use \cite{Lim2025}.
Among studies focused on Bangladesh, Hemel and Sakib identify real-time
updating as a future direction \cite{HemelSakib2024}, and Ruma et al.\ and
Islam et al.\ point to richer inputs as future work
\cite{Ruma2023,Islam2026a}, but none of them delivers a decision-support tool.
This thesis addresses that gap with an interactive web-based interface
(Section~\ref{sec:web_interface}) that presents station-level forecasts
alongside the operational forecasts of the Flood Forecasting and Warning
Centre (FFWC), used as an external reference.

\section{Critical Comparison of Existing Studies}
\label{sec:2.14}

Taken together, the reviewed literature falls into four research strands that
have developed largely in isolation from one another.
Table~\ref{tab:lit-comparison} compares these strands by data modality, scale
and architecture, and key limitation.

The first strand, Bangladesh-specific forecasting, uses one or a few data
modalities at a time and ranges from a few stations to large pooled networks, but it is always
trained in a centralized setting
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024,Hossain2025}.
The second strand, deep-learning architecture research, has established strong
methods for multi-step forecasting, but almost entirely outside Bangladesh and
with centralized training
\cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023,Ni2024,Kumar2023,Hameed2025,Waqas2024,Aghenda2025,Li2024,Lin2021,Li2025}.
The third strand, federated and clustered federated learning, shows that FedAvg
and cluster-specific training handle client heterogeneity reasonably well, but
only on generic benchmarks or in a two-station hydrological case
\cite{McMahan2017,Zhu2021,Chen2025,Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026,Amanzholova2026}.
The fourth strand, explainable AI, has made SHAP the standard interpretability
method for hydrological deep learning, but has not yet applied it within a
clustered or federated model
\cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024,Khorrami2025,Parasar2025}.

As the last column of Table~\ref{tab:lit-comparison} shows, the limitation of
each strand is precisely what another strand provides. This observation forms
the basis of the research gap in Section~\ref{sec:2.15}.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:2.15}

No study identified in this review brings the four strands together. None
combines multivariate inputs, a multi-station and multi-cluster network, a wide
range of forecast horizons, federated and cluster-aware training, and
SHAP-based interpretation, together with a comparison against an external
reference (FFWC) through an accessible visualization platform. More
specifically:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item No Bangladesh-focused study combines discharge, water level,
    rainfall, temperature and evaporation in a single forecasting framework
    (Section~\ref{sec:2.3}).
    \item No reviewed study tests a single architecture across a horizon range
    as wide as 3~hours to 15~days (Section~\ref{sec:2.8}).
    \item No reviewed study applies clustered federated learning, as distinct
    from a single global federated model or centralized cross-station
    transfer, to a hydrological forecasting network
    (Sections~\ref{sec:2.9}--\ref{sec:2.11}).
    \item No reviewed study applies SHAP within a clustered or federated
    hydrological model (Section~\ref{sec:2.12}).
    \item Dedicated visualization or decision-support tools for water-level
    forecasting in Bangladesh, compared against an external reference such as
    FFWC, are largely absent (Section~\ref{sec:2.13}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:2.16}

This chapter reviewed flood and water-level forecasting, its data and methods, multi-horizon forecasting, station heterogeneity, and federated, clustered federated and explainable approaches. These strands have largely developed separately, so no existing framework is at once multivariate, multi-cluster, multi-horizon, federated and interpretable. Chapter 3 presents CFL-EDLSTM, developed to fill this gap.
