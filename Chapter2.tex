\section{Introduction}

This chapter works through the literature behind the proposed Clustered Federated Learning Encoder--Decoder LSTM (CFL-EDLSTM) framework, moving from the broad to the specific. It starts with flood and water-level forecasting and the data typically used for it, then covers traditional, machine-learning, and deep-learning approaches, including the specific architectures (LSTM, GRU, Encoder--Decoder LSTM, TCN) and tree-based models (XGBoost) that matter for this thesis. From there it moves to multi-horizon forecasting and station heterogeneity, which is where the argument for federated and clustered learning begins, before turning to explainable AI and visualization. It closes with a critical comparison of the reviewed studies and a statement of the gap this thesis addresses.

\section{Flood and Water-Level Forecasting}

Flood and water-level forecasting means predicting future river or reservoir conditions --- water level, discharge, or the occurrence of a flood --- from historical hydrological and meteorological records, with early warning as the end goal. A systematic review of 363 flood-prediction studies published between 2018 and 2025 found that machine learning and deep learning now dominate the field, and that no single architecture wins across every study \cite{Aghenda2025}. A related review of deep-learning applications reached much the same conclusion, adding that data quality, interpretability, and computational cost remain recurring difficulties \cite{Kumar2023}. Bangladesh has its own substantial body of work, drawing mostly on records from the Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB) and the Bangladesh Meteorological Department (BMD) \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024}. It shows that data-driven forecasting is feasible here, though the studies differ considerably in what they use as input.

\section{Hydrological and Meteorological Data}

Which data a study uses is one of the more telling differences across this literature, and a large part of the motivation for this thesis. Some studies work from a single variable: Ruma et al.\ forecast water level from water-level records alone across a network of Bangladeshi stations \cite{Ruma2023}, and Islam et al.\ forecast discharge from discharge alone at two Old Brahmaputra stations, acknowledging that leaving out precipitation is a real limitation \cite{Islam2026a}. Others combine variables: Islam et al.\ pair daily water level with rainfall and temperature using 1--5 day lag features \cite{Islam2026b}, Cui et al.\ combine discharge with areal rainfall and evaporation \cite{Cui2022}, and Rajab et al.\ trim 18 meteorological features to 16 for rainfall forecasting \cite{Rajab2023}. A third group uses meteorology only, predicting flood occurrence from BMD weather records with no discharge or water-level data \cite{Rakin2023,HemelSakib2024}. Taken together, no Bangladeshi study reviewed here combines discharge, water level, rainfall, temperature, and evaporation in one framework, a point Section 2.15 returns to.

\section{Traditional Statistical Approaches}

Before machine learning, flood forecasting relied mostly on physically based hydrological models and classical statistical regression. Little of the literature collected here is purely statistical, but two studies keep regression in their comparisons. Rajab et al.\ tested Multiple Linear and Polynomial Regression alongside many machine-learning and deep-learning models, and found Random Forest ($R^2 = 0.768$) and Polynomial Regression ($R^2 = 0.764$) among the stronger performers \cite{Rajab2023}; Islam et al.\ kept Linear Regression alongside Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and LSTM for daily water level \cite{Islam2026b}. Regression therefore remains useful as a simple, interpretable baseline, but it is generally matched or bettered by tree-based ensembles and deep learning once the relationships become nonlinear and time-dependent, as they are in hydrology.

\section{Machine Learning-Based Forecasting}

Ensemble and kernel-based methods have been applied widely to flood-related forecasting. For Bangladesh flood occurrence, Rakin et al.\ compared ten classifiers on 65 years of BMD data, with LightGBM (97.76\%) just ahead of Random Forest (97.71\%) \cite{Rakin2023}, while Hemel and Sakib found Gradient Boosting strongest at 95.38\% among five models \cite{HemelSakib2024}. For continuous targets, Islam et al.\ found LSTM and BiLSTM ahead of Random Forest, LightGBM, and Support Vector Regression on composite discharge performance, though the tree-based and kernel-based models were not far behind \cite{Islam2026a}, and Hossain et al.\ compared six models, including XGBoost, for monthly extreme water level at Islampur \cite{Hossain2025}. The clearest single result is Hameed et al.'s, where XGBoost beat both LSTM and Random Forest for monthly runoff in a glacierized catchment ($R^2 = 0.904$, NSE $= 0.797$) \cite{Hameed2025}, a reminder that gradient-boosted trees are a genuinely competitive method and not merely a fallback.

\section{Deep Learning for Water-Level Forecasting}

Deep learning is where most recent activity has gone. In Aghenda et al.'s review, LSTM was the most common architecture, making up 21\% of implementations \cite{Aghenda2025}, and Kumar et al.\ single out LSTM and GRU for capturing temporal dependencies, with hybrids such as ConvLSTM adding spatial structure \cite{Kumar2023}. Waqas and Humphries explain why: ordinary RNNs suffer vanishing and exploding gradients that limit long-range learning, which LSTM's gating is built to avoid \cite{Waqas2024}. The subsections below look at LSTM, GRU, Encoder--Decoder LSTM, and TCN in turn.

\subsection{LSTM}

Ruma et al.\ give a direct Bangladeshi example: a particle-swarm-optimized LSTM (PSO-LSTM) forecasting water level across Ganges, Brahmaputra, and Meghna stations reached an NSE of about 0.957 at 15 days \cite{Ruma2023}. Waqas and Humphries' review of many LSTM variants (bidirectional, stacked, attention-based, and hybrids) concludes that LSTM generally beats plain RNNs and GRUs in hydrology, though hyperparameter sensitivity and weak generalization across settings remain problems \cite{Waqas2024}, consistent with LSTM's lead in the wider flood literature \cite{Aghenda2025}. None of these studies trains LSTM in a federated or clustered setting, which is the space this thesis's federated components are meant to fill.

\subsection{GRU}

GRU simplifies LSTM's gating while still handling temporal dependencies. Ni et al.\ paired it with TCN and kernel-density error estimation and reported a 13\% RMSE reduction over standalone LSTM, GRU, and TCN \cite{Ni2024}; Li et al.\ included it among several baselines (RNN, LSTM, TCN, Attention-TCN) for semi-arid streamflow \cite{Li2024}; and Waqas and Humphries note LSTM--GRU hybrids elsewhere in the literature \cite{Waqas2024}. GRU thus looks like a lighter alternative to LSTM and not a clear improvement on it, and this thesis treats it accordingly, as a baseline (Section 3.13) rather than a candidate for the core architecture.

\subsection{Encoder--Decoder LSTM}

The Encoder--Decoder LSTM casts multi-step forecasting as a sequence-to-sequence problem: an encoder compresses past observations into a fixed representation and a decoder unrolls it into future predictions. Kao et al.\ applied this to flood forecasting in Taiwan's Shihmen Reservoir catchment, forecasting inflow one to six hours ahead from hourly data for 23 typhoon events; their LSTM-ED beat a feed-forward encoder--decoder by about 3\% in RMSE at one hour and about 38\% at six hours, so the benefit grew with the horizon \cite{Kao2020}. Cui et al.\ fed an exogenous, process-model forecast into the decoder (LSTM-EDE) across 3--12 hour horizons in two Chinese basins; it degraded least as the horizon lengthened, reaching NSE of 0.81 (Lushui) and 0.93 (Jianxi) at 12 hours \cite{Cui2022}. More recently, Lim et al.\ found a Seq2Seq LSTM surrogate outperformed a standard LSTM for real-time multi-step flood forecasting \cite{Lim2025}, and Yuan et al.\ proposed a Time Residual LSTM to address the degradation seen in Kao et al.'s baseline at longer lead times \cite{Kao2020,Yuan2023}. Across these studies, encoder--decoder designs hold up better at longer horizons than their comparators. Where horizons are reported, however, they stop at 12 hours, and all four studies are centralized; this thesis extends the idea to 15 days and across 25 stations grouped into four clusters.

\subsection{TCN}

The Temporal Convolutional Network uses dilated causal convolutions instead of recurrence. Lin et al.\ found TCN competitive with LSTM on NSE, RMSE, and bias for multi-lead-time flood forecasting, though it lost ground at longer lead times \cite{Lin2021}; Li et al.'s Attention-TCN cut MAE by 6.9--25\% relative to a standard LSTM on heterogeneous semi-arid streamflow \cite{Li2024}; and Ni et al.'s GRU-TCN hybrid outperformed a standalone TCN \cite{Ni2024}. At the long end, Li et al.\ combined Empirical Mode Decomposition with TCN and GRU to reach $R^2 = 0.9922$ and MAPE $= 3.25\%$ for 15-day streamflow, at considerably greater complexity \cite{Li2025}. TCN is thus a credible non-recurrent alternative to LSTM and earns its place as one of this thesis's baselines (Section 3.13), although, like the studies above, it has not been tested in a multi-station or federated setting.

\section{Tree-Based Models}

Gradient-boosted decision trees sit apart from both the regression methods of Section 2.4 and the neural architectures above, and, as Section 2.5 noted, hold up well in hydrological forecasting.

\subsection{XGBoost}

XGBoost appears repeatedly in this literature, both standalone and as a comparison baseline. Hameed et al.'s result (Section 2.5) is the strongest evidence of it matching or beating LSTM \cite{Hameed2025}. Within Bangladesh, it appears among the models compared by Hossain et al.\ at Islampur \cite{Hossain2025} and by Islam et al.\ at Islampur and Sarishabari \cite{Islam2026b}, and among the classifiers of Rakin et al.\ and Hemel and Sakib, where Hemel and Sakib report 94.47\% accuracy for it \cite{Rakin2023,HemelSakib2024}. Each of these evaluations is confined to a single station or a single-site classification problem, whereas this thesis runs XGBoost as a baseline across all four clusters and seven horizons (Section 3.13), a scale of comparison none of these studies attempt.

\section{Multi-Horizon Forecasting}

Forecast accuracy tends to fall as the horizon lengthens, and several studies document it directly. Kao et al.'s encoder--decoder advantage widened with the horizon \cite{Kao2020}, Cui et al.'s exogenous-input design degraded least up to 12 hours \cite{Cui2022}, and Yuan et al.\ built the Time Residual LSTM to address degradation at longer horizons \cite{Kao2020,Yuan2023}. At longer horizons the evidence is thinner: Ruma et al.\ showed that 15-day water-level forecasting is achievable in Bangladesh (NSE $\approx 0.957$) \cite{Ruma2023}, Islam et al.\ evaluated discharge at six horizons out to 30 days and found LSTM strongest at 15 and 30 days \cite{Islam2026a}, and Li et al.\ reached $R^2 = 0.9922$ at 15 days \cite{Li2025}. None of these evaluates one architecture across a range as wide as 3 hours to 15 days, which is the design adopted here.

\section{Heterogeneity in Monitoring Stations}

Station behaviour differs, sometimes considerably, even within one river system. Islam et al.\ trained a discharge model at Mymensingh and tested it at nearby Islampur without retraining; performance held up reasonably well but varied by location and horizon \cite{Islam2026a}. A related study transferred models from Islampur to Sarishabari and found that transferability depended on the input scenario and the target station \cite{Islam2026b}. Even geographically close stations in one sub-basin are thus different enough for performance to shift between them, which is essentially the non-IID problem that federated learning research describes in general terms \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}.

\section{Federated Learning}

Federated learning lets distributed parties train a model together without handing over raw data, a natural fit where hydrological and meteorological records are held separately by different stations or offices, as they typically are in Bangladesh.

\subsection{Conventional FL}

McMahan et al.'s Federated Averaging (FedAvg) is where this line begins: clients run several rounds of local training and a server averages their parameters into a shared model, which cut the communication rounds needed to reach a target accuracy by roughly one to two orders of magnitude relative to federated SGD on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and language tasks \cite{McMahan2017}. Train locally, share only parameters, never the data: this is the mechanism this thesis borrows. FedAvg itself, however, was built and tested on generic image and language benchmarks under a single shared model; it says nothing about hydrology or about clients that differ strongly, the problem taken up in Section 2.10.3.

\subsection{FL for Time-Series}

Federated learning for hydrological time series is still rare, but there is a direct precedent. Amanzholova et al.\ built a federated LSTM for next-day discharge at two stations in the Chu--Talas basin; it came within about 1\% of the centralized model at the more homogeneous station (NSE 0.948 against 0.956) and clearly beat both alternatives at the more divergent one (NSE 0.903 against 0.528 centralized and $-4.230$ local-only) \cite{Amanzholova2026}. Chen et al.\ show more generally, on non-hydrological time-series benchmarks, that standard federated learning struggles once client data become heterogeneous \cite{Chen2025}. The precedent is encouraging but limited to two stations, one variable, a one-day horizon, and no clustering; this thesis tests whether the approach holds at a larger scale, with 25 stations, five variables, seven horizons, and an explicit clustering step.

\subsection{Non-IID Data}

Client data are rarely as similar as the theory would like. Zhu et al.'s survey shows that non-IID data can cause slower convergence, lower accuracy, and divergence under standard FedAvg, and reviews data-based and algorithmic remedies across horizontal and vertical federated learning \cite{Zhu2021}; Chen et al.\ show the same for time series \cite{Chen2025}. This maps directly onto the station heterogeneity of Section 2.9 \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b}. Neither offers a hydrology-specific fix, which leaves the mitigation, clustering clients before training, to the next section.

\section{Clustered and Federated Learning}

Clustered Federated Learning handles non-IID data by grouping similar clients and training a model per group instead of one shared model. Ghosh et al.'s Iterative Federated Clustering Algorithm (IFCA) repeatedly estimates each client's cluster and updates that cluster's model, beating both global and purely local training on heterogeneous benchmarks, for example 95.25\% against 89.73\% and 80.05\% on a four-cluster Rotated MNIST setup \cite{Ghosh2021}. Harshvardhan et al.'s SR-FCA removes the need to know the number of clusters in advance and outperformed Local, Global, and IFCA baselines across several benchmarks \cite{Harshvardhan2022}. Ben Ali et al.\ organize the field into server-side, client-side, and metadata-based clustering, and note that cluster-quality metrics exist but that choosing the number of clusters remains an open problem \cite{BenAli2026}. All three rely on image or text benchmarks with artificially constructed heterogeneity, which leaves open what a cluster should mean when the clients are monitoring stations. This thesis defines its four clusters (Upstream, Middle, Downstream, Urban) from hydrological domain knowledge, which none of these algorithms were tested against.

\section{Explainable AI}

As forecasting models grow more accurate, explaining their predictions matters more, particularly for early warning. Khorrami et al.\ reviewed 83 studies applying explainable AI to runoff and water-level forecasting and found SHAP to be the dominant post-hoc method \cite{Khorrami2025}.

\subsection{SHAP}

SHAP, introduced by Lundberg and Lee, attributes a model's output to its input features and is the only attribution satisfying local accuracy, missingness, and consistency; it was also more sample-efficient than LIME and Shapley sampling in their experiments \cite{Lundberg2017}. Though not built for hydrology, being model-agnostic it applies equally well there. Huang et al.\ used SHAP (via a Gradient Explainer) with a coupled CNN-LSTM for the Beijiang River Basin (NSE 0.838 at 25 hours) and found that recent downstream runoff dominated the predictions \cite{Huang2024}; Hemel and Sakib used SHAP and LIME on a Bangladesh flood classifier and identified rainfall as a strong predictor \cite{HemelSakib2024}; and Parasar et al.\ showed that SHAP could reveal how rainfall and antecedent discharge shift in influence under monsoon conditions \cite{Parasar2025}. None of these sits inside a clustered or federated model or asks whether feature importance differs between parts of a river network, which is the question this thesis puts to SHAP in Section 3.17 by computing it separately for each of the four clusters.

\section{Visualization and Decision-Support Systems}

This is the thinnest part of the literature collected here. Lim et al.'s Seq2Seq LSTM surrogate was built explicitly for real-time operational use \cite{Lim2025}. Among the Bangladesh-focused studies, Hemel and Sakib name real-time hydrological data and real-time updating as future directions \cite{HemelSakib2024}, while Ruma et al.\ and Islam et al.\ point to richer inputs, such as meteorological variables, as future work \cite{Ruma2023,Islam2026a}; none delivers a decision-support tool. This thesis addresses that gap by building an interactive web-based visualization interface (Chapter 3) that presents station-level forecasts alongside water-level information published by the FFWC, used as an external point of comparison and not as validated ground truth.

\section{Critical Comparison of Existing Studies}

Stepping back, the reviewed literature falls into four groups that have had little to do with one another. Bangladesh-specific forecasting studies use a single data modality at a time, from a handful of stations to large pooled networks, but always in a centralized setting \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024,Hossain2025}. Deep-learning architecture research establishes strong methods for multi-step forecasting, but almost entirely outside Bangladesh and in a centralized setup \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023,Ni2024,Kumar2023,Hameed2025,Waqas2024,Aghenda2025,Li2024,Lin2021,Li2025}. Federated and clustered federated learning research shows that FedAvg and cluster-specific training handle client heterogeneity reasonably well, but only on generic benchmarks or a two-station hydrological case \cite{McMahan2017,Zhu2021,Chen2025,Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026,Amanzholova2026}. And explainable-AI research has made SHAP the standard interpretability method for hydrological deep learning, without yet placing it inside a clustered or federated model \cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024,Khorrami2025,Parasar2025}.

\section{Research Gap}

No study identified in this review brings all four of these strands together: multivariate inputs (discharge, water level, rainfall, temperature, evaporation), a multi-station, multi-cluster network (25 stations across four hydrologically defined groups), a wide range of horizons (3 hours to 15 days), federated and cluster-aware training, and SHAP-based interpretation, compared against an external reference (FFWC) through an accessible visualization platform. More specifically:

\begin{itemize}
    \item No Bangladesh-focused study combines all five hydrometeorological variables used in this thesis within a single forecasting framework (Section 2.3).
    \item No reviewed study tests one architecture across a horizon range as wide as 3 hours to 15 days (Section 2.8).
    \item No reviewed study applies clustered federated learning --- as distinct from a single global federated model, or a purely centralized cross-station transfer-learning setup --- to a hydrological forecasting network (Sections 2.9--2.11).
    \item No reviewed study applies SHAP within a clustered or federated hydrological model, or compares feature importance across hydrologically distinct station clusters (Section 2.12).
    \item Dedicated visualization or decision-support tooling for Bangladesh water-level forecasting, compared against an external reference such as the FFWC, is largely absent from the literature identified in this review (Section 2.13).
\end{itemize}

Chapter 3 sets out the CFL-EDLSTM framework built to address this gap.

\section{Summary}

This chapter moved from flood and water-level forecasting and the data it uses, through traditional, machine-learning, and deep-learning approaches, to multi-horizon forecasting and station heterogeneity, which together motivate federated and clustered federated learning, and finally to explainable AI. A critical comparison showed that Bangladesh-specific forecasting, deep-learning architectures, federated and clustered learning, and explainable AI have developed largely in isolation from one another, leaving open a multivariate, multi-cluster, multi-horizon, federated, and interpretable forecasting framework for Bangladesh, which Chapter 3 sets out to build.
