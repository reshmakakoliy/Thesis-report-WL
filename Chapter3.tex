\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:3.1}

This chapter sets out how the CFL-EDLSTM framework introduced in Chapter~1 and motivated by the gaps identified in Chapter~2 was actually built. It starts with the overall research design and a high-level picture of the proposed framework, then narrows into the concrete details: the study area, the 25 monitoring stations, where the data came from, what the five variables actually look like, how the raw records were cleaned and aligned, how the stations were grouped into clusters, and how the time-series inputs and forecasting horizons were set up. The architecture itself, the baseline models, the evaluation design, the SHAP procedure, and the visualization platform are covered in the sections that follow this one.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:3.2}

This is a quantitative, experimental study built around a comparative design: one proposed framework (CFL-EDLSTM) evaluated against four baseline architectures, across four station clusters and seven forecasting horizons, using historical observational data rather than data generated for the purpose of the study. The work is applied rather than purely theoretical --- the goal is a forecasting system that could plausibly be used, not just a proof of concept --- which is also why a web-based visualization platform and an external comparison against FFWC are part of the design rather than an afterthought.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Overall Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:3.3}

At its core, the framework combines two ideas that Chapter~2 examined separately: a sequence-to-sequence Encoder--Decoder LSTM for multi-step forecasting \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022}, and a clustered federated training procedure in which stations are grouped by hydrological similarity and each group acts as a federated client, trained and combined through Federated Averaging \cite{McMahan2017,Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022}. Rather than pooling every station's data into one central model, each of the four clusters trains on its own stations' data, and only model parameters --- never raw windows --- are shared and aggregated across clusters. The shared model that results is then adapted locally, so each cluster forecasts with a model that reflects its own hydrological character; Section~\ref{sec:3.12} sets out the details.The overall workflow of the proposed framework is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:3.1}.

\section{Study Area}
\label{sec:3.4}

The study covers 25 water-level monitoring stations distributed across the
northern, central, western and Dhaka-metropolitan parts of Bangladesh. Together
they span a network of 13 named river systems.
Figure~\ref{fig:3.2.a} shows how these stations are arranged along

station, and the dashed grey line marks the Padma and Meghna, which are shown
only for context because no station from this study lies on them.

Reading the figure from north to south, the Teesta and Dharla in the north
flow toward the Brahmaputra near Chilmari. The Brahmaputra continues as the
Jamuna, which is the river with the most stations in the study (four of its
own, and six including the two upstream Brahmaputra stations). To the west, the
Karatoa system, which includes the Jamuneswari and the Deonai--Charalkata
channels, runs south and is joined by the Ghagot. The Atrai is linked to this
system through the Karatoa--Atrai--Gur--Gumani--Hurasagar network and reaches
the Jamuna near Baghabari. East of the Jamuna, the Old Brahmaputra branches off
toward Mymensingh and feeds the Lakhya. Further downstream, the Dhaleswari and
the Kaliganga--Bethua carry water toward the Dhaka region, where the Buriganga,
Turag, Lakhya and Balu complete the network around the capital.

The Buriganga, Turag, Lakhya and Balu are also where the tidal stations are
found. The Bangladesh Water Development Board (BWDB) classifies each station as
tidal or non-tidal. Of the 25 stations, 5 are tidal, all in the Urban cluster
on these four channels, and the remaining 20 are non-tidal. We report this
distinction as background about the study area, not as an input to the
clustering procedure, which is defined separately in Section~\ref{sec:3.10.1}.

\section{Dataset Description}
\label{sec:3.7}

Across the five variables, the dataset totals 1,092,687 raw observations before any cleaning or alignment: 762,813 for water level, 46,812 for discharge, 71,110 for rainfall, 40,761 for evaporation, and 171,191 for temperature. Water level's much larger share reflects both its wider station coverage and its finer native resolution (5 observations/day, against daily for discharge, rainfall, temperature, and evaporation).

Coverage across variables is genuinely uneven rather than a uniform grid. Of the 13 districts represented anywhere in the dataset, only four --- Bogura, Dhaka, Rangpur, and Sirajganj --- have all five variables present. Most districts have three or four, and one (Narayanganj) have only water level and one other variable. This unevenness is exactly why station clustering (\S\ref{sec:3.10}) and careful preprocessing (\S\ref{sec:3.9}) matter as much as they do here --- a model can't assume every station looks like every other station going in.

\subsection{Water Level}
\label{sec:3.8.1}

Water level is the target variable throughout this thesis. It is recorded at 25 BWDB gauge stations five times a day --- 06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00, and 18:00, three hours apart during the day with a twelve-hour gap overnight --- the finest native resolution of any variable in the dataset, and it is retained at that native resolution as the study's master modelling timeline (\S\ref{sec:3.9.3}), rather than being collapsed down to a single daily value. Measured in metres above mean sea level (mMSL), it is the quantity every forecast horizon in this thesis ultimately predicts, following the same target-variable choice made by \cite{Ruma2023} and, for a smaller station set, \cite{Islam2026b}.

\subsection{Discharge}
\label{sec:3.8.2}

Discharge (mean daily discharge, MDD) is recorded at 15 of the 25 stations, in cubic metres per second, and used both directly and through derived trend features. Discharge is the input variable \cite{Islam2026a} relied on exclusively for their Old Brahmaputra forecasting work --- and one of the two limitations they themselves flagged was the exclusion of exactly the kind of meteorological variables (rainfall, temperature) this thesis adds alongside it.

\subsection{Rainfall}
\label{sec:3.8.3}

Rainfall is recorded daily at 13 stations, in millimetres, and used both directly and as cumulative windows to capture delayed catchment response. This mirrors the lag-based and cumulative-rainfall treatment used by \cite{Islam2026b} and the areal-rainfall approach of \cite{Cui2022}, both of which found rainfall to carry real, if secondary, predictive weight relative to antecedent discharge or water level.

\subsection{Temperature}
\label{sec:3.8.4}

Temperature is the one variable in this dataset that is not station-specific: it comes from a combination of a Mendeley dataset and NASA POWER reanalysis data, at daily resolution, covering 11 districts, with gaps filled via interpolation and values mapped onto stations by district. Because temperature is district-level rather than station-level, it still carries less direct/observational weight than the station-specific variables, and any gap-filling interpolation should be treated with the same caution as elsewhere in the dataset --- worth being explicit about in any later discussion of SHAP-based feature importance (\S3.16 / Chapter~4), since a variable mapped from district to station shouldn't be interpreted quite the same way as one measured directly at the station.

\subsection{Evaporation}
\label{sec:3.8.5}

Evaporation is recorded daily at 7 stations --- the narrowest coverage of any variable --- and, like temperature, is linearly interpolated to daily values where gaps exist. Its inclusion follows the precedent of \cite{Cui2022}, who found that adding evaporation alongside rainfall improved multi-step forecasting accuracy in their Encoder--Decoder framework, even though its coverage in that study was similarly narrower than the other inputs.

\subsection{Data Cleaning}
\label{sec:3.9.1}

Before anything else, timestamps across all five sources are parsed into one consistent datetime format, station identifiers are standardized against the naming inconsistencies already flagged in \S\ref{sec:3.5} (Bogra/Bogura, Shirajgonj/Sirajganj, and similar), and every record is sorted by station and time. Water-level readings are mapped to the nearest of the five target slots --- 06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00, or 18:00 --- by closest value alone, with no separate tolerance threshold applied. Where more than one reading lands in the same station-slot, they're combined by taking the mean. Duplicate records, impossible numeric values, inconsistent units, and likely transcription errors are all checked for --- but extreme water-level values are inspected rather than dropped automatically, since an extreme reading during this kind of study is just as likely to be a real flood as it is to be an error.

Before any of the four supporting variables get merged in, the station metadata itself is checked. Rainfall, temperature, and evaporation are assigned to the 25 water-level stations primarily by district match --- whatever district a station is recorded under is used to find the corresponding rainfall/temperature/evaporation source for that same district --- and where no matching source exists in that station's own district, the nearest available station geographically is used instead. Discharge (MDD) is mapped directly to its corresponding water-level station wherever a measurement exists. Which source gauge fed which station is kept on record throughout, rather than discarded once the merge is done.

\subsection{Handling Missing Values}
\label{sec:3.9.2}

Missing water-level readings aren't treated as random noise. A large share of them are almost certainly tied to flooding itself, since gauges can fail or go unread precisely when water levels spike --- which means the gaps that matter most are exactly the ones a careless imputation method would handle worst. Where a gap coincides with a known event, it's checked against BWDB flood reports and the surrounding water-level trajectory at that station, and only reconstructed where both the report and the neighboring readings genuinely support a specific value --- with reconstructed points flagged separately from directly observed ones from that point on. Where the evidence isn't strong enough to reconstruct with any confidence, the gap stays marked as missing rather than being papered over.

As a result, MDD values at the ten monitoring stations without discharge sensors are represented as zero throughout the series. Missing values in rainfall, temperature, and evaporation are processed using the same sequence of steps.

\subsection{Temporal Alignment}
\label{sec:3.9.3}

Water level defines the modelling timeline here, at its native five-observations-a-day schedule (06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00, 18:00), rather than being collapsed down to a single daily value. The four supporting variables --- rainfall, temperature, evaporation, and discharge --- are all daily, so each one is mapped to its corresponding station and matched by date across all five of that date's water-level slots; a given day's rainfall total, for instance, appears identically in all five rows for that date. That repetition is there to align the feature matrix, not to imply five independent rainfall measurements were actually taken.

The definition and reporting window of each daily variable are checked before use. A daily total that includes measurements occurring after a WL prediction time must not be treated as already known at that time. Inputs must reflect only information available at the forecast origin. Where suitable, daily lags are generated by shifting the original daily series by calendar days before joining it to WL, rather than shifting rows in the expanded five-observation dataset.

Daytime observations are approximately three hours apart, but the interval from 18:00 to the following 06:00 is twelve hours. The series is therefore not uniformly sampled every three hours. Preprocessing preserves the five-slot daily structure or represents elapsed time explicitly where supported by the model. A 15-day horizon corresponds to 75 WL observations; for example, a 30-day input window contains 150 WL observations followed by a 75-observation target.Figure~\ref{fig:3.5} illustrates how the daily and sub-daily series are brought onto one timeline. Water level is observed five times a day, whereas discharge, rainfall, temperature and evaporation have one value per day, which is repeated across the five water-level slots of that date.

\subsection{Normalization}
\label{sec:3.9.4}

% Every continuous variable is scaled before it reaches the model, since stable optimization depends on it. Water level, discharge, rainfall, temperature, and evaporation are all scaled separately, since none of them share units or ranges, and the exact method --- standardization or min--max, consistent with the approaches used by \cite{Ruma2023} and \cite{Rakin2023} --- will be chosen once the actual data distributions are in front of us rather than picked in advance. Whether water level needs station-wise scaling is still an open question too; if local baselines differ enough between stations, scaling per station may be worth it, but that decision has to weigh its effect on later cross-station and cross-cluster comparisons, not just on model convergence.

% Whatever method is chosen, its parameters are fit on the training data only, then applied unchanged to validation and test. Missingness indicators and station identifiers are left alone rather than scaled as if they were ordinary continuous measurements. Every fitted scaler is saved, predictions are inverse-transformed back into physical units before they're reported, and reconstructed water-level values keep their flag all the way through, so a reconstructed point is never mistaken for a directly observed one downstream.
Every continuous variable is scaled before it reaches a model, since stable optimization depends on it, and the method used throughout this thesis is standardization (z-score scaling) rather than min--max or any other transform. Water level, discharge, rainfall, temperature, and evaporation are each scaled separately, since none of them share units or ranges, and a single shared scale across all five would let the variable with the largest raw magnitude dominate the loss regardless of its actual predictive importance.

Exactly how the scaler is fit differs slightly by model, in line with each model's own data representation described in Section~\ref{sec:3.13}. For Vanilla LSTM and TCN, which treat each station as an independent sequence, a single StandardScaler for the inputs and a separate one for the target are fit on the concatenated training portions of all 25 stations together, so no individual station's scale comes to dominate the shared scaler. GRU, which trains jointly across all stations, instead fits its own input and multi-station target scalers on the chronological training portion of that joint representation. XGBoost is the one exception: as a tree-based model, it has no need for feature scaling at all, and its engineered inputs are used directly in their prepared numeric form without a StandardScaler applied.

Whatever the grouping, every scaler's parameters are fit on training data only, then applied unchanged to validation and test, so that no information from those held-out periods leaks into the input representation before a model ever sees it. Missingness indicators and station identifiers are left alone rather than scaled as if they were ordinary continuous measurements. Every fitted scaler is saved, predictions are inverse-transformed back into physical units before they are reported, and reconstructed water-level values keep their flag all the way through, so a reconstructed point is never mistaken for a directly observed one downstream.


\subsection{Data Splitting}
\label{sec:3.9.5}

The data is split chronologically into training, validation, and test sets using a 70:15:15 proportion, respectively --- a random split would scatter temporally adjacent, and even future, observations across training and test sets, which defeats the point of a forecasting evaluation. Rather than committing to a single split up front, several chronological configurations are being tried, with the boundary between training, validation, and test moved and re-evaluated to see which split actually yields the most reliable performance, consistent with the chronological (not random) approach used by \cite{Cui2022}. Each candidate boundary is chosen with the same considerations in mind --- what dates are actually available, how station coverage changes across the period, and how flood events are distributed across the timeline --- and whichever configuration is used, it's documented rather than left implicit.

Input and target windows are always built in temporal order, with every target strictly following its input, and the model, scalers, and any imputation parameters are fit using training data alone regardless of which split is being tested. Validation is reserved for model selection, and the test period only ever gets used for a final evaluation. Across all of this, the 15-day horizon (75 water-level observations) is the evaluation point, and errors are reported back in the original water-level units rather than left in scaled form.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:3.9.6}

Beyond the five raw variables, a set of derived features is built from the cleaned, aligned dataset to make the delayed and cumulative nature of hydrological response more directly visible to the model than the raw series alone would.Figure~\ref{fig:3.9.6a} provides an overview of the feature engineering process, with each step explained in the following subsections.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[
        width=0.85\textwidth
    ]{figures2/feature-eng.png}

    \caption{Feature Engineering Workflow}
    \label{fig:3.9.6a}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Rainfall accumulation.}
To capture how antecedent rainfall builds up before it shows in the river, rainfall is accumulated over look-back windows of 3, 7, 14, and 30 days. For a rainfall series $R$, the accumulated rainfall over the $k$ preceding days is:

\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{acc},k}(t) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} R(t - i),
  \qquad k \in \{3,\, 7,\, 14,\, 30\}
  \label{eq:3.1}
\end{equation}

which captures both short, sharp rainfall events and longer wet spells, using only rainfall that occurred strictly before the forecast origin.

\paragraph{Lag features.}
Rainfall, discharge, temperature, and evaporation are each given lagged versions at offsets of roughly 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, and 14 days, depending on what the data actually supports, and water-level lags are built from its own preceding observations or matching daily slots. These daily-variable lags are computed on the original daily series first, before that series gets expanded across the five water-level slots --- doing it the other way around would treat five copies of the same value as five independent lags and quietly produce the wrong interval.

\paragraph{Discharge-derived features.}
Recent discharge conditions are represented using rolling mean values over 3-, 7-, 14-, and 30-day windows. These features help the model capture changes in river flow over different time scales. Missing MDD values are handled during preprocessing through interpolation, forward filling, backward filling, and zero-filling for any values that remain missing, as described in \S\ref{sec:3.9.2}. Consequently, stations without discharge sensors have zero-valued MDD series, and their discharge-derived rolling means are also zero.

\paragraph{Temporal features.}
Calendar and observation-time information is folded in as well --- which of the five daily slots an observation falls in, day of year, and related calendar variables --- with cyclical quantities like time-of-day and day-of-year encoded via sine/cosine transforms. The elapsed time between consecutive observations is also carried through explicitly, given the schedule's own 12-hour overnight gap against the roughly 3-hour daytime gaps established in \S\ref{sec:3.9.3}.

\paragraph{Leakage prevention.}
All of the above is computed strictly chronologically, using only information that would genuinely have been available at each forecast origin. The reporting window of every daily variable is checked specifically for this. The final feature set is decided based on data availability and validation performance, not fixed in advance. The forecasting task itself targets 15 days ahead, or 75 water-level observations under the five-observations-per-day schedule.

\section{Proposed CFL-EDLSTM Framework}
\label{sec:3.12}

Everything described so far in this chapter feeds into one forecasting framework, which this section sets out in full. In short, CFL-EDLSTM trains a single encoder--decoder LSTM architecture across the four station clusters defined in Section~\ref{sec:3.10}. Each cluster acts as one federated client that learns from the data of its own stations; a server then combines what the four clients have learned into a shared global model using weighted Federated Averaging \cite{McMahan2017}, and every cluster keeps its own locally adapted version of the model for producing forecasts. The clusters are fixed in advance from hydrological knowledge, rather than discovered during training as in iterative clustering schemes such as IFCA \cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022}, so clustering decides \emph{who the clients are}, while federation decides \emph{how they learn together}.

The reasoning behind this design follows directly from the problem set out in Chapter~2. Stations on the same river system can behave quite differently from one another (Section~2.9), and training one model on all of them together risks averaging that difference away, while training a separate model per station gives up whatever the stations could teach each other. Federated learning offers a way to learn jointly without pooling raw data \cite{McMahan2017}, but plain federated averaging struggles when client data are strongly non-IID \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. Grouping stations into hydrologically coherent clients, and letting each client keep a locally adapted model, is this thesis's response to that tension. A federated LSTM has been applied to hydrological forecasting before \cite{Amanzholova2026}, but with two stations, a single variable, and a one-day horizon; here the same idea is applied to four clients, five variables expanded into 42 input features, and horizons out to 15 days. Table~\ref{tab:3.8} collects the configuration used throughout.

\subsection{Encoder--Decoder Architecture}
\label{sec:3.12.1}

Forecasting 75 future water-level values from 150 past observations is a sequence-to-sequence problem, and the encoder--decoder pattern \cite{Sutskever2014,Cho2014} is built for exactly that: one network reads the input sequence and compresses it into a state, and a second network unrolls that state into the output sequence. The Vanilla LSTM baseline in Section~\ref{sec:3.13.1} does the same job with a single dense layer that emits all 75 values at once; the encoder--decoder instead generates them one step at a time, so each forecast value can condition on the one before it. In hydrology, an LSTM encoder--decoder has been shown to hold up better than a feed-forward encoder--decoder as the horizon grows \cite{Kao2020}, to accumulate less error than recursive decoding when the decoder is given suitable extra inputs \cite{Cui2022}, and to remain competitive in recent multi-step flood work \cite{Lim2025,Yuan2023}.Figure~\ref{fig:3.8} presents the encoder--decoder architecture used in CFL-EDLSTM. The encoder reads the 30-day input window and compresses it into a hidden state, which initialises the decoder. The decoder then generates the 75 forecast steps one after another, feeding each prediction back as the next input.

For a station $s$ and window start $t$, the encoder receives the previous 30 days of observations, 150 time steps at five observations per day, and the model forecasts the next 15 days, 75 steps:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{X}_{t,s} = \big[\mathbf{x}_{t,s},\, \mathbf{x}_{t+1,s},\, \dots,\, \mathbf{x}_{t+149,s}\big], \qquad
  \mathbf{Y}_{t,s} = \big[y_{t+150,s},\, y_{t+151,s},\, \dots,\, y_{t+224,s}\big],
  \label{eq:3.2}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_{t,s} \in \mathbb{R}^{42}$ is the feature vector and $y_{t,s}$ is water level (mMSL). Each feature vector holds the five base variables (water level, rainfall, MDD, evaporation, temperature); four rainfall accumulations over 3, 7, 14, and 30 days; 24 lagged values (six lags each for rainfall, MDD, temperature, and evaporation); four rolling means of MDD; and five time features (sine and cosine of hour of day and of day of year, plus the elapsed time since the previous observation). Water level is both the target and one of the inputs, so the encoder sees recent water-level history alongside everything else. How these features are built is described in Section~\ref{sec:3.9.6}.

The encoder is a two-layer LSTM \cite{HochreiterSchmidhuber1997} with 128 hidden units per layer and dropout of 0.2 \cite{Srivastava2014}. It reads the 150 input steps in order, updating a hidden state and a cell state at each step through the usual input, forget, and output gates:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{i}_j &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_i \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_i), &
  \mathbf{f}_j &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_f \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_f),\\
  \mathbf{o}_j &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_o \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_o), &
  \mathbf{g}_j &= \tanh(\mathbf{W}_g \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_g \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_g),\\
  \mathbf{c}_j &= \mathbf{f}_j \odot \mathbf{c}_{j-1} + \mathbf{i}_j \odot \mathbf{g}_j, &
  \mathbf{h}_j &= \mathbf{o}_j \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_j),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:3.3}
\end{equation}
written here for a single layer, with $\sigma$ the logistic sigmoid and $\odot$ the element-wise product; in the second layer, the first layer's hidden state takes the place of $\mathbf{x}_j$. The encoder's final hidden and cell states act as a compressed summary of the 30-day history and are handed directly to the decoder.

The decoder is a second two-layer LSTM, again with 128 hidden units, initialized from those final states:
\begin{equation}
  \big(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_0,\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{dec}}_0\big) = \big(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{enc}}_{150},\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{enc}}_{150}\big).
  \label{eq:3.4}
\end{equation}
It then generates the 75 forecast values one at a time. At step $q$ it takes the previous water-level value $u_q$ as input, updates its states, and a linear layer projects its hidden state to a single water-level value:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \big(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q,\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q\big) &= \mathrm{LSTM}_{\mathrm{dec}}\big(u_q,\, \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_{q-1},\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{dec}}_{q-1}\big),\\
  \hat{y}_q &= \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q + b_o, \qquad q = 1, \dots, 75.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:3.5}
\end{equation}
Where $u_q$ comes from depends on the mode. During training it can be the observed value, a practice known as teacher forcing \cite{WilliamsZipser1989}; at inference no observations of the future exist, so the decoder is fed its own previous prediction. The first step is seeded with the last observed water level in the input window.

Generating the sequence step by step has a known cost. Because inference feeds predictions back in, early errors can compound, and a decoder trained on observed values never practices recovering from its own mistakes, the error-accumulation and exposure-bias problem that \cite{Cui2022} describe for recursive decoding. The framework accepts this risk in exchange for a decoder that produces a coherent trajectory, and Chapter~4 reports error at each of the seven horizon checkpoints precisely so that any such degradation is visible rather than averaged away.

\subsection{Local Model Training}
\label{sec:3.12.2}

Each of the four clusters in Table~\ref{tab:3.5} is one federated client, $c_1$ through $c_4$, and holds every training window for the stations that belong to it. Before training, the cluster definitions are validated: no station may appear in two clusters, every station in the dataset must be assigned to one, and every station named in a cluster must exist in the data. The number of stations in cluster $k$, written $n_k = |S_k|$, later serves as that client's weight in aggregation (Section~\ref{sec:3.12.4}).

Before windows can be built, all stations are placed on a common time axis. Each station's date and time fields are combined into a timestamp, rows with invalid or duplicate timestamps are dropped, and the rest are sorted chronologically; the common axis is then the intersection of every station's timestamps, and only observations on that axis are kept. This gives every station the same temporal index and lets the data be arranged as a [time, station, feature] array with a matching [time, station] array of water-level targets. Any remaining gaps are filled by interpolation, then forward and backward filling, with a final zero fill where nothing else is possible, and the pipeline rejects any non-finite value that survives. The price of a shared axis is that a timestamp missing at even one station is lost for all of them, so CFL-EDLSTM is evaluated on fewer windows than the station-wise baselines of Section~\ref{sec:3.13}, and Chapter~4 reads the comparison with that in mind.

The common series is then split chronologically: the first 70\% is used for training, the next 15\% for validation, and the final 15\% for testing, and time is never shuffled. Windows are generated separately inside each split, so none crosses a split boundary; a window starting at $t$ is valid only if it fits entirely within its split, that is, $a \le t \le b - 150 - 75$ for a split spanning indices $a$ to $b$. Inputs are standardized feature by feature, and water level is standardized again with its own target scaler. Both scalers are fitted on the training portion only and applied unchanged to validation and test, and the target scaler is later used to convert predictions back into metres.

Each client trains against Huber loss on the standardized targets \cite{Huber1964},
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\delta(r) =
  \begin{cases}
    \tfrac{1}{2}\, r^{2}, & |r| \le \delta,\\[2pt]
    \delta\big(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\big), & |r| > \delta,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Huber}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \ell_\delta(\hat{y}_j - y_j), \quad \delta = 1,
  \label{eq:3.6}
\end{equation}
the same choice made for the neural baselines (Section~\ref{sec:3.14.3}). Optimization uses Adam \cite{Kingma2015} with a learning rate of 0.005 and a batch size of 64, with gradients clipped to a maximum norm of 1 to keep the recurrent updates stable \cite{Pascanu2013}. In every federated round, a client starts from the parameters it has just received from the server, trains on its own windows for three local epochs, and returns the result; over 30 rounds that amounts to 90 local epochs per client in total.

\subsection{Federated Model Updates}
\label{sec:3.12.3}

Training proceeds in 30 federated rounds, each following the same five steps (Figure~\ref{fig:3.9}). The server broadcasts the current global parameters to all four clients; each client trains locally for three epochs on its own windows; each client returns its updated parameters; the server combines them into the next global model (Section~\ref{sec:3.12.4}); and each client's locally trained model is evaluated on that cluster's validation windows. Only parameter tensors travel between clients and server. Training windows and station records never do, which is the property that makes federated averaging attractive for distributed monitoring networks \cite{McMahan2017}. The federation in this thesis is simulated on a single workstation (Section~\ref{sec:3.19}): the four clients are separate data partitions with separate local training loops, and the aggregation step is the only point at which they interact.

Only weighted Federated Averaging is used. Variants such as FedProx \cite{Li2020FedProx} and SCAFFOLD \cite{Karimireddy2020} add a proximal term or a control-variate correction to counter client drift when local data differ, but neither is applied here. Client drift is a genuine concern in this setting, since clients with hydrologically different data can pull the shared parameters in different directions during local training \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. Grouping similar stations into one client makes each client's data more internally consistent, though differences between clients remain, and those are what the aggregation weights and local adaptation described next have to handle.

\subsection{Cluster-Wise Aggregation}
\label{sec:3.12.4}

Aggregation happens at the level of clusters. With $K = 4$ clients, the server computes each client's weight from the number of stations in its cluster and takes a weighted average of every parameter tensor,
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_k = \frac{n_k}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} n_j}, \qquad
  \mathbf{w}^{(r+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k\, \mathbf{w}_k^{(r)}, \qquad
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k = 1,
  \label{eq:3.7}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}_k^{(r)}$ are client $k$'s parameters after local training in round $r$. A cluster therefore contributes to the next global model in proportion to how many stations it represents. Given the cluster sizes in Table~\ref{tab:3.5}, the Upstream, Middle, Downstream, and Urban clusters carry weights of $6/25$, $8/25$, $5/25$, and $6/25$, that is 0.24, 0.32, 0.20, and 0.24, which sum to one.

Weighting by station count rather than by raw window count might look like a departure from the original FedAvg rule, which weights each client by its number of local training examples \cite{McMahan2017}. In this design the two coincide in practice: because every station is aligned to the same time axis, each station produces the same number of windows, so a cluster's window count is simply its station count times a constant. Weighting by stations also states the intent plainly, that every station should count equally in the global model, whichever cluster it falls in.

It is worth being clear about what this step does and does not produce. The aggregated model is a single global model that has learned from all 25 stations. The cluster-specific models used for forecasting are not these aggregated parameters but the locally adapted states each cluster reaches after its own local training in a round (Section~\ref{sec:3.12.5}). Keeping both is a common way of balancing shared learning against local fit in federated settings \cite{Zhu2021}. It is also a deliberately simple reading of ``clustered'' federated learning: here the cluster structure defines the clients, whereas methods such as IFCA let the server assign clients to groups and maintain a separate model for each group \cite{Ghosh2021}.

\subsection{Model Redistribution}
\label{sec:3.12.5}

At the start of each round, the newly aggregated global parameters are sent back to all four clients and replace whatever parameters they held, so every round begins from the same shared starting point. A client's locally trained state from the end of the previous round is not simply thrown away, though. After local training, each cluster's model is evaluated on its own validation windows and kept as that cluster's personalized model, so each cluster can be assessed with the model that reflects its own data distribution.

After every round, a single weighted validation loss is calculated across the four clusters,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}^{(r)} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} m_k\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val},k}^{(r)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} m_k},
  \label{eq:3.8}
\end{equation}
where $m_k$ is the number of validation windows in cluster $k$; unlike the aggregation weights, these are based on window counts. Whenever this loss improves on its best previous value, a checkpoint is saved containing the aggregated global model, each cluster's personalized state, both scalers, the station and cluster definitions, the 42 feature names, the input and output lengths, and the architecture metadata. Selecting the checkpoint by validation loss, rather than taking the final round, guards against late-round overfitting in the same way as for the baselines (Section~\ref{sec:3.14.1}).

Once all 30 rounds are complete, the best checkpoint is loaded. For each cluster, a fresh encoder--decoder is created, that cluster's personalized state is loaded into it, and its test windows are forecast. All predictions and targets are inverse-transformed back into metres before any metric is calculated, and the mean absolute error and root mean squared error defined in Section~\ref{sec:3.16} are computed over the concatenated test predictions of all four clusters.

% One practical limitation should be stated plainly: no random seed was fixed for these runs, so repeating the training can produce slightly different results. The chronological split, training-only scalers, and independent windows per split are applied identically on every run, so any run-to-run variation comes from the training process itself (initialization, batch order, dropout) rather than from changes to the data pipeline.


\section{Baseline Models}
\label{sec:3.13}

Table~\ref{tab:3.6} gives an overview of the four baseline models. XGBoost (300 estimators, depth 6, learning rate 0.05) acts as a simple sanity check (Baseline~0), and the Vanilla LSTM is the standard recurrent reference (Baseline~1). The GRU, which has no separate cell state, is included to test the effect of the recurrent architecture, and the TCN, built from four dilated causal blocks, offers a convolutional alternative. The three neural baselines are trained with the Huber loss, whereas XGBoost uses squared error.

Four baseline models were trained and evaluated to give the proposed CFL-EDLSTM something concrete to be measured against. They are matched to it as closely as their data representations allow: the same task (an input history of 30 days, 150 observations at five observations per day, and a forecast horizon of 15 days, 75 observations); the same seven horizon checkpoints (3 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, and 15 days, corresponding to decoding steps 1, 2, 4, 5, 15, 35, and 75); the same 70/15/15 chronological split proportions, with no window crossing a split boundary; and scalers fitted on training data only, which keeps information from the validation and test periods from leaking into the input representation. Missing values are handled in the same spirit: interpolation followed by forward and backward filling, with a final fallback value where interpolation alone is not possible (a median or zero for the baselines, zero for CFL-EDLSTM).

The setups are not identical, however, and the difference matters for how Chapter~4 should be read. Vanilla LSTM, TCN, and XGBoost split each station's own record chronologically and independently, so every station contributes all of its available timestamps. GRU instead works on a joint representation aligned on the union of all stations' timestamps, and CFL-EDLSTM works on their intersection (Section~\ref{sec:3.12.2}), so that only timestamps present at every station survive. CFL-EDLSTM is therefore evaluated on fewer windows, and its validation and test periods are defined by the shared time axis rather than by each station's own record. Chapter~4 flags this wherever the models are compared.


\subsection{Rationale for Baseline Selection}
\label{sec:baseline_rationale}

The four baselines were chosen to answer a specific question: does the
clustered federated encoder--decoder improve on established alternatives that
differ from it in one clear respect? Each baseline therefore represents a
different modelling family. XGBoost is a strong non-deep-learning reference for
tabular lagged features. The Vanilla LSTM is the standard recurrent model and
isolates the effect of the encoder--decoder design, since it shares the same
recurrent core. The GRU tests whether a simpler gated cell is enough, and the
TCN offers a convolutional alternative that has no recurrence. All four are
also practical in this setting, because they are small enough to train per
cluster on roughly 16 years of data per station and their parameters can be
averaged in federated rounds.

Several other models were considered and set aside. A plain RNN cannot retain
the multi-day lag between upstream rainfall and downstream water level because
of vanishing gradients, and the LSTM was designed to remove exactly this
limitation. A bidirectional LSTM reads the sequence in both directions and
would therefore use future values that are unavailable at forecast time, which
makes its evaluation invalid for forecasting. CNN--LSTM models are suited to
gridded inputs such as radar or satellite rasters, whereas our data are point
series from gauge stations. A feed-forward network on lagged features performs
the same role as XGBoost with less interpretability. Transformer-based models,
including the Temporal Fusion Transformer, typically need much more data than
we have per station, and their attention matrices are costly to exchange in a
federated setting.

Graph-based models were examined most closely, because stations along a river
form a natural network. We experimented with a spatio-temporal graph neural
network (ST-GNN). We did not include it in the comparison, because lagged upstream features already carry much of the station-to-station relationship, and because a graph model would
need data to be shared across stations, which conflicts with our aim of keeping raw data within clusters. 

% Graph and attention-based models are therefore left for future work (Section~\ref{sec:future_work}).

\subsection{Vanilla LSTM}
\label{sec:3.13.1}

Vanilla LSTM is included as Baseline 1 --- the standard recurrent architecture every other model in this thesis is, implicitly, measured against. LSTM was introduced specifically to solve the vanishing-gradient problem that plain RNNs run into over long sequences, by giving the network a dedicated cell state gated by input, forget, and output gates \cite{HochreiterSchmidhuber1997}. That gating is what lets a rainfall signal recorded several days earlier still influence a prediction made today, which matters directly here: in the Brahmaputra basin, the chain from upstream rainfall to a downstream peak water level typically unfolds over five to ten days, and a model without some mechanism for carrying information that far forward would simply lose it.

Each of the 25 monitoring stations is treated as an independent temporal sequence for this baseline. Station files are combined into a single long-form table, sorted by station and timestamp, with duplicate station/timestamp rows removed. At each time step the model sees ten input features: water level, rainfall, MDD, evaporation, temperature, and five derived time features (hour\_sin, hour\_cos, doy\_sin, doy\_cos, delta\_hours). The target is the next 75 water-level observations for that same station. Gaps are interpolated independently per station, and the input and target scalers --- both StandardScaler --- are fitted on the concatenated training portions of every station together, so that no single station's scale ends up dominating the shared scaler.

The network itself is two stacked LSTM layers of 128 hidden units each, with dropout of 0.2, followed by a fully connected decoder that outputs all 75 forecast values at once. It trains with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of $1\times10^{-3}$, a batch size of 64, for 30 epochs, using Huber loss \cite{Huber1964} and gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 1.0. Whichever checkpoint achieves the lowest validation loss is the one retained --- final test predictions come from that checkpoint, not from whatever the model happened to look like at the last training epoch, since the two are not always the same thing.

\subsection{GRU}
\label{sec:3.13.2}

GRU is included as Baseline 2, and its role here is deliberately narrow: it exists to test whether LSTM's extra representational capacity --- specifically, its separate cell state --- is actually earning its keep on this task, or whether a simpler gating mechanism gets you most of the way there. GRU merges LSTM's input and forget gates into a single update gate and drops the separate cell state entirely, using the hidden state directly as both memory and output \cite{Cho2014}. That simplification costs roughly 25\% fewer parameters than an equivalently sized LSTM, which translates into faster training per epoch and a smaller weight tensor to communicate per federated round --- relevant both to the local-vs-LSTM comparison reported in Chapter~4 and to the communication-cost argument made in Section~3.12.

Unlike the station-wise LSTM, GRU is trained on all stations jointly here. Every station is aligned onto the union of their timestamps, and at each timestamp the model sees the water level and available auxiliary features for every station at once, plus the five global time features. It predicts, for every timestamp, the future water level of every station simultaneously --- a tensor shaped roughly [batch, 75, number of stations]. Missing timestamps and values are interpolated on this shared time index, and any auxiliary column unavailable for a given station is simply treated as missing before interpolation and fallback filling run. Standardization for both inputs and targets is fitted only on the chronological training portion.

Architecturally, GRU mirrors the LSTM baseline as closely as possible on purpose: two stacked GRU layers of 128 hidden units, dropout 0.2, a decoder producing 75 $\times$ (number of stations) values, and the identical training recipe --- Adam at $1\times10^{-3}$, batch size 64, 30 epochs, Huber loss \cite{Huber1964}, gradient clipping at 1.0, best-checkpoint selection by validation loss. Holding the optimizer, loss function, batch size, and epoch budget fixed across both models isn't an oversight; it's what turns the LSTM-vs-GRU comparison in Section~4.5 into a genuine architecture ablation rather than a comparison muddied by two different training recipes.

\subsection{XGBoost}
\label{sec:3.13.3}

XGBoost is included as Baseline 0: a non-deep-learning reference point that sets the minimum bar any recurrent or convolutional architecture in this thesis needs to clear before its added complexity is justified. XGBoost is an ensemble of decision trees trained sequentially, where each new tree is fit specifically to correct the residual errors left by the ones before it \cite{ChenGuestrin2016}. It has no built-in notion of sequence or time at all, which means every bit of temporal structure the model uses has to be handed to it explicitly, as engineered lag and rolling features, rather than learned implicitly the way a recurrent model would.

For every station, each training sample here carries 150 lagged water-level values (ordered oldest to newest), the current rainfall, MDD, evaporation, and temperature readings, the time features, and a one-hot station-identity indicator. The target is the next 75 water-level values. Since a single regressor can't natively produce a 75-step sequence, XGBoost is actually trained as 75 independent regressors, one per forecast step.

Each window's split assignment depends on where the full input-plus-output window falls within that station's chronological record, not just where it starts:

\begin{itemize}
  \item training windows are those that end within the first 70\% of the record
  \item validation windows end within the first 85\%
  \item test windows start only after that 85\% boundary
\end{itemize}

Any window that would straddle a split boundary is discarded outright rather than truncated to fit. No StandardScaler is applied here, since the tree-based model works directly on the prepared numeric values.

Each of the 75 regressors is an XGBRegressor with a squared-error objective, a maximum of 300 estimators, maximum depth 6, learning rate 0.05, row and column subsampling of 0.8 each, L1 regularization of 0.1, L2 regularization of 1.0, the histogram tree-construction method, and early stopping after 30 rounds without validation improvement.

\subsection{TCN}
\label{sec:3.13.4}

TCN --- a Temporal Convolutional Network --- is included as an additional sequence-modelling comparison alongside the two recurrent baselines, using dilated causal convolutions in place of recurrence to reach back across long time spans. It earns its place in the comparison on architectural merit and training speed rather than as the thesis's primary multi-step architecture: as discussed in Section~3.11, TCN's output head, like Vanilla LSTM's, still needs to be paired with a recursive or direct multi-output strategy to reach a 15-day horizon at all, and, unlike LSTM and GRU, it doesn't carry the same established presence in the flood-forecasting literature reviewed in Chapter~2 \cite{Ni2024,Lin2021,Li2024,Li2025} for an examiner to situate it against directly within the Bangladeshi context specifically. It is reported here, accordingly, as a secondary sequence baseline rather than promoted to Baseline 1 or 2 status.

TCN uses the same station-wise, ten-feature representation as Vanilla LSTM, producing a 75-step target for a single station at a time. Data are prepared independently per station, with input and target scalers fitted on the combined training sections of every station, and each split windowed independently so that, again, no window crosses a split boundary. The network itself is four residual temporal blocks with channel widths (64, 64, 128, 128), a kernel size of 3, dilation factors of 1, 2, 4, and 8, and dropout of 0.2, followed by a decoder producing all 75 forecast values. Training uses Adam at $1\times10^{-3}$, a batch size of 128 --- larger than either recurrent baseline, reflecting how much more readily convolution parallelizes than recurrence does --- for 30 epochs, Huber loss \cite{Huber1964}, gradient clipping at 1.0, and best-checkpoint selection by validation loss. Beyond the overall test metrics reported in Section~4.5, TCN's evaluation script also breaks results down by forecast horizon and by station.

\section{Hyperparameter Optimization}
\label{sec:3.14}

\subsection{Optimization Strategy}
\label{sec:3.14.1}

Across every neural model in this thesis --- Vanilla LSTM, GRU, TCN, and the proposed CFL-EDLSTM --- model selection during training is governed by validation loss, not training loss. A checkpoint is saved every time validation loss improves, and it's that best checkpoint, not whatever the model looked like at the final epoch, that generates the test predictions reported in Chapter~4, since the two frequently turn out to differ. This is the main lever used to keep overfitting in check during training, working alongside dropout (0.2 throughout) and gradient clipping (maximum norm 1.0) at the optimization level. XGBoost doesn't have an epoch loop to checkpoint in the same sense, so its equivalent safeguard is early stopping on the validation set after 30 rounds without improvement, combined with row and column subsampling (0.8 each) and L1/L2 regularization (0.1 and 1.0 respectively) to keep the depth-6 trees from simply memorizing the training split.

\subsection{Tuned Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:3.14.2}

Every model in this thesis was trained with a fixed, documented configuration rather than with a separate hyperparameter search. The values in Table~\ref{tab:3.7} are the ones used for every result reported in Chapter~4, and the only selection made on validation data is the choice of checkpoint (and, for XGBoost, the stopping point) described in Section~\ref{sec:3.14.1}. That keeps the comparison simple to describe and to reproduce, but it also means the results reflect these particular settings and not the best each architecture could reach with extensive tuning. A baseline that improved with more careful tuning would narrow its gap to the other models, so Chapter~4 should be read with that in mind.

Where the architectures allow it, the neural baselines share their settings. Vanilla LSTM and GRU use the same two layers of 128 hidden units, dropout of 0.2 \cite{Srivastava2014}, the Adam optimizer \cite{Kingma2015} at a learning rate of $1\times10^{-3}$, 30 epochs, a batch size of 64, and gradient clipping at a maximum norm of 1 \cite{Pascanu2013}. Holding all of this fixed is what lets the comparison between them isolate the architectural difference, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:3.13}. TCN follows the same optimizer, loss, dropout, and epoch budget, and differs mainly where its structure requires it: four residual blocks with channel widths (64, 64, 128, 128) in place of recurrent layers, and a larger batch size of 128, since convolution parallelizes more readily than recurrence.

XGBoost has its own set of controls, because it learns by adding trees and not by gradient descent on network weights. Each of its 75 regressors uses at most 300 trees of depth 6, with row and column subsampling of 0.8, L1 and L2 regularization of 0.1 and 1.0, and early stopping after 30 rounds without validation improvement \cite{ChenGuestrin2016}. Its learning rate of 0.05 is a shrinkage factor applied to each new tree, so it should not be compared directly with the learning rates of the neural models.

CFL-EDLSTM shares the baselines' hidden size, dropout, batch size, loss, optimizer, and gradient clipping, but its training schedule is organized differently because it is federated. Instead of a single run of 30 epochs, each of the four cluster clients trains for three local epochs in each of 30 federated rounds \cite{McMahan2017}, for 90 local epochs in total, and it uses a higher local learning rate of 0.005 and a two-layer decoder alongside its two-layer encoder (Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). These differences come with the federated setup and are not a tuned advantage, but they do mean the settings of CFL-EDLSTM and the baselines are comparable and not identical.

\subsection{Loss Function and Regularization}
\label{sec:3.14.3}

Every neural model in this thesis --- the three baselines and CFL-EDLSTM alike --- is optimized with Huber loss rather than plain mean squared error \cite{Huber1964}. Huber loss behaves quadratically for small residuals and switches to linear beyond a threshold, which is a useful property here specifically: it dampens the influence of extreme water-level spikes --- a brief sensor artifact, say, or a genuine flash-flood peak --- on the gradient, without simply discarding those points from training the way a pure MAE loss effectively would by under-weighting small errors relative to large ones. All final metrics reported in Chapter~4 are computed after inverse-transforming predictions back into physical water-level units (metres), so Huber loss itself --- which operates in the scaled, standardized training space --- never shows up directly as a reported evaluation number. Regularization otherwise comes from dropout (0.2) on every recurrent and convolutional model, and from the L1/L2 penalties and subsampling already described for XGBoost above.

\section{Multi-Horizon Forecasting Setup}
\label{sec:3.15}

Every model in this thesis is evaluated at the same set of forecast horizons, so that Chapter~4's cross-model comparison happens on genuinely identical terms rather than being an artifact of different evaluation choices. Given an input window of observations $[t-150, \ldots, t-1]$ --- 30 days at five observations per day --- and an output window of $[t, \ldots, t+74]$ --- 15 days at that same rate --- the reported horizon checkpoints are 3 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, and 15 days, corresponding respectively to decoding/prediction steps 1, 2, 4, 5, 15, 35, and 75. These labels are fixed, shared evaluation points used identically across every model in this thesis, and should be read that way throughout Chapter~4, rather than as an independently inferred sampling interval.

Results are reported under three consistent groupings: \textit{overall} (every station, window, and forecast step combined), \textit{station-wise} (all forecast steps for one station), and \textit{horizon-wise} (one forecast step, across all stations and test windows). Overall MAE and RMSE, both in metres, are treated as the primary comparison metrics in Chapter~4, since they remain directly comparable even across architectures that end up with different numbers of usable test windows --- a difference Section~4.4 addresses explicitly for the graph-based models.

\section{Model Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:3.16}

Seven metrics are reported for every model and every evaluation grouping: MAE, RMSE, MSE, NSE (Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency), R\textsuperscript{2} (coefficient of determination), EVS (explained variance score), and IOA (index of agreement). Every one of them is computed after inverse-transforming predictions back to the original water-level scale.

\subsection{Mean Absolute Error (MAE)}
\label{sec:3.16.1}

MAE is simply the mean of the absolute differences between predicted and observed water level, in metres. It's the most directly interpretable of the seven metrics for a hydrological audience specifically, because it expresses typical forecast error in the same physical unit the water-level readings themselves are already in --- and for that reason it serves as the headline comparison metric throughout Chapter~4.

\subsection{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)}
\label{sec:3.16.2}

RMSE is the square root of the mean squared difference between predicted and observed water level, also in metres. Because errors are squared before they're averaged, RMSE punishes large individual errors --- a badly missed flood peak, for instance --- more heavily than MAE does. It's reported alongside MAE rather than instead of it for exactly that reason: a model with a low MAE but a comparatively high RMSE is one whose errors are usually small but occasionally quite large, and that distinction matters a great deal for a flood-forecasting model specifically.

\subsection{Coefficient of Determination (R\texorpdfstring{\textsuperscript{2}}{2})}
\label{sec:3.16.3}

R\textsuperscript{2} expresses the share of variance in the observed water-level series that the model's predictions account for, with 1.0 indicating perfect explanatory power. NSE, reported alongside it, is the hydrology-specific analogue of R\textsuperscript{2} that dominates the flood-forecasting literature \cite{NashSutcliffe1970}. In the results produced for this thesis, NSE and R\textsuperscript{2} take the same value for every model and grouping without exception, so the two are reported together as a single combined NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} column in Chapter~4's result tables rather than as two separate ones.

\subsection{Additional Metrics}
\label{sec:3.16.4}

MSE (mean squared error, in square metres) is reported mainly as the intermediate quantity RMSE is derived from, and appears in the full result tables for completeness rather than as something discussed on its own. EVS (explained variance score) is closely related to R\textsuperscript{2} but, unlike it, doesn't penalize a constant bias in the predictions the same way --- so a model whose EVS sits noticeably above its R\textsuperscript{2} is one that tracks the shape of the true series well but carries a systematic offset. IOA (index of agreement) is bounded between 0 and 1 and tends to be less sensitive than NSE/R\textsuperscript{2} to a small number of unusually large errors, which is why, in Section~4.6, IOA and NSE occasionally rank models slightly differently at the longest, 15-day horizon.


\section{Explainability Using SHAP}
\label{sec:3.17}

Forecast accuracy tells us how well a model performs, but not what it has learned. To look inside the trained models, we applied SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), which assigns each input value a contribution to a prediction, measured against the model's output on a set of reference inputs. The analysis was designed to answer three questions: which input features drive the forecast most, how far back in the 30-day input window the model looks, and whether these patterns change between clusters and forecast horizons.

\subsection{Model and Data Explained}

The analysis was run on the proposed CFL-EDLSTM. Its four cluster-specific models, one per cluster, each use the 42 input features . Each cluster keeps its own personalised set of weights, and each of these four models was explained separately. Inputs were standardised with the scalers fitted on the training period, exactly as in training, and all explained windows were taken from the chronological test period (from 23 October 2023), so the explanations describe behaviour on data the models had not seen.

\subsection{Reference and Explanation Windows}

SHAP expresses a contribution relative to a baseline, so we first fixed a common reference. A background set of 32 windows was drawn at random from the training period across all 25 stations, and the same set was used for every cluster and horizon. For each cluster, 48 windows were then drawn at random from the test period, pooled across that cluster's stations. All sampling used a fixed seed (42), so the selected windows can be reproduced.

\subsection{Explaining One Forecast Step}

The model outputs 75 values, whereas SHAP explains one number at a time. We therefore explained the final step of four forecast days, day~1, day~3, day~7 and day~15, which correspond to the 1-, 3-, 7- and 15-day horizons evaluated in Chapter~4. The model was kept in evaluation mode so that dropout was off and the outputs were deterministic.

\subsection{Expected Gradients}
Attributions were computed with the gradient-based SHAP explainer, which
implements Expected Gradients, a combination of Integrated Gradients and SHAP.
For a model $f$, an explained input $x$ and a background distribution $D$, the
attribution of input element $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\phi_i(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x' \sim D,\; \alpha \sim U(0,1)}
\left[ (x_i - x'_i)\,
\frac{\partial f\bigl(x' + \alpha (x - x')\bigr)}{\partial x_i} \right].
\label{eq:expected_gradients}
\end{equation}
In words, a random background window $x'$ and a random point on the line
between $x'$ and $x$ are chosen, the gradient of the output is taken there, and
it is multiplied by the distance of $x$ from $x'$. Averaging over many draws
gives each element's contribution; we used 100 draws per explained window.
Because one window has $150 \times 42$ input elements, each window receives a
matrix of 6{,}300 SHAP values, one for every pair of timestep and feature. The
values approximately add up to the difference between the explained prediction
and the average prediction on the background.


\subsection{Excluding Water Level from the Reported Results}

Water level is an input to the model, so SHAP also produces an attribution for it. Past water level is by far the strongest predictor of future water level, and reporting it would hide the contribution of everything else. We therefore computed the attributions with water level present, and then removed its column before any averaging, ranking or plotting. The attributions of the remaining 41 features are unchanged by this step. They describe each feature's contribution while the model can also see water level, not what the feature would contribute to a model trained without it.

\subsection{Aggregation}
Let $\Phi$ be the array of SHAP values with $N$ windows, $T=150$ timesteps and
$F=41$ features. The importance of feature $f$ is its mean absolute SHAP value
over all windows and timesteps, and the importance of input timestep $t$ is
the mean absolute value over all windows and features:
\begin{equation}
I_f = \frac{1}{NT}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl|\Phi_{n,t,f}\bigr|,
\qquad
I_t = \frac{1}{NF}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{f=1}^{F}\bigl|\Phi_{n,t,f}\bigr|.
\label{eq:shap_importance}
\end{equation}
Absolute values are used because SHAP values carry a sign, and a feature that
raises the forecast in some windows and lowers it in others would otherwise
cancel out and look unimportant. To obtain an overall picture, the results of
all four clusters and four horizons were pooled, giving $4 \times 4 \times 48 =
768$ windows with equal weight. Because the model is trained on standardised
water level, SHAP values are in units of standard deviations of water level;
they can be converted to metres by multiplying by the training standard
deviation, and the rankings are the same in either unit.

\section{Experimental Procedure}
\label{sec:3.19}

Training and evaluation proceed in a fixed order so that each stage gives the next something to be compared against. 

The four baselines are trained first, each on its own data representation (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}). XGBoost serves as the sanity-check reference: if a sequence model cannot beat it, that points to a problem in preprocessing, not a need for more architectural complexity. Vanilla LSTM and GRU then provide the standard recurrent comparison and the LSTM-versus-GRU architecture ablation, with TCN as a secondary sequence comparison. CFL-EDLSTM is trained as described in Section~\ref{sec:3.12}: 30 federated rounds across the four cluster clients using weighted Federated Averaging \cite{McMahan2017}, with the checkpoint that has the lowest weighted validation loss retained and each cluster's locally adapted model used for its test forecasts.

Every model is evaluated on the final 15\% of its chronological data, at the same seven horizon checkpoints, using the metrics defined in Section~\ref{sec:3.16}. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:3.13}, CFL-EDLSTM's test windows come from the timestamps shared by all stations, so its comparison with the baselines carries the caveat described there. Training and evaluation were carried out on an AMD Ryzen 5 3600 six-core processor at 3.60~GHz, 24~GB of installed RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3060 with 12~GB VRAM; PyTorch models use CUDA where available, and XGBoost is configured for the same CUDA device.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:3.20}

This chapter has set out how the study was built, from the raw records to the models that will be judged against each other. The data come from 25 monitoring stations along the Brahmaputra river system and five variables --- water level, discharge, rainfall, temperature, and evaporation --- which were cleaned, aligned, and expanded into 42 input features (Section~\ref{sec:3.9}). The stations were grouped into four clusters, Upstream, Middle, Downstream, and Urban, using their position along the river and how they are grouped operationally by FFWC (Section~\ref{sec:3.10}). Each model reads the previous 30 days of observations and forecasts the next 15, with results reported at seven horizon checkpoints from 3 hours to 15 days (Sections~\ref{sec:3.11} and~\ref{sec:3.15}).

The proposed CFL-EDLSTM treats each cluster as a federated client. The clients train a shared encoder--decoder LSTM locally, a weighted Federated Averaging step combines them into a global model, and each cluster keeps its own locally adapted version for forecasting, so raw data are never pooled across clusters (Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). It is compared with four baselines --- XGBoost, Vanilla LSTM, GRU, and TCN (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}) --- under fixed, documented configurations and not a separate hyperparameter search (Section~\ref{sec:3.14}). All models are scored on the same seven metrics (Section~\ref{sec:3.16}), and SHAP and a web-based interface with an FFWC comparison view cover interpretation and presentation (Sections~3.17 and~3.18).

Two limits are worth carrying into the results. The federation is simulated on a single workstation, and CFL-EDLSTM works on the timestamps shared by all stations, so its test windows are fewer than those of the station-wise baselines. Chapter~4 reads the comparison with both in mind and reports what the procedure produced.
