\chapter{Methodology}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:3.1}

This chapter describes how the CFL-EDLSTM framework introduced in
Chapter~\ref{Ch_Chapter1} and motivated by Chapter~\ref{Ch_Chapter2} was built
and tested. It begins with the research design and an overview of the framework
(Sections~\ref{sec:3.2} and~\ref{sec:3.3}), then describes the study area,
stations and data (Sections~\ref{sec:3.4}--\ref{sec:3.8}). Preprocessing,
station clustering and the forecasting setup follow
(Sections~\ref{sec:3.9}--\ref{sec:3.11}). The proposed framework, the baselines
and the training configuration are set out next
(Sections~\ref{sec:3.12}--\ref{sec:3.14}), followed by the evaluation design and
metrics (Sections~\ref{sec:3.15} and~\ref{sec:3.16}), the SHAP analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:3.17}), the web interface (Section~\ref{sec:3.18}) and the
experimental procedure (Section~\ref{sec:3.19}). The results are reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Research Design}
\label{sec:3.2}

This is a quantitative, experimental study with a comparative design. One
proposed framework, CFL-EDLSTM, is evaluated against four baseline
architectures across four station clusters and seven forecasting horizons,
using historical observations. The work is applied: the aim is a forecasting
system that could plausibly be used, which is why a web interface and a
comparison with FFWC are part of the design and not an afterthought.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Overall Proposed Framework}
\label{sec:3.3}

At its core, the framework joins two ideas that Chapter~\ref{Ch_Chapter2}
examined separately: a sequence-to-sequence encoder--decoder LSTM for
multi-step forecasting \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022}, and clustered federated training,
in which stations are grouped by hydrological similarity and each group acts as
a federated client combined through Federated Averaging
\cite{McMahan2017,Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022}. Each of the four clusters trains
on its own stations' data, and only model parameters, never raw windows, are
shared and aggregated. The shared model is then adapted locally, so each
cluster forecasts with a model that reflects its own hydrology
(Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). Figure~\ref{fig:3.1} outlines the workflow, from data
preparation and clustering through federated training to forecasting,
interpretation and the web interface.

\section{Data Sources}
\label{sec:3.6}

Table~\ref{tab:3.2} lists the source, station coverage, use, collection period
and unit of each variable. Water level, discharge, rainfall and evaporation come
from BWDB gauge stations (25, 15, 13 and 7 stations respectively), and
temperature is district-level data from a Mendeley dataset and NASA POWER
covering 11 districts. Water level is the only series that spans the full
period, 1 January 2010 to 31 March 2026, so it sets the master timeline. The
other variables start or end at different dates (temperature covers only
2012--2024), and periods outside a variable's own record are filled as
described in Section~\ref{sec:3.9.2}. Temperature is district-level and not
station-level, so it is mapped onto stations by district
(Section~\ref{sec:3.9.1}) and should not be read as a direct gauge measurement.

\section{Dataset Description}
\label{sec:3.7}

Before any cleaning or alignment, the dataset holds 1,092,687 raw observations:
762,813 for water level, 46,812 for discharge, 71,110 for rainfall, 40,761 for
evaporation and 171,191 for temperature. Water level dominates because it covers
more stations and is recorded at a finer resolution (five observations a day,
against one for the other four variables).

Coverage across variables is uneven. Of the 13 districts in the dataset, only
four (Bogura, Dhaka, Rangpur and Sirajganj) have all five variables, most have
three or four, and Narayanganj has only water level and one other variable. This
unevenness is why clustering (Section~\ref{sec:3.10}) and careful preprocessing
(Section~\ref{sec:3.9}) matter: a model cannot assume that every station looks
like every other. Descriptive statistics of the final dataset are given in
Section~\ref{sec:4.2}.

\label{sec:3.8}

\subsection{Water Level}
\label{sec:3.8.1}

Water level is the target throughout the thesis. It is recorded at 25 BWDB
stations at 06:00, 09:00, 12:00, 15:00 and 18:00, three hours apart during the
day with a twelve-hour gap overnight. This is the finest resolution in the
dataset and is retained as the master modeling timeline
(Section~\ref{sec:3.9.3}). Measured in meters above mean sea level (mMSL), it
is the quantity every forecast horizon predicts, as in \cite{Ruma2023} and, for
fewer stations, \cite{Islam2026b}.

\subsection{Discharge}
\label{sec:3.8.2}

Discharge (mean daily discharge, MDD) is recorded at 15 of the 25 stations, in
m\textsuperscript{3}/s, and is used directly and through rolling-mean features.
The ten stations without a discharge record carry a zero-valued series
(Section~\ref{sec:3.9.2}). Islam et al.\ relied on discharge alone for their Old
Brahmaputra work and named the absence of meteorological variables as a
limitation \cite{Islam2026a}, which is what the rainfall, temperature and
evaporation inputs here address.

\subsection{Rainfall}
\label{sec:3.8.3}

Rainfall is recorded daily at 13 stations, in millimeters, and is used directly
and as cumulative windows to capture the delayed response of the catchment. This
follows the lag and cumulative treatment of \cite{Islam2026b} and the
areal-rainfall approach of \cite{Cui2022}, both of which found rainfall to carry
real, if secondary, predictive weight.

\subsection{Temperature}
\label{sec:3.8.4}

Temperature is the one variable that is not station-specific. It comes from a
Mendeley dataset and NASA POWER at daily resolution for 11 districts, with gaps
filled and values mapped to stations by district. Because it is district-level
and partly gap-filled, it should not be interpreted like a variable measured at
the station, a point that matters when reading the SHAP results
(Section~\ref{sec:3.17}).

\subsection{Evaporation}
\label{sec:3.8.5}

Evaporation is recorded daily at 7 stations, the narrowest coverage of any
variable, with gaps filled before use. Its inclusion follows \cite{Cui2022},
who found that adding evaporation to rainfall improved multi-step accuracy in an
encoder--decoder framework, although it had narrower coverage than the other
inputs there as well.

\label{sec:3.9}

Figure~\ref{fig:3.4} gives an overview of the preprocessing. The raw records are
cleaned and checked for gaps, aligned to the common water-level timeline,
normalized, split chronologically and finally expanded through feature
engineering. Each step is described below.

\subsection{Data Cleaning}
\label{sec:3.9.1}

Timestamps from all five sources are parsed into one datetime format, station
identifiers are standardized (Section~\ref{sec:3.5}), and records are sorted by
station and time. Each water-level reading is assigned to the nearest of the five
daily slots, and readings falling in the same station-slot are averaged.
Duplicates, impossible values, inconsistent units and likely transcription errors
are checked for. Extreme water-level values are inspected and not dropped
automatically, because in flood work an extreme reading is as likely to be a real
event as an error.

The station metadata is checked before the supporting variables are merged.
Rainfall, temperature and evaporation are assigned to the 25 stations by
district match, and where the station's own district has no source, the nearest
available station is used. Discharge is mapped directly to its own station
wherever a measurement exists, and the source gauge used for each station is kept
on record.

\subsection{Handling Missing Values}
\label{sec:3.9.2}

Missing water-level readings are not random noise. Many are likely tied to
flooding itself, since gauges can fail or go unread when levels spike. We
therefore first tried to recover missing values from the FFWC reports, but these
covered only part of the gaps and often did not match the missing stations or
times, so few values could be recovered this way. The remaining gaps were filled in three steps: linear interpolation, then
forward filling followed by backward filling, and finally zero for any value
that still could not be filled. The final dataset therefore has no missing
values (Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). Filled values are estimates and not
measurements, which matters most for the long dry-season stretches with
constant values and for the stations with no discharge record.

Discharge is handled separately because it is not measured at every station.
Its gaps go through the same three steps, so the ten stations without discharge
sensors end up with zero-valued MDD series throughout. Missing values in
rainfall, temperature and evaporation follow the same sequence.

\label{sec:3.9.3}

date. This repetition aligns the feature matrix and does not imply five

Each daily variable's reporting window is checked before use: a daily total
that includes measurements after a prediction time must not be treated as known
at that time, so inputs reflect only information available at the forecast
origin. Daily lags are generated on the original daily series before it is joined
to water level, and not by shifting rows of the expanded five-observation table.

The series is not uniformly sampled. Daytime observations are about three hours
apart, but the interval from 18:00 to 06:00 the next day is twelve hours, so the
elapsed time between observations is carried as an explicit feature
(Section~\ref{sec:3.9.6}). A 15-day horizon corresponds to 75 water-level
observations, and a 30-day input window to 150.

\subsection{Normalization}
\label{sec:3.9.4}

Every continuous variable is standardized (z-score scaling) before it reaches a
model. Water level, discharge, rainfall, temperature and evaporation are scaled
separately because they share neither units nor ranges, and a single shared scale
would let the variable with the largest raw magnitude dominate the loss.

How the scaler is fitted differs slightly by model, in line with each model's
data representation (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}). Vanilla LSTM and TCN treat each
station as an independent sequence and fit one input scaler and one target
scaler on the concatenated training portions of all 25 stations, so no single
station's scale dominates. GRU, which trains jointly across stations, fits its
own scalers on the training portion of its joint representation, and
CFL-EDLSTM fits its scalers on the training portion of the shared time axis
(Section~\ref{sec:3.12.2}). XGBoost, being tree-based, needs no scaling. In every
case the scalers are fitted on training data only and applied unchanged to
validation and test, so nothing from the held-out periods leaks into the inputs.
Missingness indicators and station identifiers are not scaled, every fitted
scaler is saved, and predictions are inverse-transformed to meters before any
metric is computed.

\subsection{Data Splitting}
\label{sec:3.9.5}

The data are split chronologically into training, validation and test sets in
the proportions 70:15:15. A random split would scatter temporally adjacent, and
even future, observations across the sets, which defeats the purpose of a
forecasting evaluation.Each station's own timeline is split in this way, so the last 15\% of every
station's record is held out for testing. A single chronological configuration
is used for every model, and on the shared time axis used by CFL-EDLSTM the test
period begins on 23 October 2023.

Input and target windows are always built in temporal order, with every target
strictly after its input and no window crossing a split boundary. The model,
scalers and fill parameters are fitted on training data alone, validation is used
only for model selection, and the test period is used only for the final
evaluation. Errors are reported at the seven horizons of
Section~\ref{sec:3.15}, in the original water-level units.

\paragraph{Rainfall accumulation.}
To capture how antecedent rainfall builds up before it shows in the river,
rainfall is accumulated over look-back windows of 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. For a
rainfall series $R$, the accumulation over the $k$ preceding days is
\begin{equation}
  R_{\mathrm{acc},k}(t) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} R(t - i),
  \qquad k \in \{3,\, 7,\, 14,\, 30\},
  \label{eq:3.1}
\end{equation}
which captures both short, sharp events and longer wet spells, using only
rainfall that occurred strictly before the forecast origin.

\paragraph{Lag features.}
Rainfall, discharge, temperature and evaporation are each given lagged versions
at 1, 2, 3, 5, 7 and 14 days. They are computed on the original daily series
before it is expanded across the five water-level slots; doing it afterwards
would treat five copies of one value as five independent lags and give the wrong
interval. Water level has no separate lag columns, since the 150-step input
window already carries its history.

\paragraph{Discharge-derived features.}
Rolling means of discharge over 3, 7, 14 and 30 days describe river flow over
different time scales. Because stations without discharge sensors carry zero
series (Section~\ref{sec:3.9.2}), their rolling means are also zero.

\paragraph{Temporal features.}
The time of day and day of year are encoded with sine and cosine transforms so
that the end of one cycle is close to its start, and the elapsed time between
consecutive observations is carried explicitly, because of the 12-hour overnight
gap against the roughly 3-hour daytime gaps.

\paragraph{Leakage prevention.}
All features are computed chronologically, using only information that would
have been available at each forecast origin, and the reporting window of each
daily variable is checked for this. The feature set is fixed at these 42 features
for all experiments.

\section{Station Clustering}
\label{sec:3.10}

Stations on one river system can behave very differently
(Chapter~\ref{Ch_Chapter2}), so training a single model on all of them risks
averaging that difference away. The stations are therefore grouped into four
clusters, each of which later becomes one federated client. This section
explains how the groups were formed (Section~\ref{sec:3.10.1}) and what they
contain (Section~\ref{sec:3.10.2}).

\subsection{Clustering Criteria}
\label{sec:3.10.1}

Stations were grouped using their hydrological and geographic position, cross-checked
manually against how stations are grouped for operational use on Bangladesh's
real-time flood forecasting platform (FFWC). Three factors informed the
grouping: position along the river system (upstream tributary, mid-river
mainstem, or downstream distributary reach); proximity to the Dhaka metropolitan
area; and, where geographic placement alone was ambiguous, FFWC's own operational
station groups, applied by hand and not derived from a statistical clustering
algorithm. The clusters are therefore fixed in advance from domain knowledge. This
addresses the gap noted in Chapter~\ref{Ch_Chapter2}, that general clustered
federated algorithms \cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026} were not built
for a hydrological network.

\section{Proposed CFL-EDLSTM Framework}
\label{sec:3.12}

Everything so far feeds into one forecasting framework, described here in full.
CFL-EDLSTM trains one encoder--decoder LSTM architecture across the four
clusters of Section~\ref{sec:3.10}. Each cluster is a federated client that
learns from its own stations' data. A server combines the four clients into a
shared global model by weighted Federated Averaging \cite{McMahan2017}, and every
cluster keeps its own locally adapted version of the model for forecasting. The
clusters are fixed in advance from hydrological knowledge, not discovered during
training as in IFCA \cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022}, so clustering decides
\emph{who the clients are} and federation decides \emph{how they learn together}.

The design follows from the problem in Chapter~\ref{Ch_Chapter2}. Stations on
one river system can behave very differently (Section~\ref{sec:2.9}), so one
model trained on all of them risks averaging that difference away, while a
separate model per station gives up what the stations could teach each other.
Federated learning allows joint learning without pooling raw data
\cite{McMahan2017}, but plain averaging struggles when client data are strongly
non-IID \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. Grouping stations into hydrologically coherent
clients and letting each keep an adapted model is this thesis's response. A
federated LSTM has been used for hydrological forecasting before
\cite{Amanzholova2026}, but with two stations, one variable and a one-day
horizon, whereas here there are four clients, 42 input features and horizons out
to 15 days. Table~\ref{tab:3.8} collects the configuration.

\subsection{Encoder--Decoder Architecture}
\label{sec:3.12.1}

Forecasting 75 future values from 150 past observations is a sequence-to-sequence
problem, and the encoder--decoder pattern \cite{Cho2014} is built
for it: one network reads the input and compresses it into a state, and a second
unrolls that state into the output. The Vanilla LSTM baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:3.13.1}) does the same job with a dense layer that emits all 75
values at once, whereas the decoder generates them one step at a time, so each
value can condition on the one before. In hydrology, an LSTM encoder--decoder has
held up better than a feed-forward one as the horizon grows \cite{Kao2020}, has
accumulated less error than recursive decoding when given suitable extra inputs
\cite{Cui2022}, and remains competitive in recent multi-step flood work
\cite{Lim2025,Yuan2023}. Figure~\ref{fig:3.8} shows the architecture.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures2/fig3-8-edlstm-architecture.png}
    \caption{Encoder--decoder LSTM architecture.}
    \label{fig:3.8}
\end{figure}

For station $s$ and window start $t$, the encoder receives the previous 30 days
(150 steps) and the model forecasts the next 15 days (75 steps):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{X}_{t,s} = \big[\mathbf{x}_{t,s},\, \mathbf{x}_{t+1,s},\, \dots,\, \mathbf{x}_{t+149,s}\big], \qquad
  \mathbf{Y}_{t,s} = \big[y_{t+150,s},\, y_{t+151,s},\, \dots,\, y_{t+224,s}\big],
  \label{eq:3.2}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}_{t,s} \in \mathbb{R}^{42}$ is the feature vector described in
Table~\ref{tab:features} and $y_{t,s}$ is water level (mMSL).

The encoder is a two-layer LSTM \cite{HochreiterSchmidhuber1997} with 128 hidden
units per layer and dropout of 0.2 \cite{Srivastava2014}. It reads the 150 steps
in order, updating a hidden and a cell state through the input, forget and output
gates:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{i}_j &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_i \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_i), &
  \mathbf{f}_j &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_f \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_f),\\
  \mathbf{o}_j &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_o \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_o), &
  \mathbf{g}_j &= \tanh(\mathbf{W}_g \mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{U}_g \mathbf{h}_{j-1} + \mathbf{b}_g),\\
  \mathbf{c}_j &= \mathbf{f}_j \odot \mathbf{c}_{j-1} + \mathbf{i}_j \odot \mathbf{g}_j, &
  \mathbf{h}_j &= \mathbf{o}_j \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_j),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:3.3}
\end{equation}
written for a single layer, with $\sigma$ the logistic sigmoid and $\odot$ the
element-wise product. In the second layer the first layer's hidden state replaces
$\mathbf{x}_j$. The encoder's final hidden and cell states summarize the 30-day
history and initialize the decoder, a second two-layer LSTM with 128 hidden
units:
\begin{equation}
  \big(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_0,\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{dec}}_0\big) = \big(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{enc}}_{150},\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{enc}}_{150}\big).
  \label{eq:3.4}
\end{equation}
The decoder then generates the 75 values one at a time. At step $q$ it takes the
previous water-level value $u_q$, updates its states, and a linear layer projects
the hidden state to a single value:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \big(\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q,\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q\big) &= \mathrm{LSTM}_{\mathrm{dec}}\big(u_q,\, \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_{q-1},\, \mathbf{c}^{\mathrm{dec}}_{q-1}\big),\\
  \hat{y}_q &= \mathbf{W}_o \mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{dec}}_q + b_o, \qquad q = 1, \dots, 75.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:3.5}
\end{equation}
During training $u_q$ can be the observed value, a practice known as teacher
forcing. At inference no future observations exist, so
the decoder is fed its own previous prediction, and the first step is seeded with
the last observed water level in the input window.

Step-by-step generation has a known cost. Because inference feeds predictions
back in, early errors can compound, and a decoder trained on observed values
never practices recovering from its own mistakes, the error-accumulation and
exposure-bias problem that \cite{Cui2022} describe. We accept this risk in
exchange for a coherent forecast trajectory, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports
error at each of the seven horizons so that any degradation is visible.

\subsection{Local Model Training}
\label{sec:3.12.2}

Each of the four clusters in Table~\ref{tab:3.5} is one federated client, $c_1$
to $c_4$, holding every training window of its own stations. Before training, the
cluster definitions are validated: no station may appear in two clusters, every
station must be assigned to one, and every station named in a cluster must exist
in the data. The number of stations in cluster $k$, $n_k = |S_k|$, later serves as
that client's aggregation weight (Section~\ref{sec:3.12.4}).

All stations are first placed on a common time axis: date and time fields are
combined into timestamps, rows with invalid or duplicate timestamps are dropped,
the rest are sorted, and the axis is the intersection of every station's
timestamps. The data are then arranged as a [time, station, feature] array with
a matching [time, station] array of water-level targets, and any remaining gaps
are filled as in Section~\ref{sec:3.9.2}. The pipeline rejects any non-finite
value.

% The common series is split chronologically, 70\% for training, 15\% for
% validation and 15\% for testing, without shuffling. Windows are generated inside
% each split so none crosses a boundary: a window starting at $t$ is valid only if
% it fits entirely in its split, that is $a \le t \le b - 150 - 75$ for a split
% spanning indices $a$ to $b$. Inputs are standardized feature by feature, and
% water level is standardized again with its own target scaler, both fitted on
% the training portion only. The target scaler later converts predictions back to
% meters. Table~\ref{tab:testwindows} in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} gives the number
% of test windows each model was scored on.


As for the other models, each station's own timeline is split chronologically,
70\% for training, 15\% for validation and 15\% for testing, without shuffling,
so every station is scored on the last 15\% of its own record
(Section~\ref{sec:4.2}). Windows are generated inside each split. Inputs are
standardized feature by feature, and water level is standardized again with its
own target scaler, both fitted on the training portion only. The target scaler
later converts predictions back to meters. The number of test windows each model
was scored on is given in Table~\ref{tab:testwindows}.

Each client trains against the Huber loss on standardized targets
\cite{Huber1964},
\begin{equation}
  \ell_\delta(r) =
  \begin{cases}
    \tfrac{1}{2}\, r^{2}, & |r| \le \delta,\\[2pt]
    \delta\big(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\big), & |r| > \delta,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Huber}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \ell_\delta(\hat{y}_j - y_j), \quad \delta = 1,
  \label{eq:3.6}
\end{equation}
as for the neural baselines (Section~\ref{sec:3.14.3}). Optimization uses Adam
\cite{Kingma2015} with a learning rate of 0.005 and a batch size of 64, with
gradients clipped to a maximum norm of 1 \cite{Pascanu2013}. In each round a
client starts from the parameters received from the server, trains on its own
windows for three local epochs and returns the result, giving 90 local epochs per
client over 30 rounds.

\subsection{Federated Model Updates}
\label{sec:3.12.3}

Training runs for 30 federated rounds, each following the same five steps
(Figure~\ref{fig:3.9}). The server broadcasts the global parameters to all four
clients; each client trains locally for three epochs; each returns its updated
parameters; the server combines them into the next global model
(Section~\ref{sec:3.12.4}); and each client's locally trained model is evaluated
on that cluster's validation windows. Only parameter tensors travel between
clients and server, never training windows or station records, which is what makes
federated averaging attractive for distributed monitoring networks
\cite{McMahan2017}. The federation is simulated on a single workstation
(Section~\ref{sec:3.19}): the four clients are separate data partitions with
separate local training loops, and aggregation is the only point at which they
interact.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures2/fig3-9-federated-workflow.png}
    \caption{Federated workflow of CFL-EDLSTM.}
    \label{fig:3.9}
\end{figure}

Only weighted Federated Averaging is used. Extensions that correct for client
drift, such as adding a proximal term to the local objective, are not applied. This is
a deliberate simplification: whatever heterogeneity handling the framework has
comes from the cluster structure and the locally adapted models of
Section~\ref{sec:3.12.5}, which keeps the contribution of clustering easy to
isolate. Client drift is still a genuine concern, since clients with different
data can pull the shared parameters in different directions
\cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. Grouping similar stations makes each client's data more
internally consistent, though differences between clients remain.

\subsection{Cluster-Wise Aggregation}
\label{sec:3.12.4}

Aggregation happens at the level of clusters. With $K = 4$ clients, the server
weights each client by the number of stations in its cluster and averages every
parameter tensor:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_k = \frac{n_k}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} n_j}, \qquad
  \mathbf{w}^{(r+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k\, \mathbf{w}_k^{(r)}, \qquad
  \sum_{k=1}^{K} \alpha_k = 1,
  \label{eq:3.7}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}_k^{(r)}$ are client $k$'s parameters after local training in
round $r$. For the cluster sizes in Table~\ref{tab:3.5}, the Upstream, Mid-river,
Downstream and Urban clusters carry weights of $6/25$, $8/25$, $5/25$ and $6/25$,
that is 0.24, 0.32, 0.20 and 0.24.

Weighting by stations and not by windows departs only in form from the original
FedAvg rule, which weights by local examples \cite{McMahan2017}. Because every
station is aligned to the same time axis, each produces the same number of
windows, so a cluster's window count is its station count times a constant.
Weighting by stations also states the intent plainly: every station should count
equally in the global model.

It is worth being clear about what this step produces. The aggregated model is a
single global model that has learned from all 25 stations, but the models used
for forecasting are not these aggregated parameters. They are the locally adapted
states each cluster reaches after its own local training in a round
(Section~\ref{sec:3.12.5}). Keeping both balances shared learning against local
fit \cite{Zhu2021}. It is also a simple reading of ``clustered'' federated
learning: here the cluster structure defines the clients, whereas methods such as
IFCA let the server assign clients to groups and maintain a model for each
\cite{Ghosh2021}.

\subsection{Model Redistribution}
\label{sec:3.12.5}

At the start of each round, the newly aggregated global parameters are sent back
to all four clients and replace whatever they held, so every round begins from the
same shared point. A client's locally trained state is not discarded, though:
after local training, each cluster's model is evaluated on its own validation
windows and kept as that cluster's personalized model.

After every round, a single weighted validation loss is computed across the four
clusters,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val}}^{(r)} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K} m_k\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{val},k}^{(r)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} m_k},
  \label{eq:3.8}
\end{equation}
where $m_k$ is the number of validation windows in cluster $k$ (unlike the
aggregation weights, these are window counts). Whenever the loss improves, a
checkpoint is saved containing the global model, each cluster's personalized
state, both scalers, the station and cluster definitions, the 42 feature names,
the input and output lengths and the architecture metadata. Selecting the
checkpoint by validation loss, and not taking the final round, guards against
late-round overfitting, as for the baselines (Section~\ref{sec:3.14.1}).

After the 30 rounds, the best checkpoint is loaded. For each cluster, a fresh
encoder--decoder is created, that cluster's personalized state is loaded into it,
and its test windows are forecast. Predictions and targets are
inverse-transformed to meters before any metric is computed, and MAE and RMSE
(Section~\ref{sec:3.16}) are calculated over the concatenated test predictions of
all four clusters.

\paragraph{Why these baselines.}
\label{sec:baseline_rationale}
Each baseline represents a different modeling family, so that the comparison
shows whether the clustered federated encoder--decoder improves on established
alternatives that differ from it in one clear respect. XGBoost is a strong
non-deep-learning reference for lagged tabular features. The Vanilla LSTM shares
the recurrent core and so isolates the effect of the encoder--decoder design. The
GRU tests whether a simpler gated cell is enough, and the TCN offers a
non-recurrent alternative, which has been used for flood forecasting in several
studies (Section~\ref{sec:2.6.4}). All four are small enough to train on roughly
16 years of data per station. They are trained centrally, per station or jointly,
and none is federated, so the comparison does not separate the effect of
federation from that of the encoder--decoder.

Other models were considered and set aside. A plain RNN cannot retain the
multi-day lag between upstream rainfall and downstream water level because of
vanishing gradients, which the LSTM was designed to remove. A bidirectional LSTM
would use future values that are unavailable at forecast time, making its
evaluation invalid. CNN--LSTM models suit gridded inputs such as radar or
satellite rasters, whereas our data are point series from gauges. A feed-forward
network on lagged features does the same job as XGBoost with less
interpretability. Transformer-based models, including the Temporal Fusion
Transformer, typically need far more data than we have per station, and their
attention matrices are costly to exchange in a federated setting. Graph-based
models were examined most closely because stations along a river form a natural
network. We experimented with a spatio-temporal graph neural network (ST-GNN)
but did not include it, because lagged upstream features already carry much of
the station-to-station relationship and a graph model would need data shared
across stations, which conflicts with keeping raw data within clusters.

\paragraph{Matching the setup.}
The baselines are matched to CFL-EDLSTM as closely as their data representations
allow: the same task (a 30-day input of 150 observations and a 15-day forecast of
75), the same seven horizon checkpoints (decoding steps 1, 2, 4, 5, 15, 35 and
75), the same 70/15/15 chronological split with no window crossing a boundary,
and scalers fitted on training data only. Missing values are handled in the same
spirit, by filling with a fallback value where needed.

The setups are not identical, and this matters for reading Chapter
\ref{ch:results}. Vanilla LSTM, TCN and XGBoost split each station's own record
independently. GRU works on a joint representation aligned on the union of all
stations' timestamps, and CFL-EDLSTM on their intersection
(Section~\ref{sec:3.12.2}), so the numbers of test windows differ across models.
The inputs also differ: Vanilla LSTM and TCN see ten features per time step
(five base variables and five time features), and XGBoost sees 150 lagged
water-level values plus the current readings, time features and a station
indicator, whereas CFL-EDLSTM uses 42 engineered features. Part of CFL-EDLSTM's
advantage may therefore come from richer inputs and not from its architecture
alone, a limitation that Chapter~\ref{ch:results} keeps in view.

\paragraph{Federated variants.}
Because the proposed model is federated, comparing it with centrally trained
baselines cannot show why the encoder--decoder LSTM was chosen as the model to
federate. XGBoost, GRU and TCN were therefore also trained under the same
clustered federated scheme as CFL-EDLSTM (the same four clusters, 30 rounds,
three local epochs and weighted averaging; Section~\ref{sec:3.12}), giving
CFL-XGBoost, CFL-GRU and CFL-TCN.Their results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:4.5.1}.

\subsection{Vanilla LSTM}
\label{sec:3.13.1}

The Vanilla LSTM is the standard recurrent architecture against which every
other model is implicitly measured. LSTM was introduced to solve the
vanishing-gradient problem of plain RNNs by giving the network a cell state
gated by input, forget and output gates \cite{HochreiterSchmidhuber1997}. This is
what lets a rainfall signal from several days earlier still influence a
prediction made today, which matters in the Brahmaputra basin, where the chain
from upstream rainfall to a downstream peak typically unfolds over five to ten
days.

Each of the 25 stations is treated as an independent sequence. Station files are
combined into one long-form table, sorted by station and timestamp, with
duplicate rows removed. At each step the model sees ten features (water level,
rainfall, MDD, evaporation, temperature, and hour\_sin, hour\_cos, doy\_sin,
doy\_cos and delta\_hours), and the target is the next 75 water-level
observations for the same station. Gaps are filled per station, and the input and
target scalers (both StandardScaler) are fitted on the concatenated training
portions of all stations.

The network has two stacked LSTM layers of 128 hidden units with dropout 0.2,
followed by a fully connected decoder that outputs all 75 values at once. It
trains with Adam at a learning rate of $1\times10^{-3}$, batch size 64 and 30
epochs, using Huber loss \cite{Huber1964} and gradient clipping at 1.0. The
checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is retained, and test predictions come
from it and not from the last epoch.

\subsection{GRU}
\label{sec:3.13.2}

The GRU tests whether LSTM's separate cell state earns its keep on this task or
whether a simpler gating mechanism gets most of the way. It merges the input and
forget gates into one update gate and uses the hidden state directly as memory
\cite{Cho2014}, which gives roughly 25\% fewer parameters than an equally sized
LSTM and faster training per epoch.

Unlike the station-wise LSTM, the GRU is trained on all stations jointly. Every
station is aligned on the union of their timestamps, and at each timestamp the
model sees the water level and available auxiliary features for every station,
plus the five global time features. It predicts the future water level of every
station simultaneously, as a tensor of roughly [batch, 75, stations]. Missing
timestamps and values are filled on this shared index, and scalers are fitted on
the chronological training portion only.

The architecture mirrors the LSTM baseline on purpose: two stacked GRU layers of
128 units, dropout 0.2, a decoder producing $75 \times$ (number of stations)
values, and the identical recipe (Adam at $1\times10^{-3}$, batch size 64, 30
epochs, Huber loss, gradient clipping at 1.0, best checkpoint by validation loss).
Holding all of this fixed turns the LSTM-versus-GRU comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:4.5} into a genuine architecture ablation.

\subsection{XGBoost}
\label{sec:3.13.3}

XGBoost sets the minimum bar that any recurrent or convolutional model needs to
clear before its added complexity is justified. It is an ensemble of decision
trees trained sequentially, each fitted to correct the residual errors of the ones
before \cite{ChenGuestrin2016}. It has no built-in notion of sequence, so all
temporal structure must be supplied as engineered features.

For each station, a training sample holds 150 lagged water-level values (oldest
to newest), the current rainfall, MDD, evaporation and temperature, the time
features, and a one-hot station indicator. The target is the next 75
water-level values, and because one regressor cannot produce a 75-step sequence,
XGBoost is trained as 75 independent regressors, one per step.

A window's split depends on where the whole input-plus-output window falls in the
station's chronological record: training windows end within the first 70\% of the
record, validation windows within the first 85\%, and test windows start after
that 85\% boundary. Any window straddling a boundary is discarded, not truncated.
No scaling is applied. Each regressor is an XGBRegressor with a squared-error
objective, at most 300 estimators, depth 6, learning rate 0.05, row and column
subsampling of 0.8, L1 and L2 regularization of 0.1 and 1.0, the histogram tree
method, and early stopping after 30 rounds without validation improvement.

\subsection{TCN}
\label{sec:3.13.4}

The Temporal Convolutional Network uses dilated causal convolutions in place of
recurrence to reach back across long time spans. It is included as an additional
sequence-modeling comparison on architectural merit and training speed, not as
the thesis's primary multi-step architecture. It has been used in flood
forecasting elsewhere \cite{Ni2024,Lin2021,Li2024,Li2025} but less so in
Bangladesh, so it is reported as a secondary baseline and not promoted to
Baseline~1 or~2.

The TCN uses the same station-wise, ten-feature representation as the Vanilla
LSTM, with a 75-step target per station. Data are prepared per station, scalers
are fitted on the combined training sections of all stations, and each split is
windowed independently so no window crosses a boundary. The network has four
residual temporal blocks with channel widths (64, 64, 128, 128), kernel size 3,
dilation factors 1, 2, 4 and 8, and dropout 0.2, followed by a decoder producing
all 75 values. Training uses Adam at $1\times10^{-3}$, batch size 128 (larger
than for the recurrent baselines, since convolution parallelizes more readily), 30
epochs, Huber loss, gradient clipping at 1.0 and best-checkpoint selection by
validation loss.

\section{Training Configuration and Regularization}
\label{sec:3.14}

\subsection{Model Selection and Overfitting Control}
\label{sec:3.14.1}

For every neural model (Vanilla LSTM, GRU, TCN and CFL-EDLSTM), model selection
is governed by validation loss and not training loss. A checkpoint is saved
whenever validation loss improves, and that best checkpoint, not the final epoch,
produces the test predictions in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. This works alongside
dropout (0.2) and gradient clipping (maximum norm 1.0) to keep overfitting in
check. XGBoost has no epoch loop, so its equivalent safeguards are early stopping
on the validation set after 30 rounds, subsampling of 0.8 for rows and columns,
and L1/L2 regularization.

\subsection{Training Settings}
\label{sec:3.14.2}

Every model was trained with a fixed, documented configuration and not a
separate hyperparameter search. Table~\ref{tab:3.7} lists the values used for
every result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, and the only selection made on
validation data is the choice of checkpoint (and, for XGBoost, the stopping
point). This keeps the comparison simple to describe and reproduce, but the
results reflect these settings and not the best each architecture could reach with
extensive tuning. A baseline that improved with more careful tuning would narrow
its gap to the other models.

Where the architectures allow it, the neural baselines share their settings.
Vanilla LSTM and GRU use two layers of 128 units, dropout 0.2
\cite{Srivastava2014}, Adam \cite{Kingma2015} at $1\times10^{-3}$, 30 epochs,
batch size 64 and gradient clipping at 1 \cite{Pascanu2013}, so the comparison
between them isolates the architecture (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}). The TCN follows
the same optimizer, loss, dropout and epoch budget, differing only where its
structure requires. XGBoost has its own controls, since it adds trees and does
not descend a gradient on network weights \cite{ChenGuestrin2016}; its learning
rate of 0.05 is a shrinkage factor on each new tree and is not comparable with the
neural learning rates.

CFL-EDLSTM shares the hidden size, dropout, batch size, loss, optimizer and
clipping of the baselines, but its schedule is federated: each of the four
clients trains for three local epochs in each of 30 rounds
\cite{McMahan2017}, giving 90 local epochs, with a local learning rate of 0.005
and a two-layer decoder (Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). These differences come with
the federated setup and are not a tuned advantage, but they do mean the settings
are comparable and not identical.

\subsection{Loss Function and Regularization}
\label{sec:3.14.3}

All neural models, the three baselines and CFL-EDLSTM, are optimized with Huber
loss and not plain mean squared error \cite{Huber1964}. Huber loss is quadratic
for small residuals and linear beyond a threshold, which dampens the influence of
extreme water-level values, such as a sensor artifact or a genuine flash-flood
peak, on the gradient without discarding those points. Because it operates in the
standardized training space, it does not appear in the evaluation; all metrics in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are computed after inverse-transforming predictions to
meters. Other regularization comes from dropout (0.2) on every recurrent and
convolutional model, and from the penalties and subsampling described for
XGBoost.

\section{Multi-Horizon Forecasting Setup}
\label{sec:3.15}

Every model is evaluated at the same horizons, so that the comparison in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is on identical terms. Given an input window of
observations $[t-150, \ldots, t-1]$ (30 days) and an output window
$[t, \ldots, t+74]$ (15 days), the reported checkpoints are 3~hours, 6~hours,
12~hours, 1~day, 3~days, 7~days and 15~days, corresponding to decoding steps 1,
2, 4, 5, 15, 35 and 75. These labels are fixed evaluation points shared by all
models and not an independently inferred sampling interval.

Results are reported under three groupings: \textit{overall} (every station,
window and forecast step), \textit{station-wise} (all steps for one station) and
\textit{horizon-wise} (one step across all stations and test windows). Overall MAE
and RMSE, in meters, are the primary comparison metrics, since they remain
comparable across models even when the number of usable test windows differs
(Section~\ref{sec:3.13}).

\section{Model Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:3.16}

Seven metrics are reported for every model and grouping: MAE, RMSE, MSE, NSE
(Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency), R$^2$, EVS (explained variance score) and IOA
(index of agreement). All are computed after inverse-transforming predictions to
the original scale. With $M$ predictions $\hat{y}_j$ against observations $y_j$
and observed mean $\bar{y}$, the main ones are
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}|\hat{y}_j - y_j|, \qquad
  \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}(\hat{y}_j - y_j)^2},
  \label{eq:mae_rmse}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{NSE} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}(y_j - \hat{y}_j)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}(y_j - \bar{y})^2}, \qquad
  \mathrm{IOA} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}(\hat{y}_j - y_j)^2}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\big(|\hat{y}_j - \bar{y}| + |y_j - \bar{y}|\big)^2}.
  \label{eq:nse_ioa}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Mean Absolute Error (MAE)}
\label{sec:3.16.1}

MAE is the mean of the absolute differences between predicted and observed water
level, in meters. It is the most directly interpretable metric for a
hydrological audience because it expresses typical error in the same unit as the
readings, and it is the headline metric throughout Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)}
\label{sec:3.16.2}

RMSE is the square root of the mean squared difference, also in meters. Because
errors are squared before averaging, it penalizes large errors, such as a badly
missed flood peak, more heavily than MAE. It is reported alongside MAE for that
reason: a model with low MAE but comparatively high RMSE makes small errors
usually and large ones occasionally, which matters for flood forecasting.

\subsection{Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$) and NSE}
\label{sec:3.16.3}

$R^2$ is the share of variance in the observed series that the predictions
account for, with 1.0 being perfect. NSE is its hydrological counterpart and
dominates the flood-forecasting literature \cite{NashSutcliffe1970}. In our
results NSE and $R^2$ are identical for every model and grouping, so
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports them as one NSE/$R^2$ column. Because stations
sit at very different mean levels, NSE computed over all stations together is
higher than at cluster or station level, so Chapter~\ref{ch:results} relies
mainly on MAE and RMSE when comparing models and clusters.

\subsection{Additional Metrics}
\label{sec:3.16.4}

MSE (in square meters) is the quantity RMSE is derived from and appears in the
full tables for completeness. EVS is close to $R^2$ but does not penalize a
constant bias, so a model whose EVS sits clearly above its $R^2$ tracks the shape
of the series well but carries a systematic offset. IOA is bounded between 0 and
1 and is less sensitive than NSE to a few unusually large errors, which is why, in
Section~\ref{sec:4.6}, IOA and NSE occasionally rank models slightly differently
at the 15-day horizon.

\section{Explainability Using SHAP}
\label{sec:3.17}

Accuracy tells us how well a model performs, but not what it has learned. To look
inside the trained models, we applied SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), which
assigns each input value a contribution to a prediction, measured against the
model's output on a set of reference inputs \cite{Lundberg2017}. The analysis
asks two questions: which input features drive the forecast most, and how far back
in the 30-day window the model looks.

\subsection{Model and Data Explained}
\label{sec:3.17.1}

The analysis was run on the proposed CFL-EDLSTM. Its four cluster-specific
models, each using the 42 input features and the encoder--decoder structure of
Section~\ref{sec:3.12}, were explained separately. Inputs were standardized with
the scalers fitted on the training period, exactly as in training, and all
explained windows came from the chronological test period (from 23 October
2023), so the explanations describe behavior on data the models had not seen.

\subsection{Reference and Explanation Windows}
\label{sec:3.17.2}

SHAP expresses a contribution relative to a baseline, so a common reference was
fixed first: a background set of 32 windows drawn at random from the training
period across all 25 stations, used for every cluster and horizon. For each
cluster, 48 windows were drawn at random from the test period, pooled across its
stations. All sampling used a fixed seed (42), so the selected windows can be
reproduced.

\subsection{Explaining One Forecast Step}
\label{sec:3.17.3}

The model outputs 75 values, whereas SHAP explains one number at a time. We
therefore explained the final step of four forecast days, day~1, 3, 7 and 15,
which correspond to the 1-, 3-, 7- and 15-day horizons of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. The model was kept in evaluation mode, so dropout was
off and the outputs were deterministic.

\subsection{Expected Gradients}
\label{sec:3.17.4}

Attributions were computed with the gradient-based SHAP explainer, which
implements Expected Gradients, a combination of Integrated Gradients and SHAP.
For a model $f$, an explained input $x$ and a background distribution $D$, the
attribution of input element $i$ is
\begin{equation}
\phi_i(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x' \sim D,\; \alpha \sim U(0,1)}
\left[ (x_i - x'_i)\,
\frac{\partial f\bigl(x' + \alpha (x - x')\bigr)}{\partial x_i} \right].
\label{eq:expected_gradients}
\end{equation}
In words, a random background window $x'$ and a random point on the line between
$x'$ and $x$ are chosen, the gradient of the output is taken there, and it is
multiplied by the distance of $x$ from $x'$. Averaging over many draws (100 per
explained window) gives each element's contribution. Because one window has
$150 \times 42$ input elements, each window receives a matrix of 6{,}300 SHAP
values, one for every pair of timestep and feature, and the values approximately
add up to the difference between the explained prediction and the average
prediction on the background.

\subsection{Excluding Water Level from the Reported Results}
\label{sec:3.17.5}

Water level is an input, so SHAP also produces an attribution for it. Past water
level is by far the strongest predictor of future water level, and reporting it
would hide the contribution of everything else. We therefore computed the
attributions with water level present and then removed its column before any
averaging, ranking or plotting. The attributions of the remaining 41 features are
unchanged. They describe each feature's contribution while the model can also see
water level, not what it would contribute to a model trained without it.

\subsection{Aggregation}
\label{sec:3.17.6}

Let $\Phi$ be the array of SHAP values with $N$ windows, $T=150$ timesteps and
$F=41$ features. The importance of feature $f$ is its mean absolute SHAP value
over all windows and timesteps, and the importance of input timestep $t$ is the
mean absolute value over all windows and features:
\begin{equation}
I_f = \frac{1}{NT}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl|\Phi_{n,t,f}\bigr|,
\qquad
I_t = \frac{1}{NF}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{f=1}^{F}\bigl|\Phi_{n,t,f}\bigr|.
\label{eq:shap_importance}
\end{equation}
Absolute values are used because a feature that raises the forecast in some
windows and lowers it in others would otherwise cancel out and look unimportant.
The results of all four clusters and four horizons were pooled for reporting,
giving $4 \times 4 \times 48 = 768$ windows with equal weight. Because the models
are trained on standardized water level, SHAP values are in standard deviations
of water level and can be converted to meters by multiplying by the training
standard deviation, and the rankings are the same in either unit.

\section{Experimental Procedure}
\label{sec:3.19}

Training and evaluation follow a fixed order, so that each stage gives the next
something to be compared against. The four baselines are trained first, each on
its own data representation (Section~\ref{sec:3.13}). XGBoost is the sanity-check
reference: if a sequence model cannot beat it, that points to a problem in
preprocessing and not a need for more architectural complexity. Vanilla LSTM and
GRU provide the standard recurrent comparison and the LSTM-versus-GRU ablation,
with TCN as a secondary comparison. CFL-EDLSTM is then trained as described in
Section~\ref{sec:3.12}: 30 federated rounds across the four cluster clients using
weighted Federated Averaging \cite{McMahan2017}, with the checkpoint of lowest
weighted validation loss retained and each cluster's locally adapted model used
for its test forecasts.

Every model is evaluated on the final 15\% of its chronological data at the same
seven horizons, using the metrics of Section~\ref{sec:3.16}. As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:3.13}, the models differ somewhat in their windows and inputs, so
the comparison carries those caveats. No random seed was fixed for training, so
repeated runs can differ slightly. Training and evaluation used an AMD Ryzen~5
3600 six-core processor at 3.60~GHz, 24~GB of RAM and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~3060
with 12~GB of memory. PyTorch models use CUDA where available, and XGBoost is
configured for the same device.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:3.20}

This chapter described how the study was built, from the raw records to the models
that are judged against each other. The data come from 25 stations in the
Brahmaputra river system and five variables (water level, discharge, rainfall,
temperature and evaporation), which were cleaned, aligned and expanded into 42
input features (Section~\ref{sec:3.9}). The stations were grouped into four
clusters, Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream and Urban, from their position along
the river and their operational grouping by FFWC (Section~\ref{sec:3.10}). Each
model reads 30 days of observations and forecasts the next 15, with results at
seven horizons from 3~hours to 15~days.

CFL-EDLSTM treats each cluster as a federated client. The clients train a shared
encoder--decoder LSTM locally, a weighted Federated Averaging step combines them,
and each cluster keeps a locally adapted version for forecasting, so raw data are
not pooled across clusters (Section~\ref{sec:3.12}). It is compared with XGBoost,
Vanilla LSTM, GRU and TCN under fixed, documented configurations
(Sections~\ref{sec:3.13} and~\ref{sec:3.14}) and scored on seven metrics
(Section~\ref{sec:3.16}). SHAP and a web interface with an FFWC comparison view
cover interpretation and presentation (Sections~\ref{sec:3.17}
and~\ref{sec:3.18}). Two limits carry into the results: the federation is
simulated on one workstation, and the models differ somewhat in windows and inputs.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports what the procedure produced.
