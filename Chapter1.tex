\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:1.1}

Bangladesh occupies the lower deltaic plain of the Ganges--Brahmaputra--Meghna
river system, one of the largest and most active river networks in the world.
Intense monsoon rainfall, large volumes of discharge from upstream catchments
and very low-lying terrain together leave the country persistently exposed to
flooding and water-logging. Figure~\ref{fig:hydro-context} shows how little
relief the country has: almost all of Bangladesh lies close to sea level, and
higher ground appears only along its northern and eastern margins. Because
rainfall, discharge and drainage conditions change from one place to another,
water-level behavior changes with them, and a model developed for one location
cannot be assumed to work equally well at another.

\caption{Elevation of Bangladesh (adapted from \cite{Dewan2022}).}
\label{fig:hydro-context}
\end{figure}

This chapter introduces the thesis. Sections~\ref{sec:1.2} and~\ref{sec:1.3}
describe the flood setting and explain why forecasting at several lead times
matters. Sections~\ref{sec:1.4}--\ref{sec:1.6} state the problem, the
motivation and the research gap. Sections~\ref{sec:1.7}--\ref{sec:1.11} set out
the aim, objectives, research questions, scope and contributions, and
Sections~\ref{sec:1.12} and~\ref{sec:1.13} give the organization of the thesis
and a summary of the chapter. No results are reported here; they are presented
in Chapter~4.

\section{Flooding and Water-Logging in Bangladesh}
\label{sec:1.2}

A large part of Bangladesh's flood problem originates outside the country.
Bangladesh lies at the downstream end of the Ganges--Brahmaputra--Meghna basin,
and only a fraction of the water in its rivers falls as rain within its own
borders. As Figure~\ref{fig:river-context} shows, the three basins extend across
India, Nepal, Bhutan and China, and the water draining from them converges on
Bangladesh before reaching the Bay of Bengal. Much of the river flow expected
in the coming week has therefore already been generated elsewhere, days
earlier.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures2/river_context.png}
\caption{The Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna basins and the major river systems
of Bangladesh (adapted from \cite{BDP2100Atlas}).}
\label{fig:river-context}
\end{figure}

The flood record shows how often this setting turns into disaster. In 1988,
about 82,000~km$^2$, close to 60\% of the country, was inundated in what is
still regarded as a once-in-50-to-100-year event \cite{Banglapedia2023Flood}.
In 1998, more than two-thirds of the country was flooded and more than 2,300
people died \cite{Banglapedia2023Flood,AbdullahAlam2024}. Severe floods
followed in 2004, 2007, most years from 2009 to 2014, and 2020, and in 2017
households in the worst-affected areas lost more than a fifth of their annual
income \cite{AbdullahAlam2024}. Flooding is thus a recurring part of life on the
floodplain, and even a modest gain in forecast accuracy or lead time benefits
a very large number of people.

The impact of high water also depends on where it occurs. In rural
floodplains, water-logging follows the river fairly directly, whereas in
rapidly growing cities poor drainage can make the same rise in water level
considerably more damaging. Studies in Bangladesh have shown that even
neighboring stations on the same river can behave differently
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b}. Upstream stations respond mainly to discharge and
rainfall from higher in the catchment, while downstream and urban stations are
shaped by a combination of river flow, local rainfall, drainage and, in some
cases, tides. There is therefore no basis for assuming that all stations behave
alike.

The forecasting problem has been approached from several directions. Ruma et
al.\ forecast water levels across a wide network of Bangladeshi stations using
an optimized LSTM \cite{Ruma2023}. Other studies combined climatic variables
with machine learning to predict flooding \cite{Rakin2023,Rajab2023}, and
recent work has addressed discharge and hydrological modeling in the Old
Brahmaputra basin \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b}. Taken together, these studies
show that data-driven forecasting is feasible in Bangladesh. They also note,
repeatedly, that individual stations have their own hydrological character,
which a single averaged model tends to obscure.

\section{Importance of Water-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:1.3}

Reliable and timely forecasts that reflect a station's position in the river
network support flood preparedness, early warning and disaster risk
reduction. Different lead times serve different decisions: a forecast a few
hours ahead supports immediate response, whereas a forecast several days ahead
supports planning.

Accuracy becomes harder to maintain as the lead time increases. Even so,
machine-learning and deep-learning models have shown clear potential for
multi-step forecasting several days ahead \cite{Islam2026a,Ruma2023}.
Encoder--decoder architectures have been applied to multi-step flood
forecasting because they generate a whole sequence of future values at once
rather than one value at a time \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022}. Both of those studies,
however, were evaluated at horizons of hours rather than days, a limitation
revisited in Chapter~2. A framework that covers lead times from the near term
to several days ahead gives decision-makers a more complete picture than one
built around a single horizon.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:1.4}

Most water-level and flood-forecasting studies in Bangladesh rely on a single
type of data or a narrow set of variables. Ruma et al.\ used water-level
records alone \cite{Ruma2023}, recent work on discharge did the same with
discharge \cite{Islam2026a}, and another line of research predicts flooding
almost entirely from climatic information
\cite{Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024}. Each approach is useful, but none
examines what can be gained by combining discharge, water level and weather
variables across a large and hydrologically varied network of stations.

A second issue concerns where the data are used for training. Stations in
different parts of a river network differ statistically, and a single model
trained on all of them together may represent none of them well. Studies in
Bangladesh have applied machine learning across multiple sites, but almost
always on the assumption that the data can first be collected in one place
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023}.

Federated learning offers an alternative, in which clients train a shared
model collaboratively while their raw data remain where they are. The
Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm of McMahan et al.\ showed how updates
from many clients can be combined without gathering the data centrally
\cite{McMahan2017}. FedAvg works best when client data are similar, however,
and standard federated learning can struggle when data are not independent
and identically distributed (non-IID) \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}, which is the
situation expected among stations in different parts of a river system.

Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) addresses this by grouping similar clients
and training each group together. Such grouping has produced better-suited
models than either a single global model or fully isolated local models
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}. The idea has not yet been applied
to a real multi-station, multi-horizon water-level forecasting problem in
Bangladesh.

Finally, the value of a forecast depends on the trust placed in it, and trust
is harder to establish when a model cannot explain its predictions. SHAP is a
widely used and theoretically grounded method for attributing a prediction to
its inputs \cite{Lundberg2017}, and it has been applied to flood and
water-level prediction \cite{Huang2024,Khorrami2025}.

The central problem of this thesis is therefore how to forecast water levels
accurately across several horizons for hydrologically different Bangladeshi
stations, without centralizing their raw data and while explaining what drives
the predictions.

\section{Research Motivation}
\label{sec:1.5}

This study is motivated by three strands of existing work.

The first concerns model architecture. Deep sequence models, and LSTM networks
in particular, have proven effective for hydrological time series, including
multi-step prediction \cite{Ruma2023,Kao2020,Cui2022}. Encoder--decoder LSTMs
suit this task because they produce a sequence of future values directly, and
recent Seq2Seq and residual-LSTM variants confirm that the approach remains
relevant \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023}. For this reason, an
encoder--decoder LSTM is adopted as the core forecasting model.

The second concerns how training is organized. Federated learning enables
collaborative training without moving raw observations \cite{McMahan2017}, but
non-IID data remain a genuine obstacle \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. Clustering
clients before training is one practical remedy
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}, and an early application to
hydrological forecasting suggests that the idea is workable
\cite{Amanzholova2026}.

The third concerns trust. When a model informs decisions such as flood
warnings, the ability to explain why it produced a given prediction matters as
much as the prediction itself. SHAP offers a principled way to provide such
explanations \cite{Lundberg2017}, and its use in flood modeling has revealed
meaningful relationships between hydrometeorological inputs and model outputs
\cite{Huang2024,Khorrami2025,Parasar2025}.

None of these elements is new on its own. What has not yet been done is to
combine multivariate hydrometeorological inputs, an encoder--decoder model,
cluster-aware federated training, multi-horizon forecasting and
interpretability within a single framework for Bangladesh.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:1.6}

Each of the relevant areas has a reasonably developed literature:
Bangladesh-specific forecasting
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024},
multi-step encoder--decoder models \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023},
federated learning and its non-IID problem
\cite{McMahan2017,Zhu2021,Chen2025}, clustered federated learning
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}, and SHAP-based explanation in
hydrology \cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024,Khorrami2025}. What is missing is the
overlap between them.

No study identified in this review combines multi-horizon water-level
forecasting, a large multivariate network of Bangladeshi stations, a
cluster-aware federated architecture, SHAP-based interpretation and a check
against an external reference such as the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre
(FFWC). This thesis addresses that gap with CFL-EDLSTM, a Clustered Federated
Learning framework built around an encoder--decoder LSTM.

\section{Aim of the Study}
\label{sec:1.7}

The aim of this study is to design, build and evaluate CFL-EDLSTM for
multi-horizon, interpretable water-level forecasting at 25 hydrologically
diverse monitoring stations within the Brahmaputra River system in Bangladesh,
without pooling raw station data across regions. The study also presents the
results through a web-based platform that displays station-level forecasts and
compares them with FFWC's operational forecasts, used as an external reference.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:1.8}

The objectives of this study are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To group the 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring stations within the
    Brahmaputra River system into four meaningful clusters: Upstream,
    Mid-river, Downstream and Urban.

    \item To develop an encoder--decoder LSTM for multivariate, multi-horizon
    water-level forecasting at seven lead times from 3~hours to 15~days.

    \item To integrate this model into a clustered federated framework in which
    each cluster acts as a federated client, weighted FedAvg combines the
    client updates, and each cluster retains a locally adapted model, so that
    raw data are not pooled across clusters.

    \item To benchmark CFL-EDLSTM against Vanilla LSTM, GRU, XGBoost and TCN
    across clusters and forecast horizons.

    \item To apply SHAP to the proposed CFL-EDLSTM in order to rank the
    hydrometeorological inputs that contribute most to its predictions,
    excluding past water level.

    \item To develop a web-based platform that presents the forecasts and
    compares them with FFWC's operational forecasts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:1.9}

This study seeks to answer the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How can 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring stations within the
    Brahmaputra River system in Bangladesh be grouped into clusters that are
    meaningful for federated training?

    \item How accurately can an encoder--decoder LSTM forecast water level from
    3~hours to 15~days ahead?

    \item How well does clustered federated learning handle the heterogeneity
    among the four clusters without pooling raw data across them?

    \item How does CFL-EDLSTM perform relative to the four baseline models
    across clusters and lead times?

    \item Excluding past water level, which inputs (discharge, rainfall,
    temperature, evaporation and the features derived from them) contribute
    most to the forecasts of CFL-EDLSTM according to SHAP, and are their
    contributions hydrologically plausible?

    \item How do the forecasts compare with FFWC's operational forecasts,
    used as an external reference?
\end{enumerate}

Questions~1 to~6 correspond to Objectives~1 to~6 in Section~\ref{sec:1.8}.

\section{Scope of the Study}
\label{sec:1.10}

The study covers 25 monitoring stations within the Brahmaputra River system in
Bangladesh, grouped into four clusters (Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream and
Urban) according to their hydrological character and position in the river
network, cross-checked against FFWC's operational station groups. The term
\emph{Brahmaputra River system} is used here in a broad sense: it covers the
Brahmaputra--Jamuna and the tributaries and distributaries connected to it,
including the rivers around Dhaka. Five variables are used: water level as the target, and discharge,
rainfall, temperature and evaporation as inputs. Forecasts are produced at
seven lead times from 3~hours to 15~days. CFL-EDLSTM is compared with four
baseline models that reflect approaches common in recent flood-prediction
literature \cite{Aghenda2025}, and its predictions are interpreted with SHAP
\cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024}, with past water level excluded from the
reported attributions.

The study does not cover real-time operational deployment, a mobile
application or the forecasting of any variable other than water level. The web
interface serves to demonstrate and check the framework; it is not an
operational early-warning system.

\section{Contributions of the Study}
\label{sec:1.11}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A grouping of 25 monitoring stations within the Brahmaputra River
    system into four federated client groups: Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream
    and Urban.

    \item CFL-EDLSTM, a clustered federated encoder--decoder LSTM for
    multivariate, multi-horizon water-level forecasting across heterogeneous
    stations, in which raw data are not pooled across clusters.

    \item A comparison of CFL-EDLSTM with four baseline models across clusters
    and seven forecast horizons.

    \item A SHAP-based analysis of CFL-EDLSTM that identifies the
    hydrometeorological inputs, other than past water level, that contribute
    most to its forecasts.

    \item A web-based platform that presents the forecasts alongside FFWC's
    operational forecasts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Organization of the Thesis}
\label{sec:1.12}

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.
\textbf{Chapter~2} reviews the literature on flood and water-level forecasting
in Bangladesh, multi-horizon deep learning, federated and clustered federated
learning, and explainable AI, and concludes with the research gap.
\textbf{Chapter~3} describes the methodology, covering the study area and data,
preprocessing, station clustering, the CFL-EDLSTM framework, the baseline
models, the evaluation design, the SHAP analysis and the web interface.
\textbf{Chapter~4} presents and discusses the results, including comparisons
with the baselines, with FFWC and with earlier studies. \textbf{Chapter~5}
concludes the thesis with the main findings, limitations and directions for
future work.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:1.13}

This chapter established the context and purpose of the thesis. Bangladesh
experiences recurring floods driven partly by water arriving from beyond its
borders, and its monitoring stations behave very differently from one another.
Earlier forecasting studies tend to rely on a narrow set of variables, assume
that data can be pooled, and offer little insight into why a model makes the
predictions it does. To address these limitations, the thesis proposes
CFL-EDLSTM, a clustered federated encoder--decoder LSTM for multi-horizon
water-level forecasting at 25 stations in the Brahmaputra River system,
supported by SHAP-based interpretation and a web interface for comparison with
FFWC's operational forecasts. Chapter~2 reviews the literature underlying these choices and examines
the research gap in detail.
