\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:1.1}

Bangladesh sits on the lower deltaic plain of the Ganges--Brahmaputra--Meghna
river system, one of the largest and most active river networks in the world.
Heavy monsoon rain, huge volumes of water arriving from upstream catchments and
very flat, low-lying land leave the country exposed to flooding and
water-logging year after year. Figure~\ref{fig:hydro-context} makes the point
clearly: almost all of Bangladesh lies close to sea level, and higher ground
appears only along its northern and eastern edges. Rainfall, discharge and
drainage change from place to place, and water level changes with them, so a
model built for one location will not necessarily work at another.

\caption{Elevation of Bangladesh (adapted from \cite{Dewan2022}).}
\label{fig:hydro-context}
\end{figure}

This chapter introduces the thesis. Sections~\ref{sec:1.2} and~\ref{sec:1.3}
describe the flood setting and why forecasting at several lead times matters.
Sections~\ref{sec:1.4}--\ref{sec:1.6} state the problem, the motivation and the

and contributions follow in Sections~\ref{sec:1.7}--\ref{sec:1.11}, and the
chapter ends with the organization of the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:1.12}) and a
short summary (Section~\ref{sec:1.13}). No results are presented here; they
appear in Chapter~4.

\section{Flooding and Water-Logging in Bangladesh}
\label{sec:1.2}

Much of Bangladesh's flood problem does not start inside the country.
Bangladesh lies at the bottom of the Ganges--Brahmaputra--Meghna basin, and only
a fraction of the water in its rivers falls as rain within its own borders. As
Figure~\ref{fig:river-context} shows, the three basins stretch across India,
Nepal, Bhutan and China, and the water draining from all of them passes through
Bangladesh on its way to the Bay of Bengal. A large share of next week's river
flow has already been set in motion elsewhere, days earlier.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures2/river_context.png}
\caption{The Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna basins and the major river systems
of Bangladesh (adapted from \cite{BDP2100Atlas}).}
\label{fig:river-context}
\end{figure}

The flood record shows how often this turns into disaster. In 1988, about
82,000~km$^2$, close to 60\% of the country, went under water in what is still
regarded as a once-in-50-to-100-year event \cite{Banglapedia2023Flood}. In 1998
more than two-thirds of the country was flooded and the death toll passed 2,300
\cite{Banglapedia2023Flood,AbdullahAlam2024}. Serious floods followed in 2004,
2007, most years from 2009 to 2014, and 2020, and in 2017 families in the
worst-hit areas lost more than a fifth of their annual income
\cite{AbdullahAlam2024}. Flooding here is a recurring part of life on the
floodplain, which is why even a small gain in forecast accuracy or lead time
reaches a very large number of people.

Where the water rises matters too. In rural floodplains, water-logging follows
the river fairly directly. In fast-growing cities, poor drainage can make the
same rise in water level far more damaging. Studies in Bangladesh show that
even neighboring stations on one river can behave differently
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b}. Upstream stations respond mainly to discharge and
rainfall from higher up the catchment, while downstream and urban stations are
shaped by a mix of river flow, local rainfall, drainage and sometimes tides.
There is no reason to assume that every station behaves alike.

Researchers have approached the forecasting problem from several sides. Ruma
et al.\ forecast water levels across a wide network of Bangladeshi stations with
an optimized LSTM \cite{Ruma2023}. Others paired climatic variables with machine
learning to predict flooding \cite{Rakin2023,Rajab2023}, and recent work has
focused on discharge and hydrological modeling in the Old Brahmaputra basin
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b}. Together, these studies show that data-driven
forecasting works in Bangladesh. They also keep returning to the same
observation: each station has its own character, and a single averaged model
tends to wash it out.

\section{Importance of Water-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:1.3}

Forecasts that are reliable, timely and aware of a station's place in the river
network support flood preparedness, early warning and disaster risk reduction.
Different lead times serve different decisions. A forecast a few hours ahead
supports an immediate response; one several days ahead gives time to plan.

Longer forecasts are harder to get right. Even so, machine-learning and
deep-learning models have shown real potential for multi-step forecasting
several days ahead \cite{Islam2026a,Ruma2023}. Encoder--decoder architectures
have been used for multi-step flood forecasting because they produce a whole
sequence of future values at once, not one prediction at a time
\cite{Kao2020,Cui2022}. Both of those studies, though, were evaluated at
horizons of hours, not days, a point Chapter~2 returns to. A framework that
covers everything from the next few hours to the next two weeks gives
decision-makers a fuller picture than one built around a single lead time.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:1.4}

Most water-level and flood-forecasting work in Bangladesh relies on a single
type of data or a narrow set of variables. Ruma et al.\ used water-level records
alone \cite{Ruma2023}, recent discharge work did the same with discharge
\cite{Islam2026a}, and another line of research predicts flooding almost
entirely from climatic information \cite{Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024}.
Each approach is useful, but none looks at what happens when discharge, water
level and weather variables are combined across a large and hydrologically
varied network of stations.

A second issue is where the data are trained. Stations in different parts of a
river network differ statistically, and one model trained on all of them
together may represent none of them well. Bangladeshi studies have applied
machine learning across multiple sites, but almost always assuming the data can
first be brought together in one place \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023}.

Federated learning offers another way. Clients train a shared model together
while their raw data stay where they are. McMahan et al.'s Federated Averaging
(FedAvg) showed how updates from many clients can be combined without collecting
the data centrally \cite{McMahan2017}. FedAvg works best when client data are
fairly similar, and standard federated learning can struggle when they are not
independent and identically distributed (non-IID) \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. That
is exactly what one would expect across stations in different parts of a river
system.

Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) responds by grouping similar clients and
training each group together. Such grouping has produced better-suited models
than either a single global model or fully isolated local ones
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}. What is missing is an application
of the idea to a real multi-station, multi-horizon water-level problem in
Bangladesh.

Finally, a forecast is only as useful as the trust placed in it, and trust is
hard to build when a model cannot explain itself. SHAP is a widely used and
well-grounded way of attributing a prediction to its inputs
\cite{Lundberg2017}, and it has been applied to flood and water-level prediction
\cite{Huang2024,Khorrami2025}.

The central problem of this thesis, then, is how to forecast water levels
accurately across several horizons for hydrologically different Bangladeshi
stations, without centralizing their raw data and while explaining what drives
the predictions.

\section{Research Motivation}
\label{sec:1.5}

Three strands of existing work motivate this study.

The first is architecture. Deep sequence models, and LSTMs in particular, have
proven effective for hydrological time series, including multi-step prediction
\cite{Ruma2023,Kao2020,Cui2022}. Encoder--decoder LSTMs suit this task because
they produce a sequence of future values directly, and recent Seq2Seq and
residual-LSTM variants show the approach is still relevant
\cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023}. This is why an encoder--decoder LSTM
sits at the core of the forecasting model.

The second is how training is organized. Federated learning allows
collaborative training without moving raw observations \cite{McMahan2017}, but
non-IID data are a real obstacle \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. Clustering clients
before training is one practical remedy
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}, and an early application to
hydrological forecasting suggests the idea is workable \cite{Amanzholova2026}.

The third is trust. When a model informs decisions such as flood warnings,
being able to say why it predicted what it did matters as much as the
prediction itself. SHAP offers a principled way to do this \cite{Lundberg2017},
and its use in flood modeling has revealed useful relationships between
hydrometeorological inputs and model outputs
\cite{Huang2024,Khorrami2025,Parasar2025}.

None of the three is new on its own. What has not been done is to bring
multivariate hydrometeorological inputs, an encoder--decoder model,
cluster-aware federated training, multi-horizon forecasting and
interpretability together in one framework for Bangladesh.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:1.6}

Each of these areas has a reasonably developed literature: Bangladesh-specific
forecasting
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024},
multi-step encoder--decoder models \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023},
federated learning and its non-IID problem \cite{McMahan2017,Zhu2021,Chen2025},
clustered federated learning \cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}, and
SHAP-based explanation in hydrology \cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024,Khorrami2025}.
What they lack is overlap.

No study identified in this review combines multi-horizon water-level
forecasting, a large multivariate network of Bangladeshi stations, a
cluster-aware federated architecture, SHAP-based interpretation and a check
against an external reference such as the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre
(FFWC). This thesis addresses that gap with CFL-EDLSTM, a Clustered Federated
Learning framework built around an encoder--decoder LSTM.

\section{Aim of the Study}
\label{sec:1.7}

The aim is to design, build and evaluate CFL-EDLSTM for multi-horizon,
interpretable water-level forecasting at 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring
stations within the Brahmaputra River system in Bangladesh, without pooling raw
station data across regions. The study also presents the results through a
web-based platform that shows station-level forecasts and compares them with
FFWC's operational forecasts, used as an external reference.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:1.8}

The objectives of this study are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To group the 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring stations within the
    Brahmaputra River system into four meaningful clusters: Upstream,
    Mid-river, Downstream and Urban.

    \item To develop an encoder--decoder LSTM for multivariate, multi-horizon
    water-level forecasting at seven lead times from 3~hours to 15~days.

    \item To integrate this model into a clustered federated framework in which
    each cluster is a federated client, weighted FedAvg combines the updates,
    and each cluster keeps a locally adapted model, so raw data are not pooled
    across clusters.

    \item To benchmark CFL-EDLSTM against Vanilla LSTM, GRU, XGBoost and TCN
    across clusters and forecast horizons.

    \item To apply SHAP to CFL-EDLSTM and rank the hydrometeorological inputs
    that contribute most to its predictions, excluding past water level.

    \item To build a web-based platform that presents the forecasts and
    compares them with FFWC's operational forecasts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:1.9}

The study sets out to answer six questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How can 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring stations within the
    Brahmaputra River system in Bangladesh be grouped into clusters that are
    meaningful for federated training?

    \item How accurately can an encoder--decoder LSTM forecast water level from
    3~hours to 15~days ahead?

    \item How well does clustered federated learning handle the differences
    among the four clusters without pooling raw data across them?

    \item How does CFL-EDLSTM perform against the four baselines across
    clusters and lead times?

    \item Leaving past water level aside, which inputs (discharge, rainfall,
    temperature, evaporation and the features derived from them) contribute
    most to the forecasts of CFL-EDLSTM according to SHAP, and do they make
    hydrological sense?

    \item How do the forecasts compare with FFWC's operational forecasts, used
    as an external reference?
\end{enumerate}

Each question corresponds to the objective with the same number in
Section~\ref{sec:1.8}.

\section{Scope of the Study}
\label{sec:1.10}

The study covers 25 monitoring stations within the Brahmaputra River system in
Bangladesh. Here the term is used in a broad sense, covering the
Brahmaputra--Jamuna and the tributaries and distributaries connected to it,
including the rivers around Dhaka. The stations are grouped into four clusters
(Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream and Urban) by their hydrological character and
position in the river network, cross-checked against FFWC's operational station
groups. Five variables are used: water level as the target, and discharge,
rainfall, temperature and evaporation as inputs. Forecasts are made at seven
lead times from 3~hours to 15~days. CFL-EDLSTM is compared with four baselines
that reflect approaches common in recent flood-prediction literature
\cite{Aghenda2025}, and its predictions are interpreted with SHAP
\cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024}, with past water level left out of the reported
attributions.

The study does not attempt real-time operational deployment, a mobile
application or forecasting of any variable other than water level. The web
interface demonstrates and checks the framework; it is not an operational
early-warning system.

\section{Contributions of the Study}
\label{sec:1.11}

This thesis makes five contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A grouping of 25 monitoring stations within the Brahmaputra River
    system into four federated client groups: Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream
    and Urban.

    \item CFL-EDLSTM, a clustered federated encoder--decoder LSTM for
    multivariate, multi-horizon water-level forecasting across heterogeneous
    stations, in which raw data are not pooled across clusters.

    \item A comparison of CFL-EDLSTM with four baselines across clusters and
    seven forecast horizons.

    \item A SHAP analysis of CFL-EDLSTM showing which hydrometeorological
    inputs, other than past water level, matter most to its forecasts.

    \item A web-based platform that presents the forecasts alongside FFWC's
    operational forecasts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Organization of the Thesis}
\label{sec:1.12}

The rest of the thesis is organized as follows. \textbf{Chapter~2} reviews the
literature on flood and water-level forecasting in Bangladesh, multi-horizon
deep learning, federated and clustered federated learning, and explainable AI,
and ends with the research gap. \textbf{Chapter~3} describes the methodology:
the study area and data, preprocessing, station clustering, the CFL-EDLSTM
framework, the baselines, the evaluation design, the SHAP analysis and the web
interface. \textbf{Chapter~4} presents and discusses the results, including the
comparisons with the baselines, with FFWC and with earlier studies.
\textbf{Chapter~5} concludes the thesis with the main findings, limitations and
future work.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:1.13}

This chapter set out the context and purpose of the thesis. Bangladesh faces
recurring floods driven partly by water arriving from beyond its borders, and
its stations behave very differently from one another. Earlier forecasting
studies tend to rely on a narrow set of variables, assume that data can be
pooled, and say little about why a model predicts what it does. This thesis
therefore proposes CFL-EDLSTM, a clustered federated encoder--decoder LSTM for
multi-horizon water-level forecasting at 25 stations in the Brahmaputra River
system, supported by SHAP-based interpretation and a web interface that
compares its forecasts with FFWC's. Chapter~2 now reviews the literature behind
these choices and sets out the research gap in detail.
