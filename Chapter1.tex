\section{Introduction}

Bangladesh lies on the lower deltaic plain of the Ganges--Brahmaputra--Meghna
river system, one of the largest and most active river networks in the world.
Heavy monsoon rainfall, discharge arriving from upstream catchments, and
generally low-lying land leave the country persistently exposed to flooding and
water-logging (Figure~\ref{fig:hydro-context}). Because rainfall, discharge and
drainage vary from place to place, water-level behavior varies with them, and a
model built for one location will not necessarily carry over to another.

This chapter introduces the thesis. It describes the flood setting and why
forecasting at several lead times matters (Sections~\ref{sec:1.2}

this work (Sections~\ref{sec:1.4}--\ref{sec:1.6}). The aim, objectives,
research questions, scope and contributions follow

organization of the thesis (Section~\ref{sec:1.12}) and a short summary
(Section~\ref{sec:1.13}). No results are presented here; they appear in
Chapter~4.

\section{Flooding and Water-Logging in Bangladesh}
\label{sec:1.2}

Much of Bangladesh's flood problem does not originate inside the country.
Bangladesh sits at the bottom of the Ganges--Brahmaputra--Meghna basin, and
only a fraction of the water in its rivers falls as rain within its own
borders. The rest comes from catchments in India, Nepal, Bhutan and China, so
a large share of next week's river flow has already been set in motion
elsewhere, days earlier (Figure~\ref{fig:river-context}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures2/river_context.png}
\caption{Major river systems of Bangladesh (adapted from \cite{BDP2100Atlas}).}
\label{fig:river-context}
\end{figure}

The flood record shows how often this matters. In 1988, about 82,000~km$^2$,
close to 60\% of the country, went under water, in what is still regarded as a
once-in-50-to-100-year event \cite{Banglapedia2023Flood}. In 1998 more than
two-thirds of the country was flooded and the death toll passed 2,300
\cite{Banglapedia2023Flood,AbdullahAlam2024}. Serious floods followed in 2004,
2007, most years from 2009 to 2014, and 2020, and in 2017 families in the
worst-hit areas lost more than a fifth of their annual income
\cite{AbdullahAlam2024}. Flooding here is a recurring part of life in the
floodplain, which is why even a small gain in forecast lead time or accuracy
reaches many people.

The effect of high water also depends on where it occurs. In rural floodplains,
water-logging follows the river fairly directly, whereas in fast-growing cities
poor drainage can make the same rise in water level considerably worse. Studies
in Bangladesh show that even neighboring stations on one river can behave
differently \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b}. Upstream stations mainly respond to
discharge and rainfall from higher up the catchment, while downstream and
urban stations are shaped by a mix of river flow, local rainfall, drainage and
sometimes tides. There is no basis for assuming that every station behaves
alike.

Researchers have taken up the forecasting problem from several sides. Ruma et
al.\ forecast water levels across a wide network of Bangladeshi stations with
an optimized LSTM \cite{Ruma2023}. Others paired climatic variables with
machine learning to predict flooding \cite{Rakin2023,Rajab2023}, and recent
work has focused on discharge and hydrological modeling in the Old Brahmaputra
basin \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b}. Together they show that data-driven
forecasting works here, and they repeatedly note that individual stations have
their own character, which averaging tends to wash out.

\section{Importance of Water-Level Forecasting}
\label{sec:1.3}

Forecasts that are reliable, timely and sensitive to a station's place in the
river network support flood preparedness, early warning and disaster risk
reduction. Different lead times serve different decisions: a forecast a few
hours ahead supports an immediate response, while one several days ahead is
better suited to planning.

Longer forecasts are harder to get right. Even so, machine-learning and
deep-learning models have shown real potential for multi-step forecasting
several days ahead \cite{Islam2026a,Ruma2023}. Encoder--decoder architectures
have been used for multi-step flood forecasting and produce a whole sequence of
future values at once, not one prediction at a time \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022},
although both of those studies were evaluated at horizons of hours, not days,
a point Chapter~2 returns to. A framework that forecasts from the near term to
the longer term gives a fuller picture than one built around a single lead
time.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:1.4}

Most water-level and flood-forecasting work in Bangladesh relies on a single
type of data or a narrow set of variables. Ruma et al.\ used water-level
records alone \cite{Ruma2023}, recent discharge work has done the same with
discharge \cite{Islam2026a}, and another line of research predicts flooding
almost entirely from climatic information
\cite{Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024}. Each is useful, but none addresses
what happens when discharge, water level and weather variables are combined
across a large and hydrologically varied network of stations.

A second issue is where the data are trained. Stations in different parts of a
river network differ statistically, and one model trained on all of them
pooled together may represent none of them well. Bangladeshi studies have
applied machine learning across multiple sites, but almost always assuming the
data can first be brought together in one place
\cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023}.

Federated learning offers an alternative: clients train a shared model
collaboratively while their raw data stay where they are. McMahan et al.'s
Federated Averaging (FedAvg) showed how updates from many clients can be
combined without collecting the data centrally \cite{McMahan2017}. It works
best when client data are fairly similar, and standard federated learning can
struggle when they are not independent and identically distributed (non-IID)
\cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}, which is what one would expect across stations in
different parts of a river system.

Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) responds to this by grouping similar clients
and training each group together, and such grouping has produced better-suited
models than either a single global model or fully isolated local ones
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}. What is missing is an application
of the idea to a real multi-station, multi-horizon water-level problem in
Bangladesh.

Finally, a forecast is only as useful as the trust placed in it, and trust is
harder to build when a model cannot explain itself. SHAP is a widely used and
well-grounded way of attributing a prediction to its inputs
\cite{Lundberg2017}, and it has been applied to flood and water-level
prediction \cite{Huang2024,Khorrami2025}.

The central problem of this thesis is therefore how to forecast water levels
accurately, across several horizons, for hydrologically different Bangladeshi
stations, without centralizing their raw data and while explaining what drives
the predictions.

\section{Research Motivation}
\label{sec:1.5}

Three strands of existing work motivate this study.

The first is architecture. Deep sequence models, and LSTM in particular, have
proven effective for hydrological time series, including multi-step prediction
\cite{Ruma2023,Kao2020,Cui2022}. Encoder--decoder LSTMs suit this task because
they produce a sequence of future values directly, and recent Seq2Seq and
residual-LSTM variants confirm the approach remains relevant
\cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023}. This is the reason for choosing an
encoder--decoder LSTM as the core forecasting model.

The second concerns how training is done. Federated learning allows
collaborative training without moving raw observations \cite{McMahan2017}, but
non-IID data are a genuine obstacle \cite{Zhu2021,Chen2025}. Clustering clients
before training is one practical remedy
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}, and an early application to
hydrological forecasting suggests the idea is workable \cite{Amanzholova2026}.

The third is trust. When models inform decisions such as flood warnings, being
able to say why a model predicted what it did matters as much as the
prediction. SHAP provides a principled way to do this \cite{Lundberg2017}, and
its use in flood modeling has revealed useful relationships between
hydrometeorological inputs and outputs \cite{Huang2024,Khorrami2025,Parasar2025}.

None of the three is new on its own. What has not been done is to combine
multivariate hydrometeorological inputs, an encoder--decoder model, cluster-aware
federated training, multi-horizon forecasting and interpretability in one
framework for Bangladesh.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:1.6}

Each front has a reasonably developed literature: Bangladesh-specific
forecasting \cite{Islam2026a,Islam2026b,Ruma2023,Rakin2023,Rajab2023,HemelSakib2024},
multi-step encoder--decoder models \cite{Kao2020,Cui2022,Lim2025,Yuan2023},
federated learning and its non-IID problem
\cite{McMahan2017,Zhu2021,Chen2025}, clustered federated learning
\cite{Ghosh2021,Harshvardhan2022,BenAli2026}, and SHAP-based explanation in
hydrology \cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024,Khorrami2025}. What they lack is overlap.

No study identified in this review combines multi-horizon water-level
forecasting, a large multivariate network of Bangladeshi stations, a
cluster-aware federated architecture, SHAP-based interpretation, and a check
against an external reference such as the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre
(FFWC). This thesis addresses that gap with CFL-EDLSTM, a Clustered Federated
Learning framework built around an encoder--decoder LSTM.

\section{Aim of the Study}
\label{sec:1.7}

The aim is to design, build and evaluate CFL-EDLSTM for multi-horizon,
interpretable water-level forecasting at 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring
stations within the Brahmaputra River system in Bangladesh, without pooling raw
station data across regions. The study also presents the results through a
web-based platform that shows station-level forecasts and compares them with
FFWC water levels, which serve as an external reference and not as ground truth.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:1.8}

The objectives of this study are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To group the 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring stations within the
    Brahmaputra River system into four meaningful clusters (Upstream,
    Mid-river, Downstream and Urban).

    \item To develop an encoder--decoder LSTM for multivariate, multi-horizon
    forecasting at seven lead times from 3~hours to 15~days.

    \item To integrate it within a clustered federated framework in which each
    cluster is a federated client, weighted FedAvg combines the updates, and
    each cluster keeps a locally adapted model, so raw data are not pooled
    across clusters.

    \item To benchmark CFL-EDLSTM against Vanilla LSTM, GRU, XGBoost and TCN
    across clusters and horizons.

    \item To apply SHAP to rank the hydrometeorological variables that
    contribute to the predictions.

    \item To build a web-based platform that presents the forecasts and
    compares them with FFWC water levels.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:1.9}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How can 25 hydrologically diverse monitoring stations within the
    Brahmaputra River system in Bangladesh be grouped into clusters that are
    meaningful for federated training?

    \item How effectively can an encoder--decoder LSTM forecast water level from
    3~hours to 15~days ahead?

    \item How well does clustered federated learning handle heterogeneity among
    the four clusters without pooling raw data across them?

    \item How does CFL-EDLSTM perform relative to the four baselines across
    clusters and lead times?

    \item Which inputs (water level, discharge, rainfall, temperature or
    evaporation) contribute most according to SHAP, and do they make
    hydrological sense?

    \item How do the forecasts compare with FFWC water levels, used as an
    external reference and not as ground truth?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope of the Study}
\label{sec:1.10}

The study uses 25 monitoring stations within the Brahmaputra River system in
Bangladesh, grouped into four clusters (Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream and
Urban) by hydrological character and position in the river network. It uses
five variables, with water level as the target and discharge, rainfall,
temperature and evaporation as inputs, and forecasts seven lead times from
3~hours to 15~days. CFL-EDLSTM is compared with four baselines, reflecting the
approaches common in recent flood-prediction literature \cite{Aghenda2025}, and
interpreted with SHAP \cite{Lundberg2017,Huang2024}.

The study does not attempt real-time operational deployment, a mobile
application or forecasting of any variable other than water level. The web
interface demonstrates and checks the framework and is not an operational
early-warning system.

\section{Contributions of the Study}
\label{sec:1.11}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A clustering of 25 monitoring stations within the Brahmaputra River
    system into four federated client groups (Upstream, Mid-river, Downstream
    and Urban).

    \item CFL-EDLSTM, a clustered federated encoder--decoder LSTM for
    multivariate, multi-horizon water-level forecasting across heterogeneous
    stations, in which raw data are not pooled across clusters.

    \item A comparison with four baselines across clusters and seven horizons.

    \item A SHAP analysis of which hydrometeorological inputs matter most.

    \item A web-based platform that presents the forecasts alongside FFWC water
    levels.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Organization of the Thesis}
\label{sec:1.12}

The rest of the thesis is organized as follows.
\textbf{Chapter~2} reviews the literature on flood and water-level forecasting
in Bangladesh, multi-horizon deep learning, federated and clustered federated
learning, and explainable AI, and ends with the research gap.
\textbf{Chapter~3} describes the methodology: the study area and data,
preprocessing, station clustering, the CFL-EDLSTM framework, the baselines, the
evaluation design, the SHAP analysis and the web interface.
\textbf{Chapter~4} presents and discusses the results, including the
comparisons with the baselines, with FFWC and with earlier studies.
\textbf{Chapter~5} concludes the thesis with the main findings, limitations and
future work.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:1.13}

This chapter set out the context and purpose of the thesis. Bangladesh faces
recurring floods that are driven partly by water arriving from outside its
borders, and its stations behave very differently from one another. Earlier
forecasting studies tend to rely on a narrow set of variables, assume that data
can be pooled, and say little about why a model predicts what it does. The
thesis therefore proposes CFL-EDLSTM, a clustered federated encoder--decoder
LSTM for multi-horizon water-level forecasting at 25 stations in the
Brahmaputra River system, supported by SHAP-based interpretation and a web
interface for comparison with FFWC. Chapter~2 now reviews the literature behind
these choices and sets out the research gap in detail.
